Support vector
machines

Many lines can separate two classes. A support vector machine picks the one that leaves the most room on both sides.

Today: a classifier built from one geometric idea, the widest margin between the classes. Two classes only; more than two classes comes later with ensembles.

Decision Boundaries

Every classifier we have seen draws a boundary between the classes. Left: a curved, irregular one. Right: a decision tree's axis-aligned rectangles, yellow where circles are the majority and blue where triangles are. Today: a classifier that draws a straight boundary, and picks the best one.

The question

Which line?

  • Lines A, B and C all separate the training points. Training error is zero for each.
  • They differ in how much room they leave. A hyperplane's margin ρ is twice its distance to the closest point. Pick a line, or build your own with the sliders.
  • The support vector machine chooses the line with the widest margin, the largest ρ. New points near the boundary are then least likely to land on the wrong side.

1 · Hyperplanes

A hyperplane is
w · x + b = 0

  • Pick a vector w and a number b. The points withf(x) = w · x + b = 0form a line in 2D, a plane in 3D, a hyperplane in general.
  • The same form as linear regression: w holds the slopes (the βs) and b is the intercept.
  • w is perpendicular to the line. Changing b slides the line without turning it.
  • f(x) > 0 on one side, f(x) < 0 on the other. Predict +1 when f(x) > 0.
  • f(x) is a score, like the scores in the ROC lecture. The SVM's default threshold is 0.

2 · Hard margin

The margin

  • A line that separates the classes.
  • The closest training points, ringed, set the margin. Point xi is at distance |w · xi + b| / ‖w‖ from the line. Labels are yi = ±1.
  • (w, b) and (2w, 2b) give the same line. Fix the scale so the closest points have |w · xi + b| = 1. The margin lines are then w · x + b = +1 and w · x + b = −1.
  • Each margin line is at distance 1/‖w‖ from the line, so the margin width isρ = 2/‖w‖

0The separable data from the start, with the best line already drawn. We first say what "room" means precisely.

1Each point's distance to the line is |w·x_i + b|/‖w‖. The margin is set by the smallest of these distances. Three points tie here, ringed.

2The scale of w is free. We use the freedom to make the closest points score exactly ±1. That is a convention, not a restriction.

3From the last slide, a point with |f| = 1 is 1/‖w‖ away. Here w = (0.5, 0.5), ‖w‖ ≈ 0.71, so ρ = 2/‖w‖ ≈ 2.83. A wide margin means a short w.

2 · Hard margin

The hard-margin
SVC

  • The widest margin with every point on its correct side, outside the margin: maximize ρ = 2 / ‖w‖
    subject to yi (w · xi + b) ≥ 1
    for every i
  • The same w wins if we instead minimize ½ ‖w‖²
    subject to the same constraints     A bowl-shaped objective with straight-line constraints: a constrained convex quadratic minimization problem. It has one best solution, and standard solvers find it.
  • The usual solution method uses Lagrange multipliers.

0Same data. Now we turn "widest margin" into a problem a computer can solve.

1The constraints say: every point at least 1 in score on its correct side, which is the scale convention from the last slide. Among all such (w, b), take the one with the widest margin ρ.

2Maximizing 2/‖w‖ is minimizing ‖w‖, which is minimizing ½‖w‖². The square removes the square root and the ½ tidies the derivative. Quadratic objective, linear constraints: a constrained convex quadratic minimization problem, a well-studied class with reliable solvers.

3We will not go into the solution method. The result to keep: the hyperplane is determined by the three ringed points alone. Delete any other point and the solution stays the same.

3 · Soft margin

When no line
separates

  • Two points sit among the other class. No line separates the data, so the hard-margin problem has no solution.
  • Let point i fall short by a slack ξi ≥ 0, and pay for it: minimize ½ ‖w‖² + C Σ ξi
    subject to yi (w · xi + b) ≥ 1 − ξi
    and ξi ≥ 0 for every i
  • ξi = 0: outside the margin. 0 < ξi ≤ 1: inside the margin, correct side. ξi > 1: wrong side, a training mistake.

0The same fifteen points plus two more: a purple point among the greens and a green point among the purples.

1Real data is rarely separable: noise, overlapping classes, mislabelled examples. We need a version that tolerates some violations.

2Each point gets its own slack ξ_i, how far its score falls short of 1. The objective now has two parts: a wide margin (small ‖w‖) and little total slack. C sets the exchange rate. This is the soft-margin SVC; the figure shows the solution for C = 1.

3The orange segments show each slack: the distance from the point to its own margin line is ξ_i/‖w‖. The two intruders have ξ > 1; they are misclassified. Points with ξ > 0 are support vectors too, as are points exactly on a margin line.

3 · Soft margin

The role of C

  • Large C: slack is expensive. The margin narrows to keep points out of it.
  • Small C: slack is cheap. The margin widens and many points sit inside it, all of them support vectors.
  • C is a hyperparameter. Tune it, together with the kernel, by grid search inside nested cross-validation, as for decision trees.

4 · Kernels

The kernel trick

  • A linear SVM on the rings: no good boundary exists.
  • The solver never needs the points themselves, only how similar each pair is: their dot products xi · xj. Replace that similarity with a kernel K(xi, xj), and the same solver draws curved boundaries. The RBF kernel is exp(−γ ‖x − x′‖²).
  • To score a new point, each support vector votes, weighted by its similarity to the point. Large γ makes votes local and the boundary wiggly.
  • The same machinery handles XOR, where no line works either.

3This should feel familiar from k-NN: the prediction is a vote of nearby training points. The differences: only support vectors vote, and their weights are learned. Slide γ up: each vote reaches less far and the boundary wraps around individual points, a sign of overfitting.

4XOR: positives in two opposite corners, negatives in the other two. Linear fails; RBF succeeds. Like C, γ is chosen on validation data.

5 · In practice

General properties

One best answer

The objective is a bowl and the constraints are straight lines. There is a single best solution, and solvers find it.

Numeric, scaled features

One-hot encode categorical features, then standardize, as for k-NN. On unscaled data the solver can be very slow or never finish.

Hyperparameters

C, the kernel and its parameters such as γ. Tune them together by grid search inside nested cross-validation.

Training cost

Kernel SVMs slow down sharply on large training sets: roughly quadratic or worse in the number of points. Linear SVMs scale much further.

Many features

Often work well with many features: in theory, generalization depends on the margin rather than on the number of features. Not a guarantee; a poor kernel or C can still overfit.

Six points to remember when using an SVM. The convexity point matters: whatever solver or starting point, you get the same model. Scaling is the most common practical mistake. The score point links back to the ROC lecture: an SVM score can be thresholded like any other, but it is not a probability.

Summary

Takeaways

Geometry

Hyperplanes

f(x) = w · x + b. Predict by its sign. The distance to the hyperplane is f(x)/‖w‖.

Hard margin

The widest margin

Minimize ½‖w‖² subject to yi f(xi) ≥ 1. The margin width is ρ = 2/‖w‖. Support vectors alone fix the answer.

Soft margin

Slack and C

Slack ξi lets points violate the margin at a cost C Σ ξi. C trades width against violations.

Kernels

Curved boundaries

Replace dot products by a kernel. Same solver, boundaries that are linear only in a hidden feature space.

Four ideas. If students keep one sentence: an SVM picks the widest margin, and only the points on the margin lines matter.

Bonus · for later

Slack is
hinge loss

  • At the solution each slack is as small as allowed:ξi = max(0, 1 − yi f(xi))
  • Substitute, and the constraints disappear: minimize ½ ‖w‖²
    + C Σ max(0, 1 − yi f(xi))     The second term is the hinge loss.
  • The hinge loss is never below the 0–1 loss, so the sum bounds the number of training mistakes. Many other classifiers have this same form: a loss on the data plus a penalty on the weights.
