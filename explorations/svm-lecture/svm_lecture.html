The question

Which line?

  • Lines A, B and C all separate the training points. Training error is zero for each.
  • They differ in how much room they leave: the widest empty street around each line. Half its width, the distance to the closest point, is the margin. Pick a line, or build your own with the sliders.
  • The support vector machine chooses the line with the widest street, which is the largest margin. New points near the boundary are then least likely to land on the wrong side.

1 · Hyperplanes

A line is
w · x + b = 0

  • Pick a vector w and a number b. The points withf(x) = w · x + b = 0form a line in 2D, a plane in 3D, a hyperplane in general.
  • w is perpendicular to the line. Changing b slides the line without turning it.
  • f(x) > 0 on one side, f(x) < 0 on the other. Predict +1 when f(x) > 0.
  • f(x) is a score, like the scores in the ROC lecture. The SVM's default threshold is 0.

2 · Hard margin

The margin

  • A line that separates the classes.
  • Its margin is the distance to the closest training point:mini yi f(xi) / ‖w‖with labels yi = ±1.
  • (w, b) and (2w, 2b) give the same line. Fix the scale so the closest points have yi f(xi) = 1. The street's edges are then f = +1 and f = −1.
  • Each edge is at distance 1/‖w‖, so the street is 2/‖w‖ wide.

0The separable data from the start, with the best line already drawn. We first say what "room" means precisely.

1y_i f(x_i) is positive exactly when point i is on its correct side, so y_i f(x_i)/‖w‖ is its distance to the line, counted positive when correct. The margin is the smallest of these. Three points tie here, ringed.

2The scale of w is free. We use the freedom to make the closest points score exactly ±1. That is a convention, not a restriction.

3From the last slide, a point with f = 1 is 1/‖w‖ away. Here w = (0.5, 0.5), ‖w‖ ≈ 0.71, so the margin is 1/‖w‖ ≈ 1.41 and the width about 2.83. A wide street means a short w.

2 · Hard margin

The hard-margin
SVC

  • The widest street with every point on its correct side, outside the street: maximize 2 / ‖w‖
    subject to yi (w · xi + b) ≥ 1
    for every i     This is the same as maximizing the margin 1/‖w‖.
  • The same w wins if we instead minimize ½ ‖w‖²
    subject to the same constraints     A bowl-shaped objective with straight-line constraints: a quadratic program. It has one best solution, and standard solvers find it.
  • The usual solution method uses Lagrange multipliers, one αi ≥ 0 per point. It gives w = Σ αi yi xi, and αi > 0 only for points on the edges: the support vectors.

0Same data. Now we turn "widest street" into a problem a computer can solve.

1The constraints say: every point at least 1 in score on its correct side, which is the scale convention from the last slide. Among all such (w, b), take the widest street.

2Maximizing 2/‖w‖ is minimizing ‖w‖, which is minimizing ½‖w‖². The square removes the square root and the ½ tidies the derivative. Quadratic objective, linear constraints: a quadratic program, a well-studied problem class with reliable solvers.

3We will not derive the multipliers. The result to keep: w is a weighted sum of the training points, and only the three ringed points get non-zero weight. Here α = 1/12, 1/6 and 1/4. Delete any other point and the solution stays the same.

3 · Soft margin

When no line
separates

  • Two points sit among the other class. No line separates the data, so the hard-margin problem has no solution.
  • Let point i fall short by a slack ξi ≥ 0, and pay for it: minimize ½ ‖w‖² + C Σ ξi
    subject to yi (w · xi + b) ≥ 1 − ξi
    and ξi ≥ 0 for every i
  • ξi = 0: outside the street. 0 < ξi ≤ 1: inside the street, correct side. ξi > 1: wrong side, a training mistake.

0The same fifteen points plus two more: a purple point among the greens and a green point among the purples.

1Real data is rarely separable: noise, overlapping classes, mislabelled examples. We need a version that tolerates some violations.

2Each point gets its own slack ξ_i, how far its score falls short of 1. The objective now has two parts: a wide street (small ‖w‖) and little total slack. C sets the exchange rate. This is the soft-margin SVC; the figure shows the solution for C = 1.

3The orange segments show each slack: the distance from the point to its own edge of the street is ξ_i/‖w‖. The two intruders have ξ > 1; they are misclassified. Points with ξ > 0 are support vectors too, as are points exactly on an edge.

3 · Soft margin

The role of C

  • Large C: slack is expensive. The street narrows to keep points out of it.
  • Small C: slack is cheap. The street widens and many points sit inside it, all of them support vectors.
  • C is a hyperparameter. Choose it on validation data, the way you choose k for k-NN.

4 · Kernels

The kernel trick

  • A linear SVM on the rings: no good boundary exists.
  • In the Lagrange-multiplier solution, the points appear only in dot products xi · xj. Replace each by a kernel K(xi, xj), a similarity. The RBF kernel is exp(−γ ‖x − x′‖²).
  • The score isf(x) = Σ αi yi K(xi, x) + bSupport vectors vote, weighted by similarity. Large γ makes votes local and the boundary wiggly.
  • The same machinery handles XOR, where no line works either.

3This should feel familiar from k-NN: the prediction is a vote of nearby training points. The differences: only support vectors vote, and their weights α_i are learned. Slide γ up: each vote reaches less far and the boundary wraps around individual points, a sign of overfitting.

4XOR: positives in two opposite corners, negatives in the other two. Linear fails; RBF succeeds. Like C, γ is chosen on validation data.

5 · In practice

General properties

One best answer

The objective is a bowl and the constraints are straight lines. There is a single best solution, and solvers find it.

Sparse

Only support vectors shape the boundary. Prediction cost grows with their number, not with the size of the training set.

Scale the features

Margins are distances. A feature measured in thousands swamps one measured in units. Standardize first, as for k-NN.

Hyperparameters

C, the kernel and its parameters such as γ. Choose them on validation data.

A score, not a probability

f(x) ranks points. Thresholds other than 0 trade TPR against FPR and trace an ROC curve.

Training cost

Kernel SVMs slow down sharply on large training sets: roughly quadratic or worse in the number of points. Linear SVMs scale much further.

Six points to remember when using an SVM. The convexity point matters: whatever solver or starting point, you get the same model. Scaling is the most common practical mistake. The score point links back to the ROC lecture: an SVM score can be thresholded like any other, but it is not a probability.

Summary

Takeaways

Geometry

Hyperplanes

f(x) = w · x + b. Predict by its sign. The distance to the hyperplane is f(x)/‖w‖.

Hard margin

The widest street

Minimize ½‖w‖² subject to yi f(xi) ≥ 1. The width is 2/‖w‖. Support vectors alone fix the answer.

Soft margin

Slack and C

Slack ξi lets points violate the street at a cost C Σ ξi. C trades width against violations.

Kernels

Curved boundaries

Replace dot products by a kernel. Same solver, boundaries that are linear only in a hidden feature space.

Four ideas. If students keep one sentence: an SVM picks the widest street, and only the points on its edge matter.

Bonus · for later

Slack is
hinge loss

  • At the solution each slack is as small as allowed:ξi = max(0, 1 − yi f(xi))
  • Substitute, and the constraints disappear: minimize ½ ‖w‖²
    + C Σ max(0, 1 − yi f(xi))     The second term is the hinge loss.
  • The hinge loss is never below the 0–1 loss, so the sum bounds the number of training mistakes. Many other classifiers have this same form: a loss on the data plus a penalty on the weights.

━ 0–1 loss (mistake or not)━ hinge loss

2Read the objective as "fit the data, but keep w small". That pattern, loss plus penalty, will come back with other models.
