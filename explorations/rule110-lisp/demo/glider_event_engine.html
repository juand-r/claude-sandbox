Glider Event Engine

Rule 110 computes with gliders that cross a periodic background, the ether. This engine treats each glider as a particle that moves by formula and simulates cells only where two of them come within two cells of each other. Each distinct collision is simulated once and remembered.

Below, a JavaScript port of the engine runs in your browser on windows cut from the real Turing-machine runs, and every row it produces is checked against a brute-force Rule 110 simulation.

Why it is exact

Rule 110 updates each cell from itself and its two neighbours. So if two non-ether patches, each evolved alone in ether, never come within two cells of each other, no cell ever sees both, and the row is exactly the union of the two isolated evolutions. A particle is a patch whose cells recur, shifted, under isolated evolution (Cook's C, Ebar and A gliders, bound groups, even pure phase slips of the ether). Between collisions, particles move by formula.

When two patches come within two cells, the engine simulates their cells together until the result splits again into pieces at least one ether tile apart. That evolution depends only on the patches' cells and ether phases, so it is stored under that key and every later identical collision costs a lookup. The C engine adds a heap of next collisions, lazily materialized ossifier train and table, and checkpoints; this page's port keeps the same rules with simpler bookkeeping.
