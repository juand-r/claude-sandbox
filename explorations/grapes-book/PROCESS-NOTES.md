# Process notes: writing chapter 1 (draft 2)

Meticulous record of the writing process, as requested. Dated 2026-10-01.

## Why the first draft failed

The user called the first draft "terrible." The instruction that followed was
specific: write again from scratch in a new document, no margin notes, skip the
persona and backstory, about grapes only, in the style of McPhee. Later
refined: "a blend of McPhee's Oranges and The Fall."

Before writing, I need to understand what went wrong. Re-reading draft 1, the
problems I see:

1. **Too much narrator, not enough grape.** The asides (Card 1 at 11:52 p.m.,
   Card 23 at 2:31 a.m., the lock checked) were building a character instead
   of teaching you about grapes. They called attention to the fiction.

2. **The backstory leaked.** "I have sometimes envied it" (about the
   hermaphrodite vine), the town he will not name, the lock, the late-night
   hours, the foxy sniff he "learned to despise in order to be admitted
   somewhere." Every one of these was a planted tell for a mystery the reader
   doesn't know about yet. The effect: the chapter kept signaling that it was
   about something other than grapes, without ever saying what. The user saw
   this as the writing trying too hard.

3. **The voice was imitating McPhee rather than writing like McPhee.** McPhee's
   prose succeeds because it appears effortless. The facts carry the interest;
   the writer stays out of the way. Draft 1 was too aware of itself as
   literary writing. Sentences like "Wine has been praised, scored, auctioned,
   forged, and explained, and it has learned to talk about itself" -- the
   parallel structure and the personification are showy. McPhee would have said
   something plainer.

4. **The Clamence influence was heavy-handed.** The "you" appeared too often.
   The general observations ("the man who notices it usually does so at the
   moment it begins to apply to himself") were confessional rather than
   observational. In The Fall, Clamence's pronouncements work because they are
   embedded in five nights of monologue; extracted and placed in an
   encyclopedia of grapes, they read as non sequiturs.

## What McPhee's voice actually requires

I re-read Oranges from memory (I know the book, not individual sentences). The
techniques:

- **Concrete specifics.** Not "grapes are widely grown" but "77.8 million
  metric tonnes." The number does the work. McPhee never tells you something
  is interesting; he shows you something interesting and moves on.

- **Simple declarative sentences.** Subject, verb, object. One idea per
  sentence. He doesn't stack clauses.

- **Juxtaposition.** Two facts placed next to each other, with no commentary.
  "Ten thousand varieties. Thirty-three of them on half the vineyard land on
  earth." The surprise is in the gap between the numbers.

- **Personal presence without personal story.** "I counted." "I asked." "I
  drove to." He is there as a pair of eyes and ears, not as a character with
  a backstory. You know almost nothing about McPhee by the end of Oranges,
  and you don't miss the knowledge.

- **No commentary.** He doesn't editorialize. The arrangement of facts IS the
  commentary. He trusts the reader.

## What The Fall's voice requires

Camus' The Fall is a monologue by Jean-Baptiste Clamence, addressed to an
unnamed stranger in an Amsterdam bar. The style:

- **Direct address.** "May I, monsieur, offer my services..." The "you" is
  present from the first sentence. The listener never speaks.

- **Performed charm.** Clamence is good company and knows it. He is witty,
  confiding, ironic about himself. The performance is visible, which is part
  of the point.

- **General observations.** Pronouncements about human nature, prompted by
  specific circumstances. "People naturally rallied to me." These are always
  sharp and specific, never vague.

- **Confessional intimacy.** He tells you things. He is honest, or appears to
  be. The honesty is also a performance.

## How to blend them

The user said "a blend" but also "about grapes only." These constrain each
other. McPhee's voice is naturally grape-focused (he reports facts); Clamence's
voice tends toward the personal (he confesses). So "blend + grapes only" means
mostly McPhee with occasional Clamence seasoning:

- The "you" appears, but rarely: a direct address here and there.
- General observations come from grape facts, not from the narrator's past.
  "We are loyal to the first few things we are given, and we call the loyalty
  taste" -- prompted by the statistic about grape varieties, not by the
  narrator's psychology.
- The narrator is present as a curious, precise reporter (McPhee) who
  sometimes makes a judgment about people (Clamence), and the judgment always
  comes from something about grapes.

## Decisions for draft 2

1. **No asides at all.** No margin notes, no cards, no timestamps. The chapter
   uses only the main text, notes, and subnotes.

2. **No backstory, no tells.** No planted clues for the mystery. No lock, no
   envying the vine, no "admitted somewhere," no serpette, no pond, no Odile.
   This is volume 1: grapes.

3. **Opening about grapes, not about the narrator.** Draft 1 opened: "You
   will want to know, I suppose, why a man who has spent half a century..."
   That's about the man. Draft 2 opens: "Let me tell you about the grape."
   That's about the grape.

4. **Iowa stays, but briefly.** The Iowa section is about how the narrator
   first encountered grapes (as jelly). That is about grapes. But it should
   be shorter and less atmospheric than draft 1, which lingered on nail kegs
   and binder twine.

5. **The counting scene stays.** Three counts, two answers, 117/118/117. It
   reveals character through action (obsessive precision) without telling the
   reader anything about the narrator's past. And it's about a bunch of
   grapes.

6. **Fewer notes, tighter.** Notes carry real content (sources, digressions)
   but don't try to be miniature essays. The Flintstones-glass note stays
   because it's charming and about the jelly.

7. **New material: the bloom.** A tactile anecdote about rubbing the waxy
   bloom off grape berries with a cloth. It's about the grape as a physical
   object. New to draft 2.

8. **New analogy: wheat and bread.** "Almost everything written about grapes
   is really about wine, in the same way that almost everything written about
   wheat is really about bread." Helps the reader understand the project.

9. **The hermaphrodite vine, without loneliness.** Draft 1: "It does not
   require anybody. I have sometimes envied it." Draft 2: "It does not
   require a partner to set fruit. This is convenient for agriculture. It is
   convenient for the vine as well, though I doubt the vine has an opinion
   about it." About grapes, not about the narrator's isolation.

10. **The Bull/Welch ending, without Clamence's confession.** Draft 1 ended
    the section with: "the man who notices it usually does so at the moment
    it begins to apply to himself." That was the narrator almost confessing
    something. Draft 2: "I say it because the pattern is old, and because you
    see it everywhere once you start to look." An observation, not a
    confession.

## What I am uncertain about

- Whether the Iowa section is still too personal for "about grapes only."
  My judgment: it is about grapes (the jelly was his first grape), so it
  belongs, but I could be wrong.

- Whether the Clamence turns are at the right frequency. Too many and the
  chapter feels philosophical; too few and The Fall's influence disappears.
  I aim for three or four in the whole chapter.

- Whether "That is how this book will go" is a strong enough ending.

- Whether the chapter is the right length. It is slightly shorter than draft 1
  (no asides) but covers the same ground.
