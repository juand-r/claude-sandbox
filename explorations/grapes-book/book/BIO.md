# The narrator: Hollis Vane

A private file for the authors. The reader never sees it. Everything here is
invented unless it is marked REAL; real-world anchors are listed at the end
and must be verified, or attributed to a source, before they appear in the
book.

Status markers: DECIDED (by the author), PROPOSED (mine, not yet confirmed),
OPEN (not decided).

## Summary

The man who signs this book is not Hollis Vane. He was born in a small Iowa
town in 1948 under another name, left it at eighteen to escape something he
has never told anyone, and joined the Army. In West Germany, in the spring
of 1968, the past caught up with him. He deserted, crossed into France in
the chaos of May 1968, and bought a new name from a forger: Hollis Vane, an
American, born the same year. As Vane he worked the vines in Burgundy for
nine years and fell in love with the vigneron's daughter, Odile. In 1977 he
left her for a job in Napa. Forty years later, both widowed, she came to
him. In October 2020, in the last days of the Glass Fire, she died in the
pond below his house. He killed her, and did not mean to. In 2021, alone in
the pandemic, he begins a book about grapes.

### Dates (PROPOSED)

Born 1948, not 1949: if he finishes high school in June 1967 and enlists that
summer, he has time for basic training and specialist training before being
posted to Germany in early 1968. Born in October 1949 he would only be 18 in
October 1967, which is too late to be in Germany by May 1968, especially if
he trains as a listener (see "The Army"). The birthday, 18 October, is kept:
it is the date of Odile's death.

## Ardath, Iowa

Invented. A town like Endora, in the same flat state: a few hundred people,
one main street, a grain elevator taller than anything else for forty miles,
a café, a hardware store (his father's), a Lutheran church, a water tower
with the town's name on it. Nothing happens there, and everybody knows when
it does. Corn, not vines: the nearest grape is in a jar of jelly.

His family (birth name OPEN): father Ambrose, who ran the hardware store;
mother Ruth, who kept bees and kept ledgers of everything. Siblings OPEN.
He has had no contact with any of them since 1968. What they believe
happened to him: OPEN.

He can refer to *What's Eating Gilbert Grape* (Hedges's novel, 1991; the
film, 1993), because of the pun, and because Endora is his town under
another name. Gilbert is the young man who could not leave; he is the one
who did, and the cost of it. He saw the film in 1994, in St. Helena, with
Peg, and left before the end.

## The dark secret (OPEN)

Something that happened in Ardath before he left in 1967. To be decided by
the author later. Constraints so far:

- It is serious enough that, when it surfaced in 1968, he chose desertion
  and a false name over facing it.
- It must be something a background investigation, or a letter from home,
  could bring to the Army's attention.
- An idea on the table, not decided: a drowning in Ardath, ruled an accident,
  which would make Odile's death the second drowning in his life.

## The Army, 1967–1968

- Summer 1967: enlists, at 18. He wants out of Ardath, and the Army is the
  quickest way.
- His role: OPEN, two options under discussion.
  - A regular soldier, posted to a unit in West Germany.
  - A "listener": enlisted in the Army Security Agency (REAL; see anchors),
    trained as an intercept operator, posted to one of its listening stations
    in West Germany, logging East German and Soviet radio traffic. This
    suits him: hours of exact logging, call signs, times to the second. And
    the top-secret clearance it required means a background investigation:
    investigators asking questions in Ardath. That is how the secret could
    surface.
- Early 1968: posted to West Germany.
- Spring 1968: the secret catches up with him (how: OPEN; depends on the
  secret and the role).
- May 1968: he deserts and crosses into France while the country is
  paralysed by the general strike. In the chaos nobody looks twice at a young
  man on the road. Deserting from a listening post would make him wanted far
  more seriously than an ordinary deserter, and would haunt him longer.

## France, 1968–1977

- A forger provides him with an American identity: Hollis Vane. How it was
  done: OPEN. One realistic method (REAL in Britain, see anchors): the birth
  certificate of a child of the right age who died in infancy, used to
  obtain a genuine passport. If so, the name belongs to a boy buried somewhere
  in the Midwest. Who the forger is: OPEN.
- Summer 1968: works in the vines, drifting south and east. By the harvest he
  is at the Domaine Ferrand, a small estate in Meursault.
- Gaston Ferrand, a silent vigneron, takes him on as a hand, then into the
  cellar. Teaches him to taste and to estimate a harvest by counting clusters,
  vine by vine. Hollis has counted things ever since. Gaston gives him a
  serpette, a small curved pruning knife. What Gaston knew or guessed about
  him: OPEN.
- Harvest 1968: he meets Gaston's daughter, Odile, then 18.
- 1968–1977: nine years at the Domaine. In love with Odile.

## Napa, 1977–2021

- 1977: the opportunity. After the 1976 Paris tasting, Napa wants people who
  know Burgundy. A growing winery hires him to buy and to advise. He flies
  home as Hollis Vane, a U.S. citizen, on his false passport, and goes. He
  tells Odile he will send for her. He does not. He and Gaston quarrel before
  he leaves; about what, he never says. (Regret one.)
- 1980: marries Peg.
- 1980s–2000s: a respected palate in Napa. Buyer, consultant, judge at
  competitions, a column in a trade newsletter. Known for exact, joyless
  tasting notes. Avoids photographs and anything that needs fingerprints.
- 1983: buys the house and its small vineyard.
- A minor scandal, settled privately: a wine sold under a better label than
  it deserved. He says it was the bottler's error. (He bends the truth when
  the truth would cost him.)
- Late 1980s–1990s: the vineyard must be replanted after phylloxera returns
  to Napa. He keeps a ledger of every vine pulled.
- 2006: Peg dies.
- 2007: Odile, long married to a wine broker in Beaune, is widowed.
- 2008: she writes to him. The first letter in 31 years.
- 2009, at harvest: she comes to Napa "for a month" and stays.
- 2009–2020: eleven years together. Not married. She keeps a strawberry bed
  by the pond and refuses to learn the names of the clones in his vineyard.
  Whether she knew his real name: OPEN.
- Night of 18–19 October 2020, his 72nd birthday by his real birth date:
  Odile dies. (Timing under discussion; see below.)
- 2021: the pandemic. He stops drinking wine. He cannot say why. He begins
  the book on grapes: the fruit before it becomes wine.

## The death of Odile

What the public record says:

- The Glass Fire (REAL) started on 27 September 2020 and was contained on
  20 October. Helicopters dipped water from vineyard ponds, his among them.
  Evacuations, smoke, emergency services stretched thin.
- She was found at about 7 a.m. on 19 October 2020 in the frost-protection
  pond below the house, by a picker arriving for work.
- She had been drinking. There was a bruise at her left temple.
- The coroner recorded drowning; manner of death "undetermined". The sheriff,
  with a county on fire, closed the case quickly.
- Two glasses and an empty bottle were on the table on the terrace: a 1968
  from the Domaine Ferrand, the harvest at which they met.

What only he knows, and does not tell:

- They argued that evening.
- He says he went down to the cellar and worked until about 2 a.m., then went
  to bed without looking for her.
- He records everything. His ledgers, cards and diaries account for every day
  since 1968, to the minute, and nothing before it. The night of 18 October
  2020 is the only gap since: the page is cut out of the diary, cleanly, at
  the gutter. The book never says who cut it.
- His serpette has been missing since that night.
- He wanted the investigation over quickly, for a reason no one could guess:
  any close look at Hollis Vane might find the deserter underneath. (His
  fingerprints were taken when he enlisted; see anchors.)

Other people:

- Thibault Ferrand, Odile's nephew, heir to her share of the Domaine.
  Believes Hollis killed her. Writes him letters, which Hollis files unread
  and numbers.

### What actually happened (DECIDED, PROVISIONAL)

He killed her, and he did not mean to. During the quarrel on the terrace, or
on the path down to the pond, he struck or pushed her; she fell, hit her
head, and went into the water. Not premeditated.

The author marked this "I think; revisit later". Open details: struck or
pushed; whether he saw her go into the water; whether he left her or tried to
reach her; the serpette; who cut the page; what the quarrel was about
(perhaps she had found out who he was).

However it is settled, the book never confirms it (`STYLE.md`, 2.5). Every
clue must keep an innocent reading.

## Habits, tics, and how he writes

- Counts: berries, steps from the desk to the door (14), cards written per
  night. Rounds nothing.
- Records times to the minute, and corrects them ("at 11:42, or rather
  11:43").
- Checks the cellar lock three times. Says it is only twice.
- Works at night, in the old tank room, at a steel table.
- Notes go on index cards, numbered, in shoeboxes labelled by year. He cites
  them as sources ("Card 1142"). The cards are this book's notes before they
  were notes.
- Types fair copies on a Hermes 3000 typewriter that Odile gave him in 2010.
  He crosses out, never erases.
- Does not drink wine anymore. Tastes grapes instead, and describes them as
  if they were wines.
- Calls her "O." at first. Her full name appears only late in the book.
- Addresses "you" in the asides. It is never clear whether "you" is the
  reader, Odile, or Thibault.
- Never writes about anything before 1968. When the book gets near it, he
  changes the subject.

## Motifs (plant early, return often)

- Strawberries: her bed by the pond; grey mould on forgotten fruit. The
  strawberry already "returns throughout this book" in chapter 1.
- Water: the pond, still surfaces, the helicopters taking water from it, the
  word "drowned", which he avoids until late.
- The 1968 bottle: the year they met, the year he became someone else.
- The serpette.
- Missing things: the cut page, missing cards, notes referred to but not
  there, the years before 1968.
- Names: his own, which is not his; the dead boy whose name he carries.
- Counting that does not come out the same twice.
- Listening (if he was a listener): radio static, call signs, voices he was
  not meant to hear.
- Gilbert Grape: the pun he cannot resist and then regrets; Endora as Ardath;
  the brother who could not leave.
- Doubles: two names, two birthdays; Odile and Peg; Odile and Odette (in
  *Swan Lake* the two are danced by one ballerina); Iowa and Burgundy; the
  grape and the wine.

## Real-world anchors (REAL; verify or attribute before use)

Checked 2026-10-01 against secondary sources (web); check primary sources
before quoting in the book.

- *What's Eating Gilbert Grape*: novel by Peter Hedges (1991), film (1993),
  set in Endora, a fictional small town in Iowa.
- Enlistment, 1960s: Regular Army volunteers enlisted for three or four years;
  at 17 with a parent's written consent, at 18 without.
- Army Security Agency: ran signals-intelligence listening stations in West
  Germany (Berlin/Teufelsberg, Herzogenaurach, Augsburg, Bad Aibling and
  others); recruits enlisted for four years, needed a top-secret clearance,
  and trained at Fort Devens, Massachusetts. Length of training for intercept
  operators, and how background investigations were done: to check.
- About 50,000 U.S. soldiers deserted during the Vietnam War; France and
  Sweden were destinations; deserters' committees existed in Paris and
  Stockholm.
- May 1968 in France: from about 18 May public transport and railways
  stopped; petrol ran short; tourists left by coach for Brussels, Geneva and
  Barcelona.
- Military fingerprints were sent to the FBI (one source: all fingerprint
  records until 1974). Enlisted fingerprint cards from before 1990 are not in
  the FBI's searchable database (DoD Inspector General, 2017).
- The "Jackal" method: a dead child's birth certificate used to obtain a
  genuine passport (Forsyth, *The Day of the Jackal*, 1971; Britain). Whether
  this worked for U.S. passports, and at a U.S. embassy abroad: to check.
- The U.S. visa waiver for visitors began in 1988 (created 1986).
- The 1976 Paris tasting (24 May 1976) raised Napa's standing. Whether it led
  to wineries hiring people with Burgundy experience: to check.
- Glass Fire: started 27 September 2020, contained 20 October 2020; about
  70,000 people under evacuation orders; helicopters dipped water from
  vineyard ponds.
- Napa: phylloxera returned in the late 1980s and 1990s when the AxR1
  rootstock failed; many vineyards were replanted. Frost-protection ponds are
  common.
- Meursault is a wine village in the Côte de Beaune, Burgundy. Vignerons
  estimate yield by counting clusters on sample vines.
- The Hermes 3000 is a Swiss portable typewriter made from the late 1950s.
- *Swan Lake*: Odette and Odile are traditionally danced by one ballerina.
- In California, a coroner can record the manner of death as "undetermined".
