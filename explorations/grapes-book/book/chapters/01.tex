\chapter{Concord}

You will want to know, I suppose, why a man who has spent half a century in
the company of wine should sit down, at seventy-one, to write a book about
grapes.\aside{Card 1. Tuesday, 7 January 2020, 11:52 p.m. I begin, as I mean
to go on, with a card.} People always want to know why. It is the first
question at a dinner and the last thing anyone remembers of the answer, so I
will keep it short. Wine has been written about enough. It has been praised,
scored, auctioned, forged, and explained, and it has learned to talk about
itself, as things do once they have been explained. The grape has not. The
grape is wine before it went to school. I find, at my age, that I prefer
things before they went to school. I include myself in this. We are all more
interesting before we have been explained, and most of us are never given
the chance to find out.

So: the grape. Let us begin where the encyclopedias begin, since that is
where I began, and since a man who has spent his life with his nose in a
glass ought to be made to look things up.

The grape belongs to the genus \emph{Vitis}, in a family of climbing plants
called the Vitaceae.% F003
\note{The family is large and mostly unknown to anyone who is not a
botanist: the \emph{Encyclop\ae dia Britannica} counts twelve genera in it,
and a group of botanists writing in 2018 (Wen and others, in the
\emph{Journal of Systematics and Evolution}) count sixteen genera and about
nine hundred and fifty species. Virginia creeper is a cousin. So is Boston
ivy, which covers so many old brick walls, and which I have always suspected
of being there to keep us from asking what is underneath.} % F028
There are sixty to eighty species of \emph{Vitis}, depending on whom you ask
and on how closely they have looked at the vines of Asia.% F004
\note{Sixty to eighty is the \emph{Britannica}'s range. The International
Organisation of Vine and Wine, which is to the grapevine roughly what the
Vatican is to the saints, says eighty, on the authority of a 2007 paper by
Wen and others. The disagreement is mostly about the Asian species, which
are many and poorly separated. I have kept a card
for each number. I do not yet know which card to throw away.}
They are native to the northern temperate zone, and most of them live in
eastern Asia and in North America. Europe and western Asia have exactly
one. % F005
That one is \emph{Vitis vinifera}, the wine-bearing vine, and it supplies
the great majority of the grapes grown anywhere in the world. % F006
It is the grape of every wine you have heard of. It is also, in the form
of the wild vine from which it was taken, a plant that comes in two sexes.
The wild vine is male or female, and the female needs a male nearby before
she will set fruit.\note{The wild form is \emph{Vitis
vinifera} subspecies \emph{sylvestris}; the cultivated form is subspecies
\emph{vinifera}. The usual account, as I write, puts the domestication in
western Asia some eight thousand years ago, and the genetic evidence also
points to the Near East (Myles and others, \emph{Proceedings of the National
Academy of Sciences}, 2011). On the sexes: Vasconcelos and colleagues, in
the \emph{American Journal of Enology and Viticulture} (2009), and Picq and
colleagues, \emph{BMC Plant Biology} (2014).\subnote{Picq and his colleagues
trace the difference to a small region of a single chromosome, which I will
not pretend to understand, except to say that the whole history of the
vineyard comes down, as so many histories do, to a very small thing that
nobody noticed for a very long time.}} % F006
The cultivated vine is, for the most part, both at once. Its flowers carry
everything they need. It does not require anybody.
I have sometimes envied it.

How many kinds of grape are there? Ten thousand, according to the same
international organisation, of which thirteen cover more than a third of
the world's vineyards and thirty-three cover half.% F007
\note{International Organisation of Vine and Wine, \emph{Distribution of the
World's Grapevine Varieties} (2017). The same paper adds that the standard
catalogue of the subject, the \emph{Vitis International Variety Catalogue},
held 21,045 names, 12,250 of them for \emph{vinifera}, but that many of
these are the same grape under different names, and that the true number of
\emph{vinifera} varieties is nearer six thousand.\subnote{A grape that
travels collects names, as people do who travel. The same vine may be one
thing in its own valley and another two valleys over, and a third thing in
a nurseryman's catalogue in another language. The catalogue's work is to
find out which names belong to one plant. It is slow work. It is also, I
think, a kind of kindness.}}
Ten thousand kinds, and half the vineyards of the world planted to
thirty-three of them. We are loyal, all of us, to the first few things we
were given, and we call the loyalty taste.

And how much grape is there? In 2018, the last year for which I have the
figures, the world grew 77.8 million metric tonnes of
grapes.% F008
\note{International Organisation of Vine and Wine, \emph{2019 Statistical
Report on World Vitiviniculture}. Of the 77.8 million tonnes, 57 per cent
went to wine (and to musts and juices), 36 per cent were eaten as table
grapes, and 7 per cent were dried. The dried share is counted as fresh
grapes, before drying, at about four kilograms of grapes to one kilogram of
raisins. The largest growers, in millions of tonnes: China 11.7, Italy 8.6,
the United States 6.9, Spain 6.9, France 6.2. The world's vineyards covered
7.4 million hectares.\subnote{In pounds, which is how I was raised to think
and how I still think when I am tired, 77.8 million tonnes is about 171.5
billion pounds. Divided among the 7.7 billion of us the United Nations
counted in 2019, it comes to 10.1 kilograms, or 22.3 pounds, a head. I have
done the division three times. It comes out the same each time, which is
more than I can say for some of my counting (see \xref{the-count}).}} % F024
That is a great deal of fruit to have so little written about it that is not
about something else.

\bigskip

I bought a bunch of grapes yesterday at the market in St.~Helena. It is
January, and nothing in this valley is ripe; the vines outside my window are
bare wood. The box said \emph{Product of Chile}, which is to say that it was
summer where these grapes were picked, and they had come north in the dark
of a ship to be eaten in the winter of strangers. % F023
I paid for them, carried them home, and put them on the kitchen scale.
They weighed 612 grams.\aside{The scale is a Salter, in metal, older than
the house. I trust it more than I trust myself. I have had it longer.}
Then I counted them.

\anchor{the-count}There were 117 berries. I counted them again, to be sure,
and there were 118. I am a careful man; I have been paid, for most of my
life, to be careful; and I could not count a bunch of grapes twice and get
the same number. I counted a third time, slowly, pulling each berry a little
aside with the tip of a pencil, and there were 117. I have decided to
believe it. It is the number I got most often, and in the end that is what
most of us mean by the truth.%
\note[cluster]{The bunch has a botanical name and an anatomy. It is a
panicle: a branched stalk carrying many flowers, and later many fruits. The
part that joins the bunch to the cane is the peduncle; the main axis is the
rachis; and each berry hangs on its own short stem, the pedicel. (The terms
are in Winkler's \emph{General Viticulture}, the textbook I was handed when
I came to California, and which I have carried around since.) A
single cluster may carry as many as a thousand flowers. If one in three of
them becomes a berry, the growers are content.\subnote{By that arithmetic my
117 berries were once some 350 flowers, and some 230 of those flowers came
to nothing. Nobody counted them either. We count the ones that set.}}

Then, because I was not tired, I cut eleven of them in half with a paring
knife on a white plate. Botanically, a grape is a true berry. It is a
single ovary grown fat: the skin is the outer wall, the flesh is the middle,
and the thin layer round the seeds is the inner wall, and there is no stone.
The skin wears a dull, waxy bloom that rubs off on the thumb; it is there to
keep the water in. The ovary has two small chambers, and each chamber holds
two ovules, so that a grape can never have more than four seeds.% F009
\note{Vasconcelos and others, \emph{American Journal of Enology and
Viticulture} (2009), on the flower; and the standard extension manuals, on
the seeds: one to four in the seeded varieties, never five. I have looked.
I have not found a fifth.}
These were sold as seedless, and the box was right. Eleven times I counted
the seeds in a grape, and eleven times there were none. It is restful, at
the end of a day, to count to zero.\aside{It is 1:47 in the morning. I keep
a farmer's hours, but from the wrong end of the night.}

\bigskip

The first grape I ever knew was not a grape. It was a jelly.

I grew up in Iowa, in a town I see no reason to name. Name it and someone
from there will write to correct me about the year the elevator burned;
leave it unnamed and it can be any of a thousand towns, which is nearer the
truth. They were all the same town. There was a grain elevator that you
could see before you could see anything else, a main street that ran one
way into corn and the other way into corn, a church, a caf\'e, and a
hardware store, which was my father's. In August the heat came up out of
the fields and stood in the street, and the only cool place in town was the
back of my father's store, among the nail kegs, where it smelled of oil and
iron and binder twine. There were no vines. If you had asked me, at ten,
what a grape was, I would have pointed at a jar.

The jar said \emph{Welch's}. The jelly inside it was purple, nearly black
in the jar and a bright, translucent violet when you spread it thin, which
my mother did not, on white bread, with peanut butter.\note{It is generally
believed that the peanut-butter-and-jelly sandwich was invented by
American soldiers in the Second World War. It was not. The first published
recipe appeared in 1901, in the \emph{Boston Cooking-School Magazine}, by
Julia Davis Chandler, and it called for currant or crab-apple jelly, not
grape. It became the lunch of American children in the Depression, when
bread came sliced and peanut butter came cheap. (Steve Estes, ``PB\&J,''
\emph{Gastronomica}, 2017, and the food historian Andrew F.~Smith.) The
soldiers ate it, too; they did not invent it. Most of what we believe about
where things come from is like this: true about the people, wrong about the
dates.} % F020
In the early 1960s Welch's put its jelly in drinking glasses printed with
the Flintstones, and when the jelly was gone the glass was yours. By then I
was too old to admit that I wanted one, and so I drank from Fred's face only
when nobody was in the kitchen.% F001
\aside{Cards tonight for the glasses, for Welch, for the sandwich, and for
Bull (several). They go into a shoebox marked 2020, behind Card 1, which
until tonight looked very lonely there.}

That jelly is made from one grape, the Concord, and the taste of it is the
taste that an American of my age means when he says ``grape.'' It is not the
taste of the grape of wine. A European, given a spoon of it, will tell you,
politely, that it tastes of something else. The word for the something else
is \emph{foxy}. The French, who borrowed it from the English ``fox grape,''
say \emph{fox\'e}, and so, in my hearing, did everyone in the wine trade,
with a small movement of the nose that I learned in my twenties to make
myself. % F027
The molecule that is chiefly
responsible has a name: methyl anthranilate.% F013
\note{Methyl anthranilate was first found in grape juice in 1921, by Power
and Chesnut, in the \emph{Journal of the American Chemical Society}; they
found it in the American grapes, the Concord and its kin, and not in the
European. A later paper (Robinson and others, \emph{American Journal of
Enology and Viticulture}, 2014) still calls it the compound ``considered to
be responsible for the distinctive `foxy' aroma and flavor'' of the
Concord. Other compounds help; this one leads.}
I made that small movement of the nose for fifty years. I am not proud of
it. It was the first thing I learned to despise, and I learned to despise
it in order to be admitted somewhere, which is how most contempt is learned.

\bigskip

The Concord has a maker, and his name was Ephraim Wales Bull.% F014

He was born in Boston in 1806 and moved to Concord, Massachusetts, in 1836,
to a house on Lexington Road. By his own account he sowed the seeds of a
wild grape in the autumn of 1843, chose among the seedlings, and waited.
His vine first bore its fruit in 1849. He showed the grape to the
Massachusetts Horticultural Society in 1853, and in 1854 C.~M.~Hovey of Boston
put it on the market.% F015
\note{The dates are in the Concord Free Public Library's papers on Bull and in
the histories kept by the United States Department of Agriculture's National
Agricultural Library. The 1843 sowing is Bull's own account, quoted at second
hand; he also spoke of raising two generations of seedlings, which does not
fit easily into six years. Where the sources disagree I have given the date
they agree on, and kept the others on cards. I have a great many cards about
Ephraim Bull.}
It was the right grape for the place. What the country wanted, Bull said,
was ``a good table and wine grape, which shall also be early, hardy and
prolific,'' and the grapes people had been trying to grow in New England
either died in the winter or failed to ripen before the first frost. The
Concord ripened early and lived through the winters. Americans planted it
widely.% F025
\note{The Concord is usually called a variety of \emph{Vitis
labrusca}, the American fox grape. It is not quite. Its parentage includes
some \emph{vinifera}: Huber and colleagues (\emph{Vitis}, 2016) make it an
offspring of the old American variety `Catawba,' which has European blood,
and of an unknown wild parent.\subnote{So the most American of grapes is not
entirely American. Few of us are, if you go back far enough, and most of us
have the good sense not to.}} % F017
Bull served in the state legislature. He did not grow rich.\note{So it is
said, by nearly everyone who writes about him. I have not seen his
accounts.} % F016
He died in 1895, in the Concord Home for the Aged, and he is buried in Sleepy
Hollow Cemetery, under a stone that says \emph{He sowed, others
reaped}.\note{That is the wording. The punctuation varies from one source to
the next: a full stop, a comma, nothing at all. I have not seen the stone. I
mean to.}% F016

Others reaped. The chief of them was a dentist. Thomas Bramwell Welch was an
Englishman, a Methodist, and a man who believed that alcohol was the ruin of
his neighbours, and he practised dentistry in Vineland, New Jersey. In 1869
he heated the juice of Concord grapes to keep it from fermenting, so that his
church could take communion without taking wine.% F018
\note{Welch's own history says he used forty pounds of Concord grapes from
the vines in front of his house. In 1897 the business built a large juice
plant in Westfield, New York, on the shore of Lake Erie, in the strip of
Concord vineyards along the lake that is still called the Grape Belt. % F026
Its grape spread, called Grapelade, dates from 1918, and its grape
jelly, by the account of the histories I have, from 1923. It still makes
the jelly from Concord grapes. It says so on the jar.} % F019
His juice was the first of the products that put his family's name into
American kitchens, my mother's among them.

We put up statues to the man who sold the thing. The man who grew it gets a
sentence on a stone, and the sentence has a comma in it that nobody can
agree on. I do not say this to blame Dr.~Welch. He reaped what was there to
be reaped, and he reaped it for the Lord, which is more than most of us can
say of our harvests. I say it because it is the way of things, and because
the man who notices it usually does so, I have found, at the moment it
begins to apply to himself.

It is late, and the bunch is still on the kitchen table, in its plastic
box, minus eleven berries. In the morning I will count it again. I expect
106. If I get 106, I will believe it at once. If I do not, I will count
until I do, and then I will believe that.\aside{Card 23, 2:31 a.m. Lights out.
The lock checked.}
