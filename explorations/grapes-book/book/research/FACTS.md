# Facts used in the book, with sources

Every real-world claim in the book is listed here before it is used
(`STYLE.md`, section 3). One entry per fact:

- **ID**: F001, F002, ... Cite it in the chapter source as a LaTeX comment,
  `% F012`, next to the sentence that uses it.
- **Claim**: one plain sentence, exactly as precise as the book will be.
- **Source**: URL(s) or bibliographic reference; prefer primary and
  authoritative sources.
- **Checked**: date, and how (read the source / secondary only).
- **Confidence**: high, medium, low. Low means: do not use, or attribute it
  ("according to ...").

The book is written in 2020 (inside the fiction). A fact may appear only if
it was known and published by then, and figures are given as a 2020 writer
would have found them (e.g. OIV 2019 report on 2018 data, not later
revisions). Entries note this where it matters ("2020 view").

Entries are added as research is done. Facts that turned out wrong are not
deleted: they are struck through with a note, so the history is visible.

### F001 Welch's Flintstones jelly glasses
- **Claim**: In the early 1960s (glasses dated 1962, 1963 and 1964) Welch's
  sold grape jelly in glass tumblers printed with Flintstones characters,
  about 4¼ inches tall, which families reused as children's juice glasses.
- **Source**: Heritage Auctions lot, "The Flintstones Welch's Grape Jelly
  Glass Tumblers Full Case of 24 (Hanna-Barbera/Welch's, 1962)",
  https://comics.ha.com/itm/memorabilia/miscellaneous/the-flintstones-welch-s-grape-jelly-glass-tumblers-full-case-of-24-hanna-barbera-welch-s-1962-/a/40237-84048.s ;
  WorthPoint listings, e.g.
  https://www.worthpoint.com/worthopedia/flintstones-welchs-jelly-glasses-1962-1900563318
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, search summaries of collector and auction
  listings (secondary).
- **Confidence**: medium. Dates and size from collector listings; good
  enough for a memory, not for a precise historical claim.

### F002 Pruning season in Napa
- **Claim**: In Napa, vines are dormant in winter and are pruned between
  late December and March; many growers wait until February or March,
  because rain carries fungal spores (notably Eutypa) into fresh pruning
  cuts, and January is used to mark the wood to cut or keep.
- **Source**: UC Master Gardeners of Sacramento County, "In the vineyard",
  https://ucanr.edu/site/uc-master-gardeners-sacramento-county/vineyard ;
  Napa Valley Register, "The all-important pruning dominates in the late
  winter vineyard",
  https://napavalleyregister.com/wine/the-all-important-pruning-dominates-in-the-late-winter-vineyard/article_b3eb35a4-4123-5f78-883f-68594374d999.html
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, search summaries (secondary).
- **Confidence**: medium-high. Read the UC page before quoting it.

### F003 The grape family and genus
- **Claim**: The grape belongs to the genus *Vitis*, in the family Vitaceae.
  Britannica counts 12 genera in the family; Wen et al. (2018) count 16
  genera and about 950 species.
- **Source**: Britannica, "Vitaceae", https://www.britannica.com/plant/Vitaceae ;
  Wen et al. 2018, Journal of Systematics and Evolution 56:262,
  https://scholarworks.uni.edu/facpub/694/
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent (read). **Confidence**: high.

### F004 How many species of Vitis
- **Claim**: Counts disagree: Britannica gives "about 60 to 80 species"
  (article "grape") and "about 60 to 70" (article "Vitaceae"); the OIV
  (2017, citing Wen et al. 2007) says 80. The spread comes mostly from
  poorly defined Asian species. Use as "sixty to eighty, depending on whom
  you ask".
- **Source**: https://www.britannica.com/plant/grape ;
  https://www.oiv.int/public/medias/5888/en-distribution-of-the-worlds-grapevine-varieties.pdf
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent (read). **Confidence**: high for
  the range. (Later counts, e.g. 83 in Plants of the World Online in 2025,
  are after 2020: do not use.)

### F005 Where wild Vitis grows
- **Claim**: *Vitis* is native to the north temperate zone (Britannica):
  most species in eastern Asia and in North America, and a single species,
  *V. vinifera*, in Europe and western Asia.
- **Source**: https://www.britannica.com/plant/grape ; OIV 2017 (above),
  which groups the genus into American (more than 20 species), East Asian
  (around 55) and Eurasian (*V. vinifera* alone).
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent. **Confidence**: high.

### F006 Vitis vinifera and its wild ancestor (2020 view)
- **Claim**: The cultivated grape, *Vitis vinifera* subsp. *vinifera*, was
  domesticated from the wild *V. vinifera* subsp. *sylvestris*; the view in
  2020 placed domestication in western Asia (the Near East) about 8,000
  years ago. Wild vines have male and female plants (dioecious); cultivated
  varieties mostly have hermaphrodite flowers.
- **Source**: Myles et al. 2011, PNAS 108(9):3530–3535,
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21245334/ ; Picq et al. 2014, BMC Plant
  Biology, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4167142/ ; Vasconcelos et
  al. 2009, AJEV 60:411, https://www.ajevonline.org/content/60/4/411
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent (Myles: citation verified, abstract
  not read). **Confidence**: high. NOT for the book: Dong et al. 2023
  (Science), two domestications about 11,000 years ago (after 2020).
- **Share of world grapes that are vinifera**: not verified; say "the great
  majority", not a percentage.

### F007 How many grape varieties
- **Claim**: The OIV (2017) speaks of "10 000 known grapevine varieties", of
  which 13 cover more than a third of the world's vineyard area and 33 cover
  half; the Vitis International Variety Catalogue listed 21,045 variety
  names (12,250 for *V. vinifera*), many of them synonyms, and the true
  number of *vinifera* varieties is estimated at about 6,000. Britannica:
  "at least 5,000 reported varieties".
- **Source**: OIV, "Distribution of the world's grapevine varieties" (Focus
  2017), https://www.oiv.int/public/medias/5888/en-distribution-of-the-worlds-grapevine-varieties.pdf
  (21,045 cited to Maul & Töpfer 2015; 6,000 to Lacombe 2012)
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent (read in full). **Confidence**: high.

### F008 World grape production, 2018 (as reported in 2019)
- **Claim**: World grape production in 2018 was 77.8 million tonnes: 57%
  wine grapes (including musts and juices), 36% table grapes, 7% dried
  grapes (counted as fresh grapes; about 4 kg of fresh grapes make 1 kg of
  raisins). Vineyards covered 7.4 million hectares. Largest producers
  (million tonnes): China 11.7, Italy 8.6, USA 6.9, Spain 6.9, France 6.2.
- **Source**: OIV, "2019 Statistical Report on World Vitiviniculture",
  https://www.oiv.int/public/medias/6782/oiv-2019-statistical-report-on-world-vitiviniculture.pdf
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent (read in full). **Confidence**: high.
  Later OIV revisions raise 2018 to 80.6 Mt; a 2020 writer quotes 77.8.

### F009 The berry, botanically
- **Claim**: Botanically the grape is a berry: skin (exocarp), flesh
  (mesocarp) and the thin layers around the seeds (endocarp), all from the
  ovary wall; no stone. The ovary has two compartments, each with two
  ovules, so a grape has at most four seeds; seeded cultivars have one to
  four. The skin carries a waxy "bloom".
- **Source**: Britannica (above); Vasconcelos et al. 2009 (above); NMSU
  Extension guide H-338 (Giese et al., August 2020),
  https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_h/H338/ ; Colorado State University,
  https://www.lsop.colostate.edu/2020/04/29/everything-you-need-to-know-is-that-a-berry/
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent. **Confidence**: high. (NMSU guide
  dated August 2020: fine to use for the fact, which is old; do not cite the
  guide by date in the book.)

### F010 The cluster
- **Claim**: A bunch of grapes is botanically a panicle. Its stalk before
  the first branch is the peduncle, its main axis the rachis; each berry
  hangs on a pedicel. A cluster can carry as many as 1,000 flowers; fruit
  set is considered good if about one flower in three becomes a berry.
- **Source**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grape_cluster (citing Winkler,
  General Viticulture, 1974, p. 118); NMSU H-338 (above).
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent. **Confidence**: medium-high
  (terms); high (flowers, fruit set).

### F011 Berries per cluster, berry size (examples only)
- **Claim**: No general range verified. Example: in one study, Cabernet
  Sauvignon clusters under normal conditions had 135 ± 16 berries (Keller et
  al., 2022: after 2020, use only as background, not in the book). The OIV
  scale for describing varieties runs from berries of about 1 g to about 9 g
  and more (e.g. about 3 g for Muscat à petits grains, 9 g or more for
  Cardinal) and bunches from up to about 100 g to about 900 g and more (about
  300 g for Chardonnay).
- **Source**: OIV Descriptor List, 2nd ed. (descriptors 220, 221, 502, 503),
  https://www.vivc.de/docs/Code_descripteurs_2ed_EN.pdf
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent (read). **Confidence**: high for the
  OIV scale.

### F012 Véraison
- **Claim**: Véraison is the onset of ripening: the berries soften, change
  colour and begin to accumulate sugar; it falls roughly 8 to 10 weeks after
  flowering (mid to late summer in the north).
- **Source**: NMSU H-338 (above); Pilati et al. 2014, BMC Plant Biology,
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4021102/
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent. **Confidence**: high. Etymology:
  not verified.

### F013 The "foxy" taste and methyl anthranilate
- **Claim**: Methyl anthranilate is considered responsible for the
  distinctive "foxy" aroma and flavour of the Concord grape (*Vitis
  labrusca*); it was first identified in grape juice in 1921 (Power and
  Chesnut), in labrusca-type grapes and not in *vinifera*. Other compounds
  contribute (2-aminoacetophenone, furaneol).
- **Source**: Robinson et al. 2014, AJEV 65(1):1,
  https://www.ajevonline.org/content/65/1/1 ; Power & Chesnut 1921, Journal of
  the American Chemical Society 43:1741–1742 (citation from summaries).
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent. **Confidence**: high (Robinson);
  medium (the 1921 citation details).

### F014 Ephraim Wales Bull
- **Claim**: The Concord grape was raised by Ephraim Wales Bull (note:
  Wales, not "Wall"), born in Boston on 4 March 1806, died in Concord,
  Massachusetts, on 26 September 1895. He moved to Concord in August 1836,
  to the house now called Grapevine Cottage on Lexington Road.
- **Source**: Concord Free Public Library, finding aid "Ephraim Wales Bull",
  https://concordlibrary.org/special-collections/fin_aids/Bull_E (read
  2026-10-01, summary of the page)
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, read. **Confidence**: high.
- **Unresolved**: his trade. The finding aid (as summarised) says
  "goldsmith"; the research agent reported "goldbeater". Do not state his
  trade until a primary source is read.

### F015 The Concord's dates
- **Claim**: Bull sowed seeds from a wild grape in the autumn of 1843 (his
  own account, quoted secondhand); the selected vine first bore good fruit
  in 1849 (the date used by the USDA National Agricultural Library and by
  Welch's); he exhibited it at the Massachusetts Horticultural Society in
  1853; it was first marketed by C. M. Hovey of Boston in 1854.
- **Source**: USDA NAL, https://www.nal.usda.gov/exhibits/speccoll/items/show/1067 ;
  Welch's, https://www.welchs.com/b2b/about-us/who-we-are/ ; Concord library
  (F014); New England Historical Society,
  https://newenglandhistoricalsociety.com/concord-grape-great-want-country/
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent; 1854 and Hovey read in the
  library finding aid. **Confidence**: high for 1849 and 1854; medium for
  1843 (secondhand quotation); medium that it won first prize in 1853.
- **Unresolved**: Bull also described two generations of seedlings, which
  does not fit 1843 to 1849 cleanly. Avoid exact numbers of seedlings
  (22,000 and 2,000 both circulate; neither verified).

### F016 Bull's later life and epitaph
- **Claim**: Bull served in the Massachusetts legislature (House from 1855
  or 1856; later the Senate). He died in 1895 at the Concord Home for the
  Aged. He is buried in Sleepy Hollow Cemetery, Concord; his epitaph reads
  "He sowed, others reaped" (punctuation varies between sources; the
  words do not). It is widely said that he died poor.
- **Source**: Concord library (F014); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephraim_Wales_Bull ;
  https://patch.com/massachusetts/concord/ephraim-w-bull-and-the-origin-of-the-concord-grape
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent. **Confidence**: high for the
  epitaph's words and place; medium for "died poor" (attribute: "it is
  said"). Punctuation: not verified from a photograph of the stone; render
  the words without claiming the stone's punctuation.

### F017 What the Concord is
- **Claim**: The Concord is usually called a cultivar of *Vitis labrusca*,
  the fox grape, but it is a hybrid with some *V. vinifera* in its ancestry
  (Huber et al. 2016 make it an offspring of 'Catawba' and an unknown wild
  parent).
- **Source**: Huber et al. 2016, VITIS,
  https://ojs.openagrar.de/index.php/VITIS/article/view/6422/6048 ;
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concord_grape
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent. **Confidence**: high. (Wen et al.,
  Frontiers in Plant Science, published 17 March 2020, is after the
  narrator's January–February writing: do not cite in chapter 1.)

### F018 Thomas Bramwell Welch and the juice
- **Claim**: Thomas Bramwell Welch (1825–1903), born in Glastonbury, England,
  a Methodist and a prohibitionist, practised as a dentist in Vineland, New
  Jersey. In 1869 he pasteurized grape juice to keep it from fermenting, to
  make an unfermented wine for communion. Welch's says he used 40 pounds of
  Concord grapes from in front of his house.
- **Source**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Bramwell_Welch ;
  https://www.welchs.com/b2b/about-us/who-we-are/ ; Cumberland County, NJ,
  https://www.cumberlandcountynj.gov/content/22604/23487/23497/24185.aspx
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent. **Confidence**: high (the 40
  pounds: attribute to Welch's).

### F019 Welch's moves to Westfield; jelly

- **Source**: Westfield village history, https://westfieldny.com/living-here/history ;

### F020 Peanut butter and jelly
- **Claim**: The first published peanut-butter-and-jelly recipe (Julia Davis
  Chandler, Boston Cooking-School Magazine, 1901) used currant or crab-apple
  jelly, not grape. The sandwich became a childhood staple in the
  Depression, helped by sliced bread (1928) and cheaper peanut butter; the
  claim that it began with soldiers in the Second World War is folklore,
  though soldiers did eat it (tins of Skippy went overseas).
- **Source**: JSTOR Daily, https://daily.jstor.org/what-happened-to-peanut-butter-and-jelly
  (summarising Steve Estes, "PB&J", Gastronomica 17:2, 2017) ; Smithsonian,
  https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/brief-history-peanut-butter-180976525/
  (quoting Andrew F. Smith; article dated January 2021, after 2020: cite
  Smith's point, not the article)
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, research agent. **Confidence**: high (1901);
  medium (Depression).

### F021 Grapes in Iowa before 1940 (HOLD for a later chapter)
- **Claim**: In 1919 Iowa ranked sixth in the nation for wine-grape
  production, harvesting more than 12 million pounds of grapes; by 1940 the
  industry "had all but been eliminated". Causes cited: the Armistice Day
  blizzard of 11 November 1940, drift of the herbicide 2,4-D used on corn
  from the mid-1940s, and the shift to row crops. Statewide prohibition
  took effect in Iowa in 1916.
- **Source**: Iowa State University, CALS,
  https://www.cals.iastate.edu/content/department-horticulture-125th-points-pride
  (read 2026-10-01: quotation confirmed); ISU "101 Iowa Grape & Wine
  Industry Facts" (2015), https://www.extension.iastate.edu/wine/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/101_facts_about_ia_grapes_n_wine_aug2015.pdf
  (snippets only); Encyclopedia Dubuque, https://www.encyclopediadubuque.org/index.php/PROHIBITION
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, ISU quotation read; the rest via research agent.
  **Confidence**: high (1919 rank and 12 million pounds); medium (causes;
  the 1916 date's exact day).

### F025 Why the Concord succeeded
- **Claim**: Bull wanted "a good table and wine grape, which shall also be
  early, hardy and prolific" (his words, as quoted by the New England
  Historical Society). The grapes then available in New England often died
  in the winter or could not ripen before the first autumn frost; the
  Concord ripened early and survived hard winters. European *vinifera*
  vines are not cold-hardy and are prone to disease in the eastern U.S.
- **Source**: New England Historical Society, "The Concord Grape – 'The
  Great Want of this Country...'",
  https://newenglandhistoricalsociety.com/concord-grape-great-want-country/ ;
  MOFGA, "Growing Grapes", https://www.mofga.org/resources/fruit/growing-grapes/
- **Checked**: 2026-10-01, search summaries (secondary). **Confidence**:
  medium. Note: one summary of the NEHS page says the grape was
  "introduced in 1848"; this conflicts with F015 (1849 first fruit, 1854
  market). Keep F015's dates.
