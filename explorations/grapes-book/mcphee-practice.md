# McPhee Practice Notebook

## Exercise 1: The Pine Barrens opening

McPhee wrote:

> Fred Brown's house is on an unpaved road that curves along the edge of a
> wide cranberry bog. What attracted me to it was the pump that stands in
> his yard. It was something of a wonder that I noticed the pump, because
> there were, among other things, eight automobiles in the yard, two of
> them on their sides and one of them upside down, all ten years old or
> older.

Continuing:

The pump was cast iron, with a long handle that had been worn to a
polish by decades of hands. I worked it. Nothing came for the first
several strokes, and then a column of water rose and poured out cold
and clear into a tin cup that someone had wired to the spout. The water
had no taste at all, or rather it tasted the way water is supposed to
taste before anything is done to it, and I drank a second cup and then
a third. In South Jersey, the water underground is acid enough to
dissolve iron pipes and patient enough to do it over twenty years. The
Pine Barrens aquifer sits in deep sand, filtered by it, and protected
by the fact that almost nobody has reason to contaminate it. The water I
was drinking had fallen as rain sometime during the Eisenhower
administration and had been working its way down through white cedar
roots and sugar sand ever since. It was in no hurry. Nothing in the
Pine Barrens is in a hurry except fire.

## Exercise 2: Basin and Range, the roadcut

McPhee wrote:

> Interstate 80, in its complete traverse of the North American continent,
> goes through much open space and three tunnels. As it happens, one
> tunnel passes through young rock, another through middle-aged rock, and
> the third through rock that is fairly old, at least with respect to the
> rock now on earth which has not long since been recycled.

Continuing:

The distances between the tunnels are not symmetrical. From Carlin
Canyon to Yerba Buena Island is four hundred and fifty miles; from
Yerba Buena to Green River is seven hundred and eighty. A driver making
the whole trip, east to west, would spend most of the first day in rock
that was already ancient when the dinosaurs arrived, cross the Sierra
Nevada through granite that was once the root of mountains twice as
tall, and finish in sediment that was lake mud when there were camels in
Wyoming. The rock gets younger as you go west, until it doesn't: the
Coast Ranges are old again, Franciscan melange, material that was
scraped off the ocean floor and plastered against the continent like
plaster against lath. Deffeyes, who could read the roadcuts the way
most people read billboards, would sometimes fall silent for fifty
miles and then say, with no preamble, "Oligocene." He had been
thinking, and the rock agreed with him.

## Exercise 3: Oranges, the juice machine

McPhee wrote:

> The custom of drinking orange juice with breakfast is not very
> widespread, taking the world as a whole, and it is thought by many
> peoples to be a distinctly American habit.

Continuing:

The Danes drink it, and the Israelis, and a certain kind of hotel
everywhere serves it in a small glass with a paper doily underneath,
but the eight-ounce tumbler of juice beside a plate of eggs is an
American arrangement, and its history is shorter than most Americans
suppose. In 1920, an orange grower in California who could not sell his
crop had the idea of squeezing it instead. The crop that year was
enormous. He bought a hand juicer, set up a roadside stand, and sold
fresh juice at five cents a glass. Within a decade, the idea had moved
indoors, and the orange-juice extractor had become a standard piece of
American kitchen equipment, somewhere between the toaster and the
rolling pin. By 1950, the fresh-squeezed glass was already being
displaced by something that came in a can. The can held concentrate.
The concentrate was orange juice with most of the water taken out. You
added the water back yourself, three cans to one, stirred with a
wooden spoon, and believed you were drinking orange juice, which in a
technical sense you were.

## Exercise 4: Coming into the Country, the river

McPhee wrote:

> From the hull, meanwhile, came the steady sound of sandpaper, of
> sliding stones, of rain on a metal roof -- the sound of the rock in
> the river, put there by alpine glaciers.

Continuing:

The glaciers had been gone twelve thousand years, but every pebble in
the river was a piece of the mountains they had carried down, and the
river was still sorting it. Big stones near the banks, fine gravel in
the channel, sand on the inside of the bends, silt where the current
slowed to nothing in an oxbow. The water did this work every day, and
had been doing it since the ice left. If you thought of a year as a
second, the river had been sorting gravel for about three and a half
hours. It was not finished. Geologically, it would never be finished.
The mountains kept pushing up and the frost kept breaking them apart
and the river kept moving the pieces toward the sea. The Kobuk was a
conveyor belt made of water. Its cargo was the Brooks Range, in pieces
small enough to carry.

## Exercise 5: The Search for Marvin Gardens

McPhee wrote:

> In the 1930's, men visiting Atlantic City went to jail, directly to
> jail, did not pass Go, for appearing in topless bathing suits on the
> beach.

Continuing:

The boardwalk in those years was seven miles long and sixty feet wide,
made of yellow pine from the Carolinas, and the shoes of a quarter of a
million visitors wore the grain of it smooth every summer. Hotels stood
along it like entries in an architectural competition that nobody could
win. The Marlborough-Blenheim was Moorish. The Traymore was Second
Empire. The Shelburne was something no one could name. The Brighton was
whatever rich people wanted it to be, which was mostly invisible from
the street. All of them are gone now, or nearly all. The wrecking ball
preceded the casino by a decent interval and followed it by none.
Where the Traymore stood, there is a parking garage. The parking garage
has its name in lights.

## Exercise 6: Encounters with the Archdruid

McPhee wrote:

> A small cabin stands in the Glacier Peak Wilderness, about a hundred
> yards off a trail that crosses the Cascade Range. In midsummer, the
> cabin looked strange in the forest. It was only twelve feet square,
> but it rose fully two stories and then had a high and steeply peaked
> roof. From the ridge of the roof, moreover, a ten-foot pole stuck
> straight up. Tied to the top of the pole was a shovel.

Continuing:

The shovel was for digging out the door. In a winter of average
snowfall, which in the North Cascades means about thirty feet, the
cabin would be buried to the ridgeline by January and the pole would
be all that showed. A traveller on snowshoes who found it could dig
down to the door and let himself in and build a fire in the stove and
wait out whatever needed waiting out. This was the system. It was the
reason the cabin rose two stories on a twelve-foot base: the first
story was the part that filled up with snow. The stove was on the
second floor. The firewood was on the second floor. Everything you
needed in winter was on the second floor, because in winter the first
floor was not a floor, it was a hole in the snow with a cabin at the
bottom of it. Fred Darvill, who built the cabin, had come to this
understanding after his first winter, when he built a fire in a stove
on the ground floor and sat beside it in six feet of meltwater.

## Exercise 7: Giving Good Weight, the market

McPhee wrote nothing I have here for this one, so I'll begin fresh in
his voice, on his subject: a farmers' market.

You set up the table at five in the morning, while the city was still
on its back. The peppers came out of the truck in bushel baskets, the
tomatoes in flats, and you put them on the table in a way that was not
exactly a display and not exactly a pile. You put the good ones where
people could see them and the less good ones where people could reach
them. This was not deception. It was salesmanship. The difference is
that deception hides the bad ones and salesmanship puts the good ones
forward. By six, you had the awning out and the cash box open and
the scale zeroed. The scale was a hanging scale, the old kind, with a
brass dial and a steel hook and a galvanized pan that could hold ten
pounds of plums. The weight of the pan was the tare. You subtracted it
every time. Or you didn't, and the customer got a free quarter pound.
Giving good weight was what you did when you tipped the pan and let a
few extra beans roll in. It cost you almost nothing and it bought you
a customer for the rest of the summer.

## Exercise 8: Oranges, the fruit itself

Starting fresh in McPhee's voice, on the physical orange:

An orange is a hesperidium, which is a kind of berry. The skin is a
rind, technically the pericarp, and the white material between the rind
and the flesh is the albedo. The flesh is divided into sections, usually
ten, separated by walls of membrane so thin that they are transparent
when you hold them up to a window. Each section contains juice
vesicles, which are the individual sacs of juice that burst when you
bite into the orange, and which are visible if you cut a section in
half and look at the cut surface. They look like fish eggs. The number
of seeds varies from none to about twenty-five, and is partly a
function of pollination: a navel orange, which is seedless, is
seedless because it is parthenocarpic, meaning that the fruit develops
without fertilization, meaning that the flower does not need to be
visited. A Valencia, which has seeds, has been visited. In commercial
groves, the visitor is almost always a bee. The bee is not interested
in making oranges. The bee is interested in making honey, and would
prefer not to spend its time in an orange grove, but the grower keeps
hives among the trees and the bees do not have a choice.

## Notes to myself

What I notice about these exercises:

1. The continuation works best when I let the facts carry the prose.
   When I have a specific number, a specific name, a specific mechanism,
   the sentence writes itself. When I don't, I drift into opinion or
   impression, which is not McPhee.

2. McPhee's humor is always embedded in a fact. The Vice-Chancellor
   who dated creation at 9 a.m. The man who built a fire on the ground
   floor and sat in meltwater. The parking garage with its name in
   lights. The joke is never separate from the information.

3. His sentences come in two sizes: short and declarative ("The stove
   was on the second floor.") or long and cumulative, a sequence of
   clauses that add detail like bricks. He does not mix the two within
   a paragraph arbitrarily; the short ones punctuate.

4. He never says "I felt" or "it seemed." He says what was there.

5. The hardest thing to get right is the absence of commentary. McPhee
   trusts the arrangement to do the work. When I add "This was the
   system," that's close but it's already more than he would say. He
   would just describe the system and let you notice that it was one.

## Exercise 9: Levels of the Game, the serve

McPhee wrote:

> Arthur Ashe, his feet apart, his knees slightly bent, lifts a tennis
> ball into the air. The toss is high and forward.

Continuing:

Graebner, at the far end, takes a step to his left and then does not
move again. He has picked his side. Ashe, coiling, brings the racquet
back behind his head in a motion that begins slowly and then
accelerates through an arc so fast that the racquet disappears for a
moment, like a spoke in a wheel. The ball comes off the strings at a
hundred and twelve miles an hour. Graebner, who has guessed right, gets
his racquet on it and blocks it back, a chip, and the ball floats over
the net with no pace at all and drops into the service box like a
wounded bird. Ashe is already there. He could hit it anywhere. He hits
it at Graebner's feet, because that is the one place where Graebner's
size works against him. The big man bends and scoops and the ball goes
into the net. Fifteen-love. Ashe walks back to the baseline and bounces
a new ball once, twice, three times. He does not hurry. Hurrying is
Graebner's problem.

## Exercise 10: The Pine Barrens, the water

McPhee wrote:

> The water of the Pine Barrens is soft and pure, and there is so much
> of it that, like the forest above it, it is an incongruity in place
> and time. In the sand under the pines is a natural reservoir of pure
> water that, in volume, is the equivalent of a lake seventy-five feet
> deep with a surface of a thousand square miles.

Continuing:

The aquifer is called the Cohansey, after a creek. It is not confined
by rock. It sits in sand, and the sand sits on clay, and the clay is
the floor. Rain falls on the Pine Barrens and goes down through the
sand and joins the Cohansey, and the process takes anywhere from ten
years to a hundred, depending on where the rain falls and how far it
has to go. The pine trees above, which look like they are growing in
dry ground, are in fact standing on a lake. Their roots go down into
it. The bogs and the streams are places where the surface of the lake
comes up to the surface of the land. When Fred Brown pumps water from
his well, he is dipping into the same body of water that feeds
Batsto River three miles to the south. The Cohansey does not know about
property lines, or township boundaries, or the twenty-six municipalities
that sit on top of it. It is one thing. So far, it has been left alone,
because nobody has found a compelling reason to pump it to Philadelphia.

## Exercise 11: Silk Parachute, the mother

McPhee wrote:

> When your mother is ninety-nine years old, you have so many memories
> of her that they tend to overlap, intermingle, and blur.

Continuing:

She was a small woman. She never weighed more than a hundred and
fifteen pounds. She kept her hair short after 1960, and before that she
kept it pinned, and before that I don't know what she did with her hair
because I was not yet alive. She could drive a car, play the piano,
set a broken bone in a dog, and balance a chequebook to the penny. She
could not carry a tune. She admitted this freely. She would open her
mouth in church and what came out was not a hymn but an assertion that
a hymn was taking place. My father, beside her, sang in a clear tenor,
and never once looked at her when she sang. He had learned not to.

## Exercise 12: A subject McPhee never wrote about: grapes

Now writing about grapes, in McPhee's voice, with no plot, no
narrator's mystery, just the subject:

A grape is a berry. Botanically, it has everything a berry requires and
nothing it does not: a skin, which is the exocarp; flesh, which is the
mesocarp; and seeds, surrounded by a thin tissue that is the endocarp.
All of this develops from the wall of the ovary of a single flower.
The strawberry, which most people would call a berry without hesitation,
is not one. Most of its flesh comes from the receptacle, and the things
that look like seeds on its surface are the actual fruits, each one an
achene. The raspberry is not a berry either. It is a cluster of tiny
drupes, each with its own seed, clinging together on a white cone that
stays behind on the plant when you pick the fruit. The grape, the
tomato, the banana, and the avocado are berries. The strawberry, the
raspberry, and the blackberry are not. Taxonomy is not interested in
what things look like. It is interested in what things are.

The grape has two seed compartments, with two ovules each, so the
maximum number of seeds is four. Most seeded varieties produce one to
three. Seedless grapes are not empty; they contain the traces of seeds
that began to develop and stopped, small soft things you can chew
without noticing. The condition is called stenospermocarpy, a word that
means, roughly, narrow-seed-fruiting, and it is not the absence of
seeds but the failure of seeds. Thompson Seedless, the grape most
Americans buy by the pound, is stenospermocarpic. So is the Flame. So
is the Crimson. The seeds are there. They just didn't finish.

## Exercise 13: Grapes, continued -- the bunch

The bunch of grapes you buy at the supermarket is a panicle. It has a
main stem, which is the rachis, and side branches, and the berries
hang from short individual stems called pedicels. The panicle began as
a flower cluster, and the flower cluster began as a shoot tip that
could have gone either way: it could have become a tendril or it could
have become flowers. In wild grapes, this decision is made by the
vine, and light has something to do with it. A shoot in deep shade
will make tendrils. A shoot in full sun will make flowers. The vine is
practical. There is no use fruiting where no bird will see the fruit.

I once bought a bunch of green grapes at a store in St. Helena, in the
Napa Valley, in January. They were from Chile. In January, in the
northern hemisphere, the table grapes come from the south, and Chile
has been the leading supplier for decades. The box said "Product of
Chile." The grapes were cold and firm and had a pale dust on them,
which is the bloom, a waxy coating that the berry makes to protect
itself. I rubbed one grape with my thumb and the bloom came off and the
skin underneath was a darker green and slightly wet-looking. You cannot
put the bloom back. Once it is disturbed, it is gone, like the
finish on a new pair of shoes after the first rain.

I counted the berries on this bunch. There were a hundred and seventeen.
I counted them again and got a hundred and eighteen. I am a careful
person, and the disagreement between the two counts bothered me more
than it should have, so I counted a third time and got a hundred and
seventeen. I accepted a hundred and seventeen. It is the answer you get
most often.

## Second round of notes

Getting closer. Some things I notice in this round:

1. When writing about grapes, the voice holds as long as I have facts.
   The stenospermocarpy passage works because the word itself is
   interesting and the explanation is concrete. The bloom passage works
   because of the shoe analogy, which is the kind of small comparison
   McPhee makes: domestic, physical, exact.

2. The counting passage (exercise 13) is the closest I've come to the
   actual book's narrator. The obsessive recount, the acceptance of
   the most frequent answer. McPhee would do this. He would count
   something that did not need counting, and he would tell you the
   discrepancy, and he would not explain why it bothered him. The
   explanation is in the fact of reporting it.

3. The mother exercise (11) risks going too far into character. McPhee
   gives physical detail and behavioral detail, but he doesn't
   psychologize. "He had learned not to" is about as far into motive
   as he goes.

4. I'm still occasionally adding a sentence that explains or comments.
   "Taxonomy is not interested in what things look like. It is
   interested in what things are." That's an editorial sentence. McPhee
   would let the list of berries-that-aren't and non-berries-that-are
   do the work without the summary. Or he would put it differently,
   without the personification: "The classification has nothing to do
   with appearance."
