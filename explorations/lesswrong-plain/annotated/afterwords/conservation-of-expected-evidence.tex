\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The essay opens with Friedrich Spee, a seventeenth-century priest who described how accused witches were condemned whatever they did: a bad life proved guilt, and so did a good one; fear proved guilt, and so did calm. Having seen many cases, Spee could see that every branch led to the same verdict.

The author states a general rule, which he names Conservation of Expected Evidence: before you look, the average of the probabilities you might end up with must equal the probability you hold now. If one outcome would raise your belief, the other must lower it. A likely confirmation can move you only a little; an unlikely refutation must move you a lot. So if silence is evidence for a hidden enemy, sabotage must be evidence against one, and anyone who says God hides Himself to test faith must count the miracles of the Bible against Him. A perfect reasoner cannot plan to be confirmed and can only test. The essay ends by remarking that human psychology is badly flawed.

\responsehead

The essay's mathematics is one line of a first probability course, the law of total probability. The essay gives it a new name, modeled on the conservation laws of physics, which the author supplies himself (``which I would name''). The step the name refers to, that the expected change in belief is zero, is never written out. A tautology, P(H) = P(H), is printed in bold in its place.

One paragraph, on confident theories gaining little from success and risking much from failure, states the rule in a form a reader can use. Elsewhere the essay overstates it twice. ``On average, you must expect to be exactly as confident as when you started out'' is false if confident means sure, since evidence is expected to reduce uncertainty. ``For a true Bayesian, it is impossible to seek evidence that confirms a theory'' is false in ordinary English, as the essay's own paragraph on likely confirmations shows. Both errors come from the same slide, between ``expect'' as an average and ``expect'' in its everyday sense.

The essay's use of its examples shows what it is for. Spee's witch trials show a tribunal that treated every outcome as guilt. Spee saw this without probability theory, and the essay does not show what the theory adds. The one example the essay builds itself is aimed at religion, and it is invalid. The law pairs the two outcomes of one observation; present silence and ancient miracle stories are two different observations. The believer's argument, as the essay states it, explains silence; it does not claim silence as evidence. The reader is then told to heed ``that quiet strain in the back of your mind.'' A reader who follows the instruction learns to feel that religious argument is wrong without checking whether the essay's argument against it is right.

The ending, ``Human psychology is so screwed up,'' completes the move. The ideal reasoner can relax; humans cannot; the essay's reader is invited to stand with the first and look down on the second. There is no advice for a human judge, scientist or believer on how to act on the rule.

In short: a textbook identity given a physicist's name, overstated twice, and misapplied in the one example the author built to use against his opponents.
\end{afterword}
