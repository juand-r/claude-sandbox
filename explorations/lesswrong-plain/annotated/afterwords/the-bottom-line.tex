\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The essay argues that a conclusion is only as good as the process that chose it. A ``clever arguer'' is paid to argue that box B holds a diamond. He writes the conclusion first, at the bottom of the page, and then lists above it only the clues that favor box B.

The author's point is that once the conclusion is written, the arguments added above it cannot change what it is worth. It tells you who paid, not what is in the box. Compare a curious observer who lists the clues for both boxes and only then estimates a probability. That number depends on the evidence.

The same happens inside one head. If you decide your squealing brakes don't need fixing because repairs are expensive, and then look for reasons, the reasons are decoration. The essay ends with a warning: this is a check on your own thinking, not a weapon against other people's conclusions.

\responsehead

The central image is excellent. Writing the conclusion first, at the bottom of the page, is a picture of motivated reasoning that stays in the mind. The closing warning against turning the idea on opponents shows good judgment.

The argument has one real flaw, and the author fixed it himself the next day. The clues the arguer lists are real clues. ``Box B has a blue stamp'' is evidence whoever says it. What the listener lacks is the clues left out. So the right response is to discount the argument for selection, not to throw it away. Yudkowsky's follow-up, ``What Evidence Filtered Evidence?'', says exactly this. The detour through parallel universes adds nothing that ordinary probability does not already give.

The idea has a long history. Benjamin Franklin tells in his autobiography how he gave up eating fish on principle. Then he saw smaller fish in the stomach of a freshly caught cod, and reasoned that if fish eat one another, he could eat them. His comment: ``So convenient a thing it is to be a reasonable creature, since it enables one to find or make a reason for everything one has a mind to do.'' Psychologists call this motivated reasoning; Ziva Kunda's 1990 review, ``The Case for Motivated Reasoning,'' is the standard reference.

Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber went further in 2011. They argued that human reasoning evolved to produce and evaluate arguments in conversation. On their account, the one-sided search for supporting arguments is what reasoning does by default. It gives good results when people with different views argue with each other and check each other's work. That is a more social and more hopeful picture than the essay's lone thinker guarding their bottom line.

As writing, the essay is too long for its point. Archaic phrases (``signs and portents'') and borrowed physics (``entanglement'') stand where plain words would do. A student would get high marks for the idea and be asked to cut a third.
\end{afterword}
