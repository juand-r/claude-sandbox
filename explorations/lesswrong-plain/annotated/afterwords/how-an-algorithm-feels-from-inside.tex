\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The post accepts the standard analysis of the falling-tree riddle: one side means acoustic vibrations by ``sound,'' the other means auditory experience, so the dispute is about a word. It asks why people argue about it at all.

The author returns to his invented example of ``bleggs'' and ``rubes,'' objects sorted by color, shape and contents. Once every feature of an object is known, the question ``is it really a blegg?'' has nothing left to stand for. He compares two neural networks. In the first, every unit is an observable feature. In the second, the features connect through a central category unit, which can stay active or inactive after all features are fixed. He suggests that the brain resembles the second network, and that this dangling unit is what the leftover question feels like from the inside.

The same feeling, he argues, drives disputes about the tree and about whether Pluto is a planet. People see their intuitions not as intuitions but as the world itself, and so they reject explanations of how their thinking goes wrong.

\responsehead

The post takes an old answer, attaches it to a hypothetical piece of brain design, and ends with an account of disagreement that no disagreement can touch.

The answer is old. The first printed version of the riddle, in 1883, already settled it by fixing a definition (``Sound is the sensation excited in the ear''). In 1907 William James settled a dispute about whether a man ``goes round'' a squirrel by distinguishing two meanings, and he recorded the reaction this post sets out to explain: some of the disputants called the distinction ``a shuffling evasion'' and insisted on ``plain honest English.'' The post presents the analysis as ``the standard rationalist view'' and credits no one.

The explanation rests on one claim: that brains categorize like Network 2, with a central unit that can vary after every feature is fixed. The only support offered is that Network 2 is ``fast, cheap, scalable,'' and the claim is hedged three times in one clause (``a far better candidate for being something vaguely like''). No neuroscience is cited. The diagram was drawn to have a dangling unit, and the dangling unit is then identified with the feeling it was drawn to explain. That is a picture of the explanandum, not an explanation of it.

The example is built for the conclusion. Bleggs are binary, fully observable, and useful only for sorting. The one real case, Pluto, is not like that, and the post's own tool shows it. A category word stands for a practical question, and ``is Pluto a planet?'' stood for one: which list the solar system's planets should be, once bodies like Eris had been found. The International Astronomical Union had to decide it in 2006. The post then declares that even saying ``it depends on how you define `planet''' is a Network 2 symptom. At that point every possible answer is a symptom, and the theory forbids nothing, which is the author's own definition of a belief that floats.

Its ending gives the reader something to use. People who resist ``everything you try to say about how the native cognitive algorithm goes astray'' are doing so because they cannot see their intuitions as intuitions. The link on ``cling to their intuitions'' goes to the author's reply to commenters who disagreed with him. The lesson the reader carries away is that critics of the author's account are showing the bias it describes.

In short: James's 1907 answer, uncredited, attached to a brain diagram with no evidence, applied wrongly to Pluto, and closed with a rule that turns every critic into a symptom.
\end{afterword}
