\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The essay lists the habits of mind that the author thinks make up good thinking:
\begin{itemize}
\item curiosity: wanting to know;
\item relinquishment: giving up beliefs the truth destroys;
\item lightness: changing your mind quickly;
\item evenness: holding favored and unfavored ideas to the same standard;
\item argument: discussing honestly with others;
\item empiricism: tying beliefs to observation;
\item simplicity: avoiding needless detail;
\item humility, which the author defines as preparing for your own errors;
\item perfectionism: never settling for almost right;
\item precision: making narrow claims that can be tested;
\item scholarship: learning many fields.
\end{itemize}
A twelfth, unnamed virtue comes before all the others: aiming at the true answer rather than at following rules. The author illustrates it with Musashi's \textsc{Book of Five Rings}, where every movement of the sword must aim to cut. The whole essay is written as a series of commands and maxims.

\responsehead

The essay reads like scripture, and it works like scripture. It speaks in commands. It borrows the authority of a samurai manual and the paradoxes of Taoism. It ends with a virtue that cannot be named and a promise that long practice will one day reveal ``the center.'' Its aim is to shape a character and give its readers an identity. Argument is almost absent.

The clearest sign is what it does to ordinary words. The dictionary defines humility as ``freedom from pride or arrogance'' (Merriam-Webster). The essay redefines it as taking ``specific actions in anticipation of your own errors.'' That is prudence, and a proud person can practice it. The redefinition removes the one thing humility has always meant, and the text shows why that is convenient. The humility section itself calls most people's grasp of rationality ``abysmal.'' The first virtue tells readers to ``be wary of those who \ldots\ modestly confess their ignorance.'' Modesty is treated with suspicion throughout. Other words are bent the same way: perfectionism, usually a fault, becomes a virtue.

The essay does not hold itself to its own virtues. It tells readers to trust observation over authority, in the voice of an authority. It condemns the Great Teacher who says the sky is green while issuing commands no reader can check. It lists relinquishment as a virtue and never relinquishes anything: no belief the author held and gave up appears anywhere in it. Its final virtue fails its own sixth: nothing observable would show whether someone had come to ``glimpse the center.''

The example of the two guesses, between 1 and 100 and between 40 and 50, is the one place the essay teaches by showing. A reader who takes the essay as a guide comes away with a set of attitudes, several of them redefined to suit the author, and a strong sense of belonging among the people who hold them. That seems to be what it is for.

In short: a manifesto written as scripture, inconsistent as an account of good thinking, and more concerned with forming a type of person than with making a case.
\end{afterword}
