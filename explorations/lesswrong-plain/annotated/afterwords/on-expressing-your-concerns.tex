\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The post draws three lessons from Asch's conformity experiments: a unanimous group can make many people say black is white, a single dissenter sharply reduces conformity, and conformity returns when the dissenter sides with the group. Dissent helps a group but costs the dissenter, who must keep it up and may be wrong.

An anecdote shows how groups become overconfident. When the author raised possible problems with a project, a newcomer answered each with reassurance. Alone, people doubt themselves; together, politeness stops them from questioning each other, and each takes the other's reassurance for confidence. At the scale of a group this is ``pluralistic ignorance.''

The author and Robin Hanson agree that common knowledge of a factual disagreement means someone is irrational, so for rationalists disagreement is serious. The most important lesson, the post says, is to distinguish expressing a concern from disagreeing. But socially there is little difference: speaking out cannot be undone, and the group will not thank you. The decision is left to the reader.

\responsehead

The post names a real problem well. Doubts that each person keeps to themselves can add up to a group that is surer than any of its members, and a single voiced doubt can release the others. The trouble is in how the post connects this to its sources and in where it ends.

Asch's experiments are made to carry more than they show. Their majorities were confederates with no doubts to hide, so they could not show people suppressing shared worries; that possibility is an extension. The claim that dissent ``was not learned'' reads one condition as the whole result. Asch himself traced the return of conformity to the partner's desertion, and found that a partner who simply left the room had an effect that outlasted his presence.

The theoretical premise is stated without its contested step. Aumann's theorem assumes that the parties share a prior, and Hanson's own statements of the result say so. To conclude that common knowledge of a disagreement shows ``someone must be irrational,'' one needs the further claim that rational agents must share priors. Hanson has argued for a version of that claim, and Hanson's paper notes that others do not accept it. The post gives neither the premise nor the dispute.

The post itself narrows its lesson. The distinction between a concern and a disagreement is called ``the most important lesson,'' and two paragraphs later the post says that socially there is not much difference, that people are not wired for it, and that a group of rationalists could only ``pretend.'' The last paragraph leaves the decision to the reader, with no guidance on when a concern is worth its cost. The one piece of outside advice mentioned, that cynical books such as \textsc{The Prince} counsel hiding dissent, is not what I found in that book. It tells a ruler to prefer advisers who speak freely when asked.

In short: a sound observation about suppressed doubts, attached to experiments that could not show it, to a premise about disagreement stated without its contested step, and to a lesson the post itself says does not hold socially.
\end{afterword}
