\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The essay distinguishes two kinds of nonconformity. Joining a rebellion that already exists is fairly easy. In Asch's conformity experiments a single ally made subjects far more willing to contradict the group. Being the first to dissent, alone, is much harder.

The author argues that ordinary rebellion, such as dressing in black or being vegetarian, is understood by the people around you. Real lonely dissent meets incomprehension instead of hostility, and that is more frightening. His example is cryonics. He says that the fear of thinking really differently, which cryonics demands, is stronger than the fear of death. He offers an evolutionary explanation: being driven out of the band alone was once fatal.

The highest form is the scientific revolutionary, who reaches new territory by pursuing correct answers. He adds that courage is no proof of being right, and that people who, like himself, dissent easily should correct for it. Nonconformity, he concludes, is not a virtue in itself.

\responsehead

The essay is presented as a lesson about courage. What it does is explain why other people do not share the author's unusual beliefs. The people who find cryonics strange are not given a reason. They are given noises (``Huh? What?''), a fear stronger than the fear of death, and an evolutionary history to account for the fear. The people who sign up are given courage. A reader who holds an unpopular belief is offered a flattering account of every blank look they receive, and the account cannot be tested, because disagreement is itself the evidence.

The support is thin at every step. The one experiment cited is reported selectively. In Asch's unanimous-majority condition, most answers were lonely dissent, and a quarter of subjects never conformed at all. The evolutionary story is labeled post facto and then used as fact. The claim that fear of weirdness beats fear of death rests on comparing skydiving with a procedure that cost tens of thousands of dollars and has never been shown to work. The historical claim does not hold up: Ettinger got the idea from a 1931 magazine story, and another writer made the same argument, unpublished, the same year Ettinger's book first appeared.

The structure undoes the argument. For most of its length the essay treats courage as the scarce thing that separates real dissent from fake. Then, in two short paragraphs, it concedes that ``the difficulty'' is in being right, and that dissent is ``just a bias either way.'' If the conclusion is true, the preceding paragraphs were aimed at the wrong target. The essay's own footnote points to Robin Hanson's argument that careless dissenters, not conformity, are the main obstacle to good new ideas. The essay neither answers it nor applies it to cryonics.

The essay also applies its test to everyone but its author. Everyone fakes lonely dissent, it says, except that ``with me it's true.'' The evidence is a childhood spent with books. The irony in the tone is real, but the next paragraph confirms the claim, and nothing in the essay shows the author paying the cost it describes rather than claiming the identity.

In short: a defense of the author's own unpopular cause dressed as a lesson on courage, built on a selectively reported experiment and a doubtful history, and ending with the author claiming the trait it says everyone fakes.
\end{afterword}
