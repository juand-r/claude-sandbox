\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The post is a dialogue. A narrator asks how evolution, a blind and brutal process, could produce beings capable of love. A second voice, addressed as ``you'', answers each case with an evolutionary explanation: mothers love children who carry their genes, friendships pay off in repeated cooperation, and moral argument grew out of tribal politics. Gandhi is explained as someone who believed and acted on general moral propositions.

The narrator keeps insisting that human kindness and our sense of beauty are surprising. The second voice replies that they seem marvelous only because they are our own values. If there were an objective standard of value outside us that agreed with what evolution happened to produce, that would be too much of a coincidence.

The narrator settles for calling human love a ``moral miracle''. The post ends with a fairy tale in which future children ask where love began, and are told of lovers made by something that did not love.

\responsehead

The post is a dialogue in which ``you'' explain and ``I'' ask. The reader is given the voice that knows the science and has the last word, and the questioner is the one who must be talked out of wonder. That choice of pronouns does some of the persuading before any argument is made.

The first real test comes with Gandhi. The questioner says the explanation offered ``could explain any possible human behavior.'' That is the right objection, and it is not answered. The reply is a list of possible causes, hedged three times, and when pressed it retreats to the claim that the story must fit ``the lawful causal development of the universe somehow.'' No one in the dialogue denied that. The question was whether this particular story explains this particular man.

The central argument is that an objective standard of value, matching the values evolution happened to give us, would be ``too much coincidence.'' This is a known position in moral philosophy, the evolutionary debunking argument, published by Sharon Street in 2006 and answered in a literature since. The post treats it as settled by rhetorical questions. It also assumes, at the point where it matters, that there is no standard of value outside human minds. That is the conclusion the argument is supposed to reach. The law that ``a complex pattern must be explained by a cause which is not already that complex pattern'' is likewise stated, not argued, and it is the point in dispute with anyone who holds that love comes from a source that already loves.

The science is offered with more confidence than it has. Flowers in general did not evolve by imitating the mating signals of bees; that deception is known almost only in orchids, and many flowering plants are not pollinated by bees at all. The claim that moral argument bears the ``specific design signature'' of the savanna is asserted, not shown. The post asks whether the reader thinks science ``does not know the story'' of why humans like flowers, and then gives a guess.

The ending is a fairy tale, and it carries two claims the post never argues: our descendants will be designed by us, and civilization will outgrow a single galaxy. Inference: that designed future, with love passed down on purpose, is the ``gift'' of the title.

In short: a dialogue that seats the reader in the winning voice, leaves its best objection unanswered, treats a live philosophical dispute as settled, gets the natural history of flowers wrong, and delivers its vision of the future as a bedtime story.
\end{afterword}
