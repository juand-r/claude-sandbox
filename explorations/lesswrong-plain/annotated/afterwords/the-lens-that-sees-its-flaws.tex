\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The essay argues that humans, unlike other animals, can understand how their own minds produce beliefs, and can use that understanding to correct them. It begins with the physical chain by which light from untied shoelaces becomes a belief that they are untied. A mouse sees, but cannot know it has a visual cortex, so it cannot correct for optical illusions. Humans can, and science is this same capacity made systematic: reasoning about which processes make the mind mirror the world.

The example is a man in an online chatroom who expected no nuclear war for a hundred years and, asked why, answered ``pure hope.'' The author argues that hope is not evidence: across many possible worlds, optimists are not more likely to live in the peaceful ones. What makes you happy tells you about yourself, not about the world. A mind that knows its biases can apply second-order corrections, and this makes it far more powerful.

\responsehead

The essay claims that understanding the flaws of your mind lets you correct them. It never shows a correction. It shows two things: an animal that cannot reflect, and a man who reflected and was mocked for what he found.

Take the demonstration first. The linked image is a motion illusion. Knowing that it is a still picture does not stop the circles turning. The flaw in the lens stays exactly where it was. The most the knowledge buys is a refusal to believe your eyes, and the essay offers no method for deciding when to refuse. Its closing claim, that self-knowledge makes the lens ``far more powerful,'' comes with no evidence, although the author had warned five months earlier that knowing about biases can make people reason worse.

Then the example. The man in the chatroom gave a reason about the world for the near term. Asked about the longer term, he looked at his reason and called it by its name: hope. That is the reflective act the essay praises. The essay treats it as the bad case. It never asks the question it forces on every other reader: what did the author's own forecast rest on? The question he posted, ``If no, why not?'', demanded reasons only from people who expected peace.

The essay is also doing something besides arguing. Eight references to mice set the reader above an animal. The reader is told twice to feel wonder. Science is redefined as the reflective reasoning of one person, and its social machinery, replication and criticism by others, appears only in scare quotes. The redefinition matters. If science is something a single mind does by thinking about thinking, then a reader who has absorbed this vocabulary can think of themself as scientific without doing any of the things that make science reliable. Five links send the reader to more of the author's posts. The parallel worlds and the Tegmark footnote lend the authority of physics to a point that needs only ordinary probability.

The one idea of substance, that a small effect should shift belief by a small amount, is in a footnote, and the footnote announces a calculation it never performs.

In short: an essay about correcting the mind's flaws that corrects nothing, praises reflection, and makes its villain of the one person in it who reflected.
\end{afterword}
