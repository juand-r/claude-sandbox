\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The essay argues that humans, unlike other animals, can understand how their own minds produce beliefs, and can use that understanding to correct them. It begins with the physical chain by which light from untied shoelaces becomes a belief that they are untied. A mouse sees, but cannot know it has a visual cortex, so it cannot correct for optical illusions. Humans can, and science is this same capacity made systematic: reasoning about which processes make the mind mirror the world.

The example is a man in an online chatroom who expected no nuclear war for a hundred years and, asked why, answered ``pure hope.'' The author argues that hope is not evidence: across many possible worlds, optimists are not more likely to live in the peaceful ones. What makes you happy tells you about yourself, not about the world. A mind that knows its biases can apply second-order corrections, and this makes it far more powerful.

\responsehead

The essay promises that understanding your mind's flaws lets you correct them, then never performs a correction. The closing ``reflective correction'' arrives without a definition, a size, or a way to tell whether it worked. And the claim that self-knowledge makes the lens ``far more powerful'' comes with no evidence, although the author had warned five months earlier that knowing about biases can make people reason worse.

The one worked example is weak. An anonymous man in a chatroom said he expected no nuclear war for a hundred years. His stated reason, that current players are not interested, covers the near term; the author asks only about the extension to a hundred years, and ``pure hope'' answers that. The question itself, ``If no, why not?'', asked only those who expected peace to justify their position. From two words the author then builds a causal story about the man's unhappiness and brain.

The material around the example adds references but no content. The central premise, that a mind can be understood and corrected as a machine can, occupies one sentence and is defended with irony. Parallel worlds and a footnote on Tegmark bring contested physics into a point that ordinary probability makes without them: optimism does not predict peace. The principle at the center, that what you want to be true is not evidence, is correct and old. It is called wishful thinking.

The one substantial idea, that a small effect should shift belief by a small amount, appears only in a footnote. It is the whole answer to how large a correction should be, and the footnote announces a calculation it never performs.

In short: an essay about correcting the mind's flaws that names the correction but never shows one, and whose one example is a remembered chat in which only one side was asked for reasons.
\end{afterword}
