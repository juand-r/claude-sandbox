\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The post argues that people expect others to understand their words as they intend them, because they themselves know what they mean. It calls this the illusion of transparency and relates it to hindsight bias.

It reports several experiments by Boaz Keysar and colleagues. Subjects who knew that a restaurant was bad tended to predict that a listener would hear sarcasm in a message praising it. Subjects taught one meaning of an obscure idiom expected uninformed listeners to arrive at the same meaning. Speakers who said ambiguous sentences estimated that they were understood 72\% of the time, when they were understood 61\% of the time; listeners who overheard, knowing the intended meaning, did not show this overestimate.

The post adds a historical case, a letter from Chamberlain that it says Hitler read as conciliatory before invading Poland. It concludes that you should not be quick to blame people who misunderstand you, because your words are probably more ambiguous than you think.

\responsehead

The post reports a real and useful finding: speakers overestimate how often they are understood. Its evidence is a set of laboratory studies, described briefly, and one historical anecdote. The studies are reported with small errors and one missed implication. The anecdote is wrong.

The missed implication concerns the mechanism. The post defines the illusion as a consequence of knowing what we mean: we know our meaning, ``and so we expect others to know it too.'' The Keysar and Henly study included overhearers who were told the intended meaning. They knew what the speaker meant, as the speakers did, and they did not systematically overestimate. So knowing the meaning is not enough to produce the bias. The study's authors locate it more narrowly, in speakers monitoring ``their own utterances.'' That is a more interesting result than the one the post states, and it points to a practical remedy: an outside listener, even one who knows the intended meaning, judges better than the speaker. The post reports the overhearer figure in one sentence and moves on.

The same study also limits the post's closing advice. Its sentences were chosen to be ambiguous. It shows that speakers are too confident about ambiguous sentences. The post ends by telling readers that their ``perfectly clear sentences'' are probably more ambiguous than they think, which is a claim about ordinary speech that the study did not test.

The Chamberlain story, taken from Keysar and Barr, is wrong on the date, the wording and the reading. The letter was sent ten days before the invasion, not two. It is polite, but it is not vague: it says Britain is ``resolved, and prepared, to employ without delay all the forces at their command.'' Hitler's reply restates the commitment and says ``I take note of this statement of yours \ldots'' On the documents, he understood the letter and attacked anyway; what he privately expected Britain to do is a separate question. The one real-world example in a post about being misunderstood is a case of being understood and disregarded.

In short: a sound finding, reported with a mechanism its own study narrows, advice that goes beyond its evidence, and a historical example that shows the opposite of what it is used for.
\end{afterword}
