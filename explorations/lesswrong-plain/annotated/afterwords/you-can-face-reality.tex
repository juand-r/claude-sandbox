\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The post consists of a single quotation, attributed to the psychologist and philosopher Eugene Gendlin, under the title ``You Can Face Reality.'' It has no commentary of its own.

The quotation makes five claims in seven short lines. What is true is already the case. Admitting it does not make it worse, and not being open about it does not make it go away. Because it is true, it is what a person actually has to deal with, while what is untrue is not there to be lived. People can bear the truth, because they are already bearing it.

The same passage closes the author's later post ``Avoiding Your Belief's Real Weak Points'' (October 2007). There the author tells readers who are questioning a cherished belief to ``rehearse only this,'' and cites Gendlin's book \textsc{Focusing}. On LessWrong the passage is known as the ``Litany of Gendlin.''

\responsehead

This is not an essay. It is a text for repetition. The author supplies a title, which turns the quotation into a promise, and line breaks, which turn a paragraph of prose into something that looks like a creed. In ``Avoiding Your Belief's Real Weak Points'' the reader is told to ``rehearse'' it. On the site it is called a litany, a word for a form of prayer. The form fits the use.

Its central claim fails exactly where a reader would need it. ``People can stand what is true, for they are already enduring it'' holds for facts whose effects one already lives with. It fails for news not yet known: a parent whose child has died is not enduring the grief until told. In the hard cases the knowing is itself the new cost.

The quotation has also been taken out of its context. In Gendlin's book it follows the sentence ``It is not harmful to the other person if you look stupid or imperfect,'' in a part of the book about people helping each other. There the truth in question is the reader's own feeling, shown to a listener (my reading of a context I could see only in search snippets). The post gives no book or page, so the reader cannot recover that setting. It keeps the authority of the named source and drops the limits the source put on it.

The author has done this before. ``Twelve Virtues of Rationality'' bases its second virtue on another line taken out of context, P.~C. Hodgell's ``That which can be destroyed by the truth should be.'' Hodgell has explained that in the novel \textsc{Seeker's Mask} (1994) the line is spoken by a character in ``the academic equivalent of a berserker fit---a ruthless drive to lay bare the truth, regardless of the cost,'' who is about to force another character to face facts ``at the worst possible time.'' In both cases the source placed the line in a setting that limits it, and the author presents it as a principle without one.

In short: a psychologist's remark about honesty with a friend, taken out of its context, laid out like a creed, and recommended for recitation.
\end{afterword}
