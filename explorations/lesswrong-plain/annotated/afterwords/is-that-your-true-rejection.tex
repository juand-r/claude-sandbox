\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The post begins with a complaint it says is common: people who reject the author's ideas about superintelligence and Friendly AI say that, without a PhD, the author should not be believed. The argument is that this is rarely their real reason. The real rejection happens first and fast, by pattern-matching the ideas to science fiction or a doomsday cult. The PhD is a justification found afterwards. If the author had a PhD, the same people would ask for tenure, or a Nobel prize. The example is Eric Drexler, who earned a doctorate for his nanotechnology work without, as far as the author knows, changing his critics' minds.

The argument then extends to business and to disagreement between rationalists. Lasting disagreements usually have sources that are hard to state, such as intuitions, professional habits or self-deception. Each side should ask itself ``Is this my true rejection?'' The post adds that the question may also be put to the other person, if it is put humbly.

\responsehead

The post draws a general rule from a personal grievance, and it applies the rule only to other people. Most of the post is spent showing that the author's critics do not mean what they say. The advice to question oneself comes in the third-to-last paragraph and is reversed in the last.

What the post describes also has an older name. To give acceptable reasons that hide the real cause of a judgment is what psychologists since Ernest Jones in 1908 have called rationalization. The author described the same process fourteen months earlier, in ``Rationalization,'' and objected there only to the word. The post does not link the two.

Used as a reply, the question has the fault the author warned against in ``The Bottom Line.'' There the warning was said to be ``not a Fully General Counterargument against conclusions you don't like.'' ``That is not your true rejection'' is exactly such a counterargument. It can be said to anyone, about any objection, and the listener cannot refute it, because on the post's own definition the true rejection is not open to introspection. If the author meets one objection and the critic raises another, the new one shows only that the old one was not the true rejection. Drexler's case shows this happening: his best-known critic gave technical reasons after the doctorate, and the post does not mention them.

The post never considers that its critics might be right about credentials. Where a listener cannot evaluate the argument, trusting the speaker's record is reasonable, and the author had said as much a year earlier. Superintelligence in 2008 was such a domain. The PhD objection was crude, but it raised a real question: who, qualified to judge, had checked these claims? The post answers the crude version and not the question.

The date of the post fits this reading. The post appeared during the author's public debate with Robin Hanson, and two days later the author put the same question to Hanson, ``in all humility.'' Reading the post as a move in that debate is an inference from the dates. But both the post itself and the author's use of the question two days later direct it at someone else.

In short: the author gives rationalization a new name and uses it to show that the stated reasons of the author's critics do not count, while a Nobel laureate's technical objections to the post's own example go unmentioned.
\end{afterword}
