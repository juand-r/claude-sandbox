\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The essay argues that a belief is worth holding only if it tells you what to expect. Two people arguing about whether an unheard falling tree makes a sound expect exactly the same things, so they are not really disagreeing about the world.

The author grants that good beliefs can be about things we never see, such as atoms or the height of a building. What matters is that they lead to something observable. From gravity and the building's height you can predict when you will hear a dropped ball land. Some beliefs connect only to each other, like a professor's claim that a novelist shows ``alienated resublimation.'' They predict nothing, and the author calls them floating beliefs.

His test is to ask of any belief what it leads you to expect, and better still, what it rules out. If the answer is nothing, give the belief up.

\responsehead

The essay offers one rule as the foundation of good thinking: a belief must predict something you could observe. As a habit, asking ``what would we expect to see differently?'' is useful. But the essay claims far more than a habit, and it does not earn the claim.

First, it presents an old idea as if it were new. Charles Peirce stated it in 1878. In the twentieth century the logical positivists made it a test of which sentences have meaning, and the test failed. Most beliefs predict nothing on their own and predict only in combination with others. A belief that looks like it floats may be doing real work inside a larger theory. The essay concedes this for atoms and floors, then forgets it when it lays down a rule for ``every question of belief.'' The reader gets a tool sharper than it should be, with no warning of where it cuts wrongly.

Second, the essay never applies its test to a belief anyone holds. Its one empty belief is a parody of literary criticism, and its one example of a belief that forbids nothing is élan vital, a theory no one defends. Real critical vocabulary, such as ``unreliable narrator,'' does tell a reader what to expect in a book, so the parody shows only that empty terms can exist. A test demonstrated only on invented and abandoned beliefs costs the reader nothing.

Third, the opening example does not show what the essay needs it to show. The two people arguing about the tree disagree about what the word ``sound'' means. That is a different failure from a belief with no content, and it has a different cure: agree on the definition. The essay mentions labels once, in its last paragraph, and never separates the two failures.

Fourth, the author does not keep his own rule. In 2008 he declared that the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics ``wins outright given our current state of evidence,'' and called it ``an obvious fact, if you have all your marbles lined up correctly.'' His case rests on Occam's razor. He grants that a rival theory differing only in ``some as-yet-untestable detail'' cannot be proved wrong. Whatever its merits, that is a belief held for its simplicity, not for anything it leads you to expect differently. By the standard of this essay, it floats. The rule is for other people's beliefs.

In short: an old idea sold as a foundation, demonstrated on invented and dead targets, and not applied by its author to his own beliefs.
\end{afterword}
