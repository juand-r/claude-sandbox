\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The essay argues that a belief is worth holding only if it tells you what to expect. Two people arguing about whether an unheard falling tree makes a sound expect exactly the same things, so they are not really disagreeing about the world.

The author grants that good beliefs can be about things we never see, such as atoms or the height of a building. What matters is that they lead to something observable. From gravity and the building's height you can predict when you will hear a dropped ball land. Some beliefs connect only to each other, like a professor's claim that a novelist shows ``alienated resublimation.'' They predict nothing, and the author calls them floating beliefs.

His test is to ask of any belief what it leads you to expect, and better still, what it rules out. If the answer is nothing, give the belief up.

\responsehead

The essay offers one rule as the foundation of good thinking: a belief must predict something you could observe. As a habit, the rule is useful. Asking ``what would we expect to see differently?'' ends many pointless arguments. And the bowling ball shows well how beliefs about unseen things can still make predictions. But the essay claims much more than a useful habit, and it does not earn the claim.

First, it presents an old idea as if it were new. Charles Peirce stated it in 1878. In the twentieth century the logical positivists made it a test of which sentences have meaning, and the test failed. Most beliefs predict nothing on their own and predict only in combination with others. A belief that looks like it floats may be doing real work inside a larger theory. The essay concedes this for atoms and floors, then forgets it when it lays down a rule for ``every question of belief.'' The reader gets a tool sharper than it should be, with no warning of where it cuts wrongly.

Second, the essay's examples tell the reader whose beliefs to suspect. The one empty belief it shows is a parody of a literature professor. The good beliefs come from physics. The implied lesson is that people who talk like humanities professors hold floating beliefs, and people who talk like physicists do not. Meanwhile the essay builds a private vocabulary of its own: anticipation, floating beliefs, paying rent, the ``martial art of rationality.'' None of it is put to its own test. What observation would the claim that floating beliefs are ``a uniquely human flaw among animal species'' lead us to expect?

Third, the opening example does not show what the essay needs it to show. The two people arguing about the tree disagree about what the word ``sound'' means. That is a different failure from a belief with no content, and it has a different cure: agree on the definition. An essay that opens with the wrong example was built around a slogan, not an analysis.

In short: a memorable statement of a sound habit, oversold as a foundation, and pointed at a convenient target.
\end{afterword}
