\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The author often meets people who think a superintelligence needs only one simple utility function, such as ``complexity,'' for everything to turn out well. They propose it too fast, and the author's own early term ``supergoal'' may have encouraged them. But a utility function can have many terms, and human values are many: the love of parent for child and of child for parent are not derived from each other or from anything else. Leave out even one value, such as the value of running one's own life, and the result could be a fate worse than death, as in Jack Williamson's ``With Folded Hands.''

Pressed, the proposers say that a superintelligence maximizing complexity would encourage mothers to love their children. That is motivated stopping, since a real complexity maximizer would look for something more complex. It is also a fake morality: defending a principle by its good consequences shows that the consequences are what is valued. Only one's morality reliably reproduces one's moral decisions, so a human morality cannot be compressed into a simple utility function.

\responsehead

The post's reply to the proposers is sound. They claim that a superintelligence that maximized complexity would encourage mothers to love their children, because that love is complex. The post answers that a real maximizer of complexity would look for whatever is most complex, and would have no reason to settle on a mother's love. The author had made the same kind of argument in ``Fake Justification'': someone who defends a \$1 million laptop by the jobs it creates has not looked for a better way to create jobs. The post also states correctly what a real argument for one master value would have to show, namely that every other good is good only when it serves that value. The \textsc{Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy} states the same requirement for utilitarian monists, who hold that one value underlies all others, and Mill tried to meet it. The post argues against a weaker opponent, one who argues from pleasant consequences.

The post names none of these opponents. The author says, ``I run across more of these people than you do,'' and states what they know and do not know (``They don't know the problem is difficult''). The one example of their reasoning is a reply quoted without a source. The previous post, ``Fake Fake Utility Functions,'' which the book leaves out, names two of their proposals: happiness and genetic fitness.

The post calls its premise a ``known fact.'' The premise is that human values are many and that none of them is derived from another. The post supports it only by referring to earlier posts, mainly ``Thou Art Godshatter,'' and our notes on that post found its central step to be a guess about human evolution presented as history. The post cites no study of how people's values relate to each other. Philosophers have long defended a related view about what is valuable, called value pluralism; W. D. Ross held it in 1930.

The example of what could go wrong comes from fiction, and the post says so. The fate worse than death is the plot of Jack Williamson's story ``With Folded Hands,'' and the post links the word ``book'' to the author's own warning against generalizing from fictional evidence. The phrase ``hyperexistential catastrophe'' links to a paper by Nick Bostrom that does not use the phrase.

In short: the post shows correctly why arguments from pleasant consequences do not establish a single master value, but it names none of its opponents, and it calls its premise, that human values are many, a known fact while supporting it only with earlier posts.
\end{afterword}
