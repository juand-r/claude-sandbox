\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The author often meets people who think a superintelligence needs only one simple utility function, such as ``complexity,'' for everything to turn out well. They propose it too fast, and the author's own early term ``supergoal'' may have encouraged them. But a utility function can have many terms, and human values are many: the love of parent for child and of child for parent are not derived from each other or from anything else. Leave out even one value, such as the value of running one's own life, and the result could be a fate worse than death, as in Jack Williamson's ``With Folded Hands.''

Pressed, the proposers say that a superintelligence maximizing complexity would encourage mothers to love their children. That is motivated stopping, since a real complexity maximizer would look for something more complex. It is also a fake morality: defending a principle by its good consequences shows that the consequences are what is valued. Only one's morality reliably reproduces one's moral decisions, so a human morality cannot be compressed into a simple utility function.

\responsehead

The post's best move is borrowed from the book's own earlier post, and it works. A process that maximized complexity would go looking for whatever is most complex; it would not stop, conveniently, at a mother's love. This is the million-dollar laptop of ``Fake Justification,'' pointed at a principle instead of a purchase. The post also says correctly what a real case for one master value would need: that every other good is good only when it serves that value. The \textsc{Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy} states the same requirement for utilitarian monists, and Mill tried to meet it. The post's target is not Mill. It is a proposer who argues from pleasant consequences.

That proposer never appears in person. The opponents are people the author meets ``more often'' than the reader does, who ``don't know the problem is difficult,'' and the one specimen of their reasoning is a reply quoted without a source. The post just before this one, which the book leaves out, at least names two of their proposals: happiness and genetic fitness.

The premise is promoted on its way in. That human values are many and not derived from one another is announced as a descriptive ``known fact.'' The evidence is a reference back to ``Thou Art Godshatter,'' whose central step our notes there call an inference presented as history. No study of how people's values relate to each other is cited. The philosophers' normative cousin of the thesis, value pluralism, is old; W. D. Ross held it in 1930.

The stakes come from fiction, and the post labels them as such. The fate worse than death is Jack Williamson's ``With Folded Hands,'' and the link on ``book'' goes to the author's own warning against generalizing from fictional evidence. The ``hyperexistential catastrophe'' links to a paper by Nick Bostrom in which the word does not occur.

In short: a sound demolition of arguments from pleasant consequences, aimed at opponents the reader never hears, and resting on a premise that arrives labelled ``known fact'' with only earlier posts behind it.
\end{afterword}
