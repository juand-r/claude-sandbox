\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The essay argues that human feelings of care do not scale with numbers. The author feels much the same about a million and a billion, and his ``care-o-meter'' cannot register the suffering of billions of people or the stakes of the far future. So feelings cannot guide action on large problems.

Three invented donors, Alice, Bob and Christine, give small sums because of social pressure, guilt or competition. A fourth, Daniel, imagines saving one oiled bird. He decides it is worth three minutes or \$3 to him, and multiplies by the number of oiled birds. He concludes that he ``actually'' cares more than he can afford, and then that every problem is his, with too many problems to address.

The author says this is not preaching. Prominent altruists, he says, are people who have learned not to trust their feelings. He recommends doing the multiplication, trusting numbers over feelings, and acting with desperation but without guilt. He plugs Giving What We Can, GiveWell, MIRI and the Future of Humanity Institute.

\responsehead

The essay is a conversion story with a recruiting pitch at the end. It sorts people into those who give from social habit and those who have ``undergone the mental shift.'' It offers the reader the second identity, and its last paragraphs name the organizations to join. One of them employed the author, which the essay does not say.

Its central word does not hold still. ``Care'' is a feeling the author cannot have, then an action taken ``anyway,'' then a quantity he possesses but cannot feel. The equivocation lets the essay describe Daniel as someone who ``actually cares'' about oiled birds to the tune of \$50,000. By the story's own account, Daniel neither feels this nor gives anything. He ends the story having given less than the three donors the essay condescends to. What changes is that he no longer finds the question rude. The only campaign in the essay that moved real money, the Ice Bucket Challenge, did it through the social pressure the essay treats as the lesser motive.

The method fails on its own terms. The multiplication starts from a number Daniel gets by introspecting on a scene written to be moving. The essay distrusts the gut for a thousand birds and trusts it for one, and gives no reason for the difference. The unit is also invented: washing an oiled bird takes about 45 minutes, not three. The arithmetic does not agree with itself: at Daniel's own rate, \$50,000 buys about five months of work, not two. For poverty and the far future, where the essay says multiplication shows that more is needed than all existing resources, no multiplication is shown.

The essay credits a blog series and none of the work it repeats. Singer made the argument about distance in 1972. Slovic had documented the fading of compassion with numbers by 2007 and drew an institutional conclusion, not a call for private desperation. The essay names Mother Teresa as an altruist who did not simply care more. Slovic's paper opens by quoting her describing a different habit, acting on the feeling for one person: ``If I look at the one, I will.''

In short: a testimony that redefines caring so that its hero can care without feeling or doing anything, built on invented people and an invented unit, and closed with a plug for the author's employer.
\end{afterword}
