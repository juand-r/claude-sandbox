\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The essay argues that people become good reasoners only when something they care about more than reasoning is at stake. It starts from a contrast in fiction: in Western comics heroes get powers first and then look for a purpose, while in Japanese fiction the power comes from having someone to protect.

It then claims that science defeated authority because it produced useful technology, not because it sounded reasonable. It answers the objection that truth should come before usefulness by saying the relationship is complicated. Its main example is a choice between saving 400 people for certain and saving 500 with probability 90\%. A parent whose daughter is among the 500 is more likely, the author suggests, to do the arithmetic and choose the gamble.

The author says his own growth as a reasoner began when he had something important to do. He concludes that one should find a truly important cause by learning to calculate, and put reasoning in its service.

\responsehead

The essay tells its readers who the real rationalists are. They are people with a cause worth more than their lives, their comfort and their pride. Ordinary altruists appear twice, as people choosing a cause the way they choose drapes and as people who ``need a hobby''. The reader is offered a place among the first group and shown the way in: ``Learn how to multiply, and perhaps you will recognize a drastically important cause when you see one.'' Inference: the cause that multiplication is meant to reveal is the one its author was working for. The essay does not name it.

The evidence does not bear the weight. The ``oft-repeated motif'' is one unfinished manga. The Western contrast rests on a description of Spider-Man's motive that is false: he fights crime out of guilt over his uncle's death, not to keep busy. The history of science is asserted in four sentences with no source. The one argument, the parent and the 500, is built to agree with itself. From the daughter's side neither option is certain, so the example removes the very bias it claims love will overcome. Her 80 against 90 is the same ratio as 400 against 450, so love and counting cannot disagree. Change the numbers so that they do, and love points the other way.

The essay also breaks its author's own rules. He had named the fallacy of generalizing from fiction and linked to it in the first sentence. He had written that every cause ``wants to be a cult''; the essay links that post and recommends a cause placed above one's life without mentioning the risk. It teaches the reader to answer the failure of what they took to be rational with ``I must have had the wrong conception of rationality'', which makes rationality impossible to fault. The only case of a teacher proved wrong is someone else's teacher.

Last, the essay's decisive evidence is the author's own story, and he later told that story differently. In ``The Magnitude of His Own Folly'' the cause ``far more important than my own life'' was present from childhood, alongside the belief that ``the Future was indestructible''. He dates the start of his training to something else: the moment he saw that failure was possible. By its only witness's later testimony, a cause worth more than his life was not enough.

In short: the claim that mastery needs a cause worth more than your life rests on one manga, a misdescribed Spider-Man, a dilemma built to agree with it and its author's unnamed mission, and it never mentions the cult risk its own link warns of.
\end{afterword}
