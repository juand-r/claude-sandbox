\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The essay asks why people who call themselves rationalists are not visibly more capable than anyone else. It starts from a common objection, that rational people do not seem happier. The author replies that the people the objectors have in mind have little rationality. Even the best researchers in the psychology of reasoning fall short of what should be possible.

His explanation is that rationality has never been systematized, trained and tested. A mathematician, an engineer or an athlete draws on organized knowledge and long practice. Rationalists improvise, and the author includes himself: he is strong in one skill he built alone and weak in others.

The model is the martial arts. Schools of fighting exist because people wanted to become formidable, and they improved because they could tell when a blow landed. Rationality lacks all three conditions: people who work together, ways to verify training, and, first, the sense that more is possible.

\responsehead

The essay turns an objection into a recruiting call. The objection was that rational people do not seem happier. The essay does not answer it. It declares the people the objectors know not really rational, raises the bar until even senior researchers fall below it, and changes the question from happiness to formidability. By the end, the absence of results has become the reason to try harder. The one explanation the essay never considers is that there is less to train than it hopes. Its own framing cannot fail. If rationalists are not formidable, they have not trained enough or wanted it enough.

The essay's own test cuts against it. It names verification as ``the essential, blocking obstacle,'' then ranks aspiration first in its closing list. Between the two, the author rates himself: more than a first-dan black belt, able to punch through brick, the creator of a sub-art of rationality ``from scratch.'' None of this is tested. By the standard the essay sets, it is a self-assessment that no one has checked.

Its account of the research is thin where it matters. It dismisses debiasing studies as hour-long exercises checked two weeks later, and cites none. Nisbett and his colleagues had studied the effect of years of university training on reasoning, and concluded that ``reasoning is taught and generalizable.'' Their short sessions also had measured effects. The essay's analogy is looser still. It claims that martial-arts schools improved because they competed in realistic contests. Some flourish without doing so, and some say openly that they are not training for combat. A school's existence shows that people will pay to be taught. It does not show that what is taught works.

The essay's standard can be applied to what followed it. The Center for Applied Rationality, which Wikipedia says originated from LessWrong, was founded in 2012 and hosts rationality workshops. The one controlled evaluation Wikipedia reports is a small randomized trial of 50 participants, measured by surveys of participants and people close to them. That is not the five-year comparison of training programs the essay called the standard.

In short: an essay that makes verification the test of a rationality school, then answers its opening objection by moving the bar, rates its author without any test, and sells the aspiration in place of the evidence.
\end{afterword}
