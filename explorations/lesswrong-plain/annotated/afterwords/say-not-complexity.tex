\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The post begins with a story. The author asked Marcello, a young olympiad competitor the author was considering as an apprentice, how an AI might learn to solve a Rubik's Cube by itself. Marcello said the AI would need ``complexity'' to do certain things. The author told him not to say ``complexity,'' and eventually explained that the word ``doesn't concentrate your probability mass.'' Marcello saw that the word, like ``emergence,'' named a gap without filling it.

The author then generalizes. Complexity is a legitimate concept with mathematical definitions, but using it to explain something you do not understand is a mistake. The mistake is a quick ``skip-over'' that happens below the level of words, and it is common in AI discussions, in academia and in AI startups. The remedy is to notice the feeling of a blank area on your map. The author and Marcello adopted the word ``magic'' as a placeholder that admits an unsolved problem, where ``complexity'' and ``emergence'' hide one.

\responsehead

The idea in this post is old and simple: a word that names a gap does not fill it. Sidney Harris drew it as a cartoon, collected in 1977, of a blackboard proof with the line ``then a miracle occurs'' in the middle, a line the post itself quotes without naming the source. What the post adds is an anecdote, a name for the error, and a remedy.

The anecdote shows the author correcting a claim the student did not quite make. Marcello said the AI ``needs complexity to do X''; the reply attacks complexity as ``a goal in itself.'' The parenthesis then shows whom the reply was really for: unnamed people who said the Internet would ``wake up.''

The diagnosis rests on unnamed crowds. The error is said to be ``extremely common,'' committed ``left and right, over and over again,'' in AGI discussions, in academia under ``huge pressure,'' in startups whose life plans would collapse. Not one instance is quoted. A post that condemns skipping over what one does not understand skips over the evidence for its own empirical claim. The general mechanism rests on a term, ``non-controlling causal node,'' that is never defined.

The remedy is circular as described. The mistake is a thought that ``feels like an explanation but isn't.'' The cure is to ``feel which parts of your map are still blank.'' The faculty that produced the error is the one asked to catch it. The post does offer two outside checks, asking whether a word ``concentrate[s] your probability mass'' and writing ``magic'' where a gap is, but its last word on the remedy is that avoiding the error ``will become a matter of instinct.'' The honest cost of the method, that the parts you can explain may turn out not to matter, is conceded only in a footnote, and the footnote turns the concession into praise for those who ``dare,'' with no evidence that most people do not.

In short: an old point, illustrated with an anecdote, supported only by unnamed crowds, and given a remedy that leans on the faculty that failed.
\end{afterword}
