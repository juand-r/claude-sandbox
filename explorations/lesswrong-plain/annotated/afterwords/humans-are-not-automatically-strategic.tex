\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The post asks why people pursue their goals so badly, like an aspiring comedian who studies by watching old cartoons. The author's answer is that most possible actions are ineffective, just as most answers on a calculus test are wrong. Nothing in evolution or culture pushes us strongly toward the few that work.

People do have goals in a limited sense. They tell stories about them and feel good or bad about the results. But they do not automatically ask what they are trying to achieve, measure progress, gather information, try different methods, and put their effort where it works. The author suggests that the deeper reason is that the part of us that reasons in words is much better than the part that drives what we do. Knowing that exercise is good does not make us want to exercise. The post ends by asking readers how they have trained themselves to be more strategic.

\responsehead

The opening analogy has one real idea: failure needs no explanation, because most actions don't work. The rest depends on an assumption the post never examines. The post's one worked example is a would-be comedian; its other cases are generic and appear in a footnote.

The assumption is that the goals people state are their real goals. The would-be comedian watches Garfield reruns, and the post concludes that he is failing to pursue his goal. A simpler reading is that he enjoys cartoons more than he wants the hard work of becoming a comedian, and that ``becoming a comedian'' is partly a story he tells. The post comes close to this. It grants that we ``tell ourselves and others stories'' about our goals, and item (g) asks whether a goal ``is really our goal.'' But it never draws the conclusion. It treats the gap between what people say they want and what they do as a lack of skill, to be fixed by training. More often, that gap may simply show what people actually want.

The post also gives the cost of optimizing only a passing clause, in item (g). Searching, planning and testing take time and effort. Herbert Simon's name for stopping at ``good enough'' is satisficing, and it is often the rational choice. Several of the footnote's examples may be cases of it.

The explanation it does offer is weak. ``Humans are only just on the cusp of general intelligence'' is a large claim with nothing behind it, and the argument does not use it. But the post does take a position: it defines ordinary life as a failure of strategy, and training as the remedy. Two years later the author co-founded the Center for Applied Rationality, which teaches that training in paid workshops.

In short: one true point about failure, a weak example, and an unexamined assumption that what people say they want is what they want.
\end{afterword}
