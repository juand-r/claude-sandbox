\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The post argues that very strong evidence is ordinary. When the author tells someone the author's own name, the listener's confidence that the name is genuine jumps from about one in a million to near certainty. That means the statement multiplied the odds by roughly twenty million. Wikipedia entries, a friend's claim to juggle, and a computer's output are similar.

From this the author draws two claims. First, you may need less evidence than you think to believe that you are an unusually good trader, or unusually well calibrated. Second, working through Bayes's rule by hand may push people toward estimates that are too timid. The last line turns a familiar maxim around: extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, but such evidence may be common.

\responsehead

The opening observation is true and worth knowing: ordinary statements can carry enormous weight as evidence. A stranger's word about their name moves the odds by a factor of millions. The post does not say why, and the reason matters for everything after it. The claim is narrow, so its prior is low only because there are many possible names, and people seldom lie about their names. Strong evidence is common for specific claims about ordinary facts that people have no reason to misreport.

The two applications are of a different kind. Both concern the reader's own excellence: whether you are in the top 1\% of traders, and whether you are much less overconfident than average. These are the beliefs where evidence is weakest and wishful thinking strongest. A good year of trading is weak evidence of skill, because returns are noisy and the traders who failed have mostly stopped, so the survivors look better than they are. An hour of calibration practice says little about judgment in real decisions. The post suggests that the needed evidence ``might be easy to get'' and gives no example of it. The move from the strongest case, your own name, to the weakest, your own merit, is made without comment.

The trading example also starts from a misstatement. The efficient market hypothesis, that prices already reflect the information available, implies that it is hard to beat the market, not that it is hard to make money; in a rising market most buy-and-hold investors make money.

The closing line turns around the maxim Carl Sagan popularized, and the reversal depends on a change in the meaning of ``extraordinary.'' Sagan's extraordinary claims were claims that contradict much of what we know, such as visits by aliens. A name is not extraordinary in that sense. For extraordinary claims in Sagan's sense, testimony is the kind of evidence that cannot be trusted, and the name example says nothing about them. The last paragraph also asserts, with no argument, that working through Bayes's rule biases people toward caution.

The result is a post that tells readers who care about reasoning well that they may be right to think themselves unusually good at it. That conclusion is welcome, and the post supports it with the cases that favor it.

In short: one true observation about ordinary testimony, extended without argument to the reader's own excellence, where it is weakest.
\end{afterword}
