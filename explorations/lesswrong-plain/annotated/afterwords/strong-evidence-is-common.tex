\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The post argues that very strong evidence is ordinary. When the author tells someone his name, their confidence that it is really his name jumps from about one in a million to near certainty. That means his statement multiplied the odds by roughly twenty million. Wikipedia entries, a friend's claim to juggle, and a computer's output are similar.

From this the author draws two claims. First, you may need less evidence than you think to believe that you are an unusually good trader, or unusually well calibrated. Second, working through Bayes's rule by hand may push people toward estimates that are too timid. The last line turns a familiar maxim around: extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, but such evidence may be common.

\responsehead

The opening observation is true and worth knowing: ordinary statements can carry enormous weight as evidence. After that the post goes wrong, and the direction in which it goes wrong is telling.

Look at what the insight is used for. Both applications are about believing that you are exceptional: in the top 1\% of traders, or much less overconfident than average. These are exactly the beliefs where evidence is weakest and wishful thinking strongest. A good year of trading is weak evidence of skill, because returns are noisy and luck is common. An hour of calibration practice says little about your judgment in real decisions. The post moves from its strongest case, your own name, to its weakest, your own excellence, without noticing the difference between them. A name is strong evidence because the claim is specific and people rarely lie about it. Self-assessment has neither property.

The one calculation that matters is wrong: 99 to 1, not 200 to 1. The last paragraph then asserts, with no argument, that working through Bayes's rule makes people too cautious. So a post about evidence ends by encouraging confidence with no evidence offered at all.

Consider who reads it. The post tells readers of a site devoted to reasoning well that they may be right to think themselves unusually good at it. It follows the pattern ``The Bottom Line'' warns against: a welcome conclusion, supported by the evidence that happens to favor it.

In short: one good observation, one wrong calculation, and a conclusion that flatters its readers.
\end{afterword}
