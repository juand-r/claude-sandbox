\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The essay argues that admitting your flaws is worth nothing unless you also plan to reduce them. What matters is progress.

It begins with Orthodox Judaism, as the author describes it. A proverb says each generation is to the one before as donkeys are to men, because law received at Sinai can only be lost in transmission. Modern rabbis therefore cannot overrule ancient ones, even on biology. As a child, the author concluded that science, which gains knowledge, must eventually surpass the Torah.

The Japanese phrase ``tsuyoku naritai,'' ``I want to become stronger,'' names the attitude the author prefers. The Yom Kippur litany Ashamnu, in which everyone confesses every sin, stands for the attitude he rejects: confession without improvement. He compares it to rationalists who announce that everyone is biased. A joke about a rabbi mocks pride in humility. The essay ends by demanding that anyone who confesses flaws also say what they will do about them.

\responsehead

The essay's one idea is that confessing a fault is empty without the resolve to fix it. It is a good idea. The essay presents it as a break with the religious tradition it describes, and it is that tradition's own rule.

Maimonides defines repentance as abandoning the sin and resolving in one's heart never to repeat it. He compares confession without that resolve to immersing in a ritual bath while holding a dead lizard. The Yom Kippur liturgy includes, in most prayer books, the confession of Rav Hamnuna, which asks ``that I may sin no more.'' The essay describes the Ashamnu, notes that it does not end with a promise to do better, and says that the ``community spirit of Yom Kippur'' is about confessing sins, not avoiding them. The rite as a whole, and the law behind it, say the opposite. The essay lets one litany stand for the tradition, and so gives the tradition's own teaching back to it as a rebuke.

The opening proverb is handled more loosely than it appears. In the Talmud the angels-and-donkeys line is conditional: if the earlier generations were angels, we are men; if they were men, we are donkeys. The idea of a decline across generations is real in the tradition, so the essay's reading is not invented. But it drops the conditional and states the line as a law, which suits the contrast it wants between a religion that only loses knowledge and a science that only gains it.

The closing rule has a shape the essay has just mocked. Three paragraphs earlier it tells the joke of the rabbi who, seeing the janitor prostrate himself, says ``Look who thinks he's nothing.'' The joke is about status kept inside a display of humility. The essay then ends: ``Never confess to me that you are just as flawed as I am unless you can tell me what you plan to do about it.'' The author, like the rabbi, decides who may claim to be as low as he is. My reading, from the parallel, is that the essay takes the rabbi's place without noticing. The added condition, a plan, is reasonable; the posture is the one the joke laughs at.

In short: a sound rule about confession and improvement, presented as a correction of a tradition that already teaches it, closing in the posture of the rabbi it has just made the butt of a joke.
\end{afterword}
