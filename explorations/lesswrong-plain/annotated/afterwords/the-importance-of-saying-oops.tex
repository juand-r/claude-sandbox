\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The essay argues that when you are wrong in a large way, you should admit it all at once. Its example is Enron, whose executives, the author says, never admitted a large mistake: they patched each failure, hid problems on the balance sheet, and after the bankruptcy Jeff Skilling told Congress that Enron had been a great company.

The author says the ``Traditional Rationality'' absorbed while growing up, learned from Heinlein and Feynman, taught people to give in to evidence eventually but not quickly. The switch to ``Bayescraft'' came after a large personal mistake, and hindsight showed that it had been admitted in small, grudging concessions. It would have been faster to say ``oops.''

Admitting a big mistake is painful but can change your life. Getting it right the first time is better, but if you are wrong, see it all at once. Many small admissions only protect your pride and set you up to repeat the error.

\responsehead

The essay's own case is withheld. Its author made ``a large mistake,'' learned the lesson of the essay from it, and then writes ``well, it's a long story'' in place of the mistake. The rule the essay sets is about acknowledging a large error to oneself, and the author says that was done; declining to tell the reader is not a breach of it. But the reader is asked to accept the method on the strength of a case they are not allowed to see.

What the reader is shown instead is Enron, and Enron is the wrong example. The essay presents its executives as people who could not bring themselves to say ``I've been stupid.'' They were people who knew and hid: the essay's own invented dialogue has them asking how to hide a problem on the balance sheet. Skilling was convicted of conspiracy and securities fraud more than a year before the essay appeared. His testimony that he thought the company ``great'' and financially strong was a denial of knowledge, not a failure of insight. A case of deliberate deception is used to illustrate self-deception. The difference matters: the remedy for fraud is not a braver inner monologue.

The rest of the support is weak. ``Traditional Rationality'' is a position the author names, summarizes as ``fuzzy verbalisms,'' and dismisses, without quoting anyone who holds it; the first item on its list, bold and falsifiable theories, is Popper's, and Popper goes uncredited. The claim that one large loss feels better than many small ones is stated as fact, with no source.

Finally, consider what the essay teaches the reader to do. It urges the reader to have a ``watershed moment,'' but gives no method for finding one's own large mistake. Its last full paragraph turns to ``others,'' who concede by millimeters and say ``I was right in principle'' or ``It could have worked.'' The essay prints those phrases in italics, for the reader to recognize when someone else says them.

In short: an essay on admitting large mistakes that withholds its own case, illustrates self-deception with a fraud, and gives the reader a way to spot the failure in others but not in themselves.
\end{afterword}
