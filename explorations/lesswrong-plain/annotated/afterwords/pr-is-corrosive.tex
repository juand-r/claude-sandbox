\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The post argues that thinking in terms of ``PR'' makes people and organizations behave worse, while thinking in terms of ``reputation'' or ``honor'' does not. Honor, in the author's sense, means keeping fixed standards and being known to keep them, as the Lannisters are known to pay their debts. PR means predicting what might make people angry and avoiding it.

The author says this second process has no fixed standard, feeds on its own fears, and pushes people toward saying nothing. She also notes that PR seems to need outside experts, while honor is something people can judge for themselves. Her advice: when you catch yourself worrying about PR, ask instead what your reputation or good name requires.

\responsehead

This is a useful distinction, briefly made. The point about experts is the strongest part. When people think about PR they hire a consultant; when they think about honor they consult themselves. That rings true, and it could be tested.

The essay is weaker on evidence and on its examples. It offers no case of an organization that changed how it thought and did better. Both of its illustrations, the Lannisters and the dueling gentleman, show honor going badly wrong. Honor cultures are a well-studied subject. Richard Nisbett and Dov Cohen found that the homicide rate among white men was higher in the American South than in the North, but only for killings that grew out of arguments and quarrels, not for killings during robberies. They traced the difference to a culture in which a man's reputation had to be defended. Honor, too, can be about what others will think. The essay's real subject is standards you choose and keep, as against reactions you forecast and dodge. ``Integrity'' may be a better word for it than ``honor.''

Public relations became a profession in the early twentieth century. Edward Bernays, one of its founders, titled his 1928 book \textsc{Propaganda}, and opened its first chapter this way: ``The conscious and intelligent manipulation of the organized habits and opinions of the masses is an important element in democratic society.'' At least one of the profession's founders described its work in exactly the terms the post distrusts.

The timing may also matter. The post appeared on 14 February 2021, the day after the New York Times published Cade Metz's article about the blog Slate Star Codex and the rationalist community. Many in that community saw the article as hostile. The post does not mention it, so the connection is my inference. It would explain the unexplained link at the end, to an essay titled ``The New York Times.''

As writing, the argument is clear once found, but hedges, chat abbreviations and vague words (``weird,'' ``loopier,'' ``residue'') bury it. Rewritten in plain prose, it would be half as long and more convincing.
\end{afterword}
