\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The post argues that thinking in terms of ``PR'' makes people and organizations behave worse, while thinking in terms of ``reputation'' or ``honor'' does not. Honor, in the author's sense, means keeping fixed standards and being known to keep them, as the Lannisters are known to pay their debts. PR means predicting what might make people angry and avoiding it.

The author argues that this second process has no fixed standard, is prone to ``self-reinforcing fears,'' and pushes people toward saying nothing. The post also notes that PR seems to need outside experts, while honor is something people can judge for themselves. The post's advice: when you catch yourself worrying about PR, ask instead what your reputation or good name requires.

\responsehead

The post calls itself ``a small detail'' and asks for critique, and its central observation is a good one. People who worry about PR hand the question to outside experts; people who think about their honor judge for themselves. The rest of the post depends on a distinction made by definition.

``Honor'' is defined as keeping fixed standards and being known to keep them. ``PR'' is defined as having no fixed standards: predicting what will upset people and avoiding it. With those definitions, the comparison is decided before any argument is made. What the post needed to show is that the two words, as people actually use them, pull thinking in these two directions. For that it offers the consultant observation and nothing else.

Its own example of honor contradicts the verdict that safeguarding honor ``will basically go fine.'' The gentleman of 1800 fought a duel to defend his visible adherence to a fixed standard, which is honor as the post defines it. Men died of it. The post may mean that honor goes fine in modern settings, but it does not say so or say why.

No evidence is offered for the main claim. It is marked ``I predict'' and ``(I think?)''. No person or organization is described that switched from one way of thinking to the other and did better or worse. That is not fatal in a short post that asks for critique, but it leaves the claim unsupported.

The timing suggests what the post was responding to. It appeared on 14 February 2021, the day after the New York Times published Cade Metz's critical article about Slate Star Codex and the rationalist community. The post's last line links an essay titled ``The New York Times.'' It does not mention the article, so the connection is my inference. If it is right, the post offers a community under press scrutiny a way to describe its own caution: other people manage PR; we defend our good name.

In short: a real point about outside experts, within a distinction made by definition, with the author's own example of honor counting against its verdict.
\end{afterword}
