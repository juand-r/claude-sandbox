\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The author recalls a synagogue's annual appeal, where members called out their pledges, and sets beside it an online appeal the author ran for a nonprofit, sent to an audience of atheists, libertarians, technophiles and programmers. Critics posted their reasons for not giving; donors gave but said nothing on the lists, so each donor saw only objections.

Why can a flying-saucer cult gather followers when ``rationalists'' cannot? Cults are held together by pluralistic ignorance, evaporative cooling and affective death spirals. Rationalists should not copy these, but a true rationalist ought to be effective, and knowing more should not make a group do worse. The author diagnoses half-rationality: a culture that practises and rewards only disagreement, as in a conference where praise at the microphone would be horrifying, and a shame of strong feeling. The remedy is to train agreement as well as dissent, to feel emotions that fit the facts, and to voice support publicly, as the synagogue's members did.

\responsehead

The post asks a fair question, and its remedy has evidence behind it. Its diagnosis has only the author's memory and impressions behind it.

The evidence for the diagnosis is one fundraising appeal, described years later by the person who ran it, and the author's impressions of the community. From these the post names ``the two major forces'' that keep the ``atheist\slash{}libertarian\slash{}technophile'' crowd from working together. The title says the crowd ``Can't'' cooperate; the body is more cautious and says only ``Perhaps that is why.'' Much of the explanation restates earlier posts: pluralistic ignorance, evaporative cooling, ``Knowing About Biases Can Hurt People'' and ``Feeling Rational.'' The post does not compare this crowd with any other, and the outside record is mixed. In the year before the post, supporters of Ron Paul, a former Libertarian Party nominee, raised over \$4.2 million online in one day. A British atheist campaign to put advertisements on buses failed to gather enough pledges on its first try, then raised over \pounds100,000 in four days on its second. Such groups can coordinate, at least sometimes. Whether they do so less often than other groups, the post does not measure.

Two findings support the post. First, its remedy, voicing support in public alongside objections, has experimental backing: in a field experiment on charitable giving, new donors gave more when told what another donor had given. Second, many Raelians, the post's example of a group held together by nonsense, call their creed an ``atheistic religion.'' So the difference between the two groups is not belief in God, which fits the post's claim that the difference lies in group culture.

Two passages are easy to misread. The paragraph saying that the ``Light Side will always be divided and weak'' and that ``the future inevitably belongs to the Dark'' states a view the post goes on to reject; it is not the conclusion. And the objection raised at the start, that the critics of the appeal may have been right, is answered at the end only with a rule about timing: objections should be voiced at another time, or alongside public support.

In short: the post's diagnosis of the author's community is based on one fundraiser and the author's impressions, and its title claims more than its hedged body; its remedy, voicing support in public alongside objections, is supported by an experiment on charitable giving.
\end{afterword}
