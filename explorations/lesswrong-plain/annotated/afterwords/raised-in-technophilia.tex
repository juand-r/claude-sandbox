\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The author describes growing up a technophile. At nine the author read Jerry Pournelle's \textsc{A Step Farther Out}, a reply to Ehrlich, the Club of Rome and the opponents of nuclear power, and took from it a picture of history: scientists and engineers on one side, the opponents of vaccination and steam engines on the other, and in the middle the ``pretenders to Deep Wisdom'' who call for regulation and committees.

The adult author still thinks this picture was not ``too wrong'' about who the Bad Guys were, and cites an estimate that slow drug approval in the United States causes far more deaths than it prevents. The mistake was to think that avoiding everything the Bad Guys did makes one a Good Guy, so that any caution felt like joining the middle. In 1997 or 1998, in a debate about whether nanotechnology would favour attack over defense, the young author saw for the first time that extinction was possible. Yet little changed: the plan to race to AI stayed, now justified as a way to get there before nanotechnology. Even a sharp Traditional Rationalist, the author concludes, falls short of what is needed.

\responsehead

As memoir the post holds up, better than most memoirs, because the archive backs it. The Extropians mailing list has the 1997 debate on nanotechnology with the young author's argument from nuclear weapons, the 1996 slogan ``Get to the Singularity as fast as possible!'', and a 1999 statement that the hurry was about humanity wiping itself out. The central confession, an old plan kept under a new reason, is on the record.

The defence of the childhood allergy is another matter. The adult author announces ``a historical record showing over-conservativeness.'' The record turns out to be one estimate about one regulator's approval of drugs, plus a list of old fights over vaccination, anaesthesia, steam engines and heliocentrism. The step from drugs to technology in general is not argued. The estimate is apparently misreported on the way: the figures appear to be Dale Gieringer's, which are per decade, not per year. The ratio survives, so the comparison does too.

The post then supplies the case its own evidence cannot reach. The drug estimate counts lives lost on each side. The young author's blind spot, in the post's words, was that ``Lives could be lost, but not the Future.'' A record about drug approval says nothing about that kind of risk, which is the kind the rest of the post is about. The post keeps both the allergy and the lesson, and does not say how to tell which case one is in.

The middle way is dismissed by adjective. The ``pretenders to Deep Wisdom'' say, ``in defiance of brute historical fact,'' that science of itself is ``neither good nor evil.'' A record of good uses is no evidence against a claim about science of itself. From these pretenders the post draws a rule for everything, that seeking a middle way ``is usually wrong,'' with ``usually'' as the hedge and one set of opponents as the evidence.

In short: a memoir the archive bears out, attached to a defence of the author's old allergy to talk of risk that rests on one regulator's record, apparently misreported by a factor of ten, and silent on the one kind of risk the post turns out to be about.
\end{afterword}
