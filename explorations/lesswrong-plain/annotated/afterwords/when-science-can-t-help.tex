\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The author recalls a theory held at eighteen, that consciousness involved quantum gravity. The young author had followed the rules as taught: the theory made a testable prediction, and Science would have permitted ten years of testing it as long as defeat was admitted afterward. What Science does not do, the essay argues, is say which theory is right before the experiment.

Some questions have large consequences but no cheap test now. The examples are macroscopic quantum superpositions, the untested parts of evolutionary psychology, and cryonics. For cryonics, the absence of revivals is only weak evidence against it, because no one expects revival to be possible with current technology. People who dismiss cryonics as ``not scientific'' misread science, whose verdict is only ``not proven,'' while 150,000 people die each day.

Where experiments cannot yet decide, the essay concludes, one must reason rationally and reach the answer before the evidence forces it.

\responsehead

The title says science cannot help on certain questions, meaning that it gives no verdict in time to act on. On the essay's main case it grants that science's verdict is correct: ``this is not a case where science actually gives the \emph{wrong answer}.'' Science says ``not proven'' about cryonics. What the essay objects to is people who read ``not proven'' as ``false,'' a misreading it could have corrected in a paragraph. The rest of its length does something else: it moves the reader from the fact that science does not decide to the author's own position.

It does this without making the case. The absence-of-evidence argument shows only that the lack of revivals is weak evidence against cryonics. It supplies no evidence for it. The positive case is declined (``I'm not going to go into those at the moment''), and its key premise, that freezing preserves the pattern of synapses, is replaced by a pointer to the author's physics posts. In its place the essay offers pressure: 150,000 deaths a day, ``family and friends,'' an ambulance analogy that assumes the ambulance reaches a hospital, and a ``calculated bet'' with no calculation.

The examples are chosen to fit. Every case where ``rationality has to take over from science'' is a position the author holds: many-worlds, evolutionary psychology, cryonics and, in the closing parenthesis, global catastrophic risk. The essay offers no case where reasoning ahead of the evidence went wrong. Its one failure, the author's youthful theory, it blames on following the rules of Science, not on reasoning ahead of the evidence.

The founding story does not show what it is used for. The young author's prediction was Penrose and Hameroff's. And by the author's own 2007 account, the rules now blamed ``helped me get out of the hole.'' One published physical objection to the microtubule idea, Tegmark's decoherence estimate of 2000, was a calculation made without waiting for an experiment: the kind of prior reasoning the essay says Science lacks.

The essay's one substantial point, that some hypotheses are supported only as parts of a larger productive theory, is Lakatos's from 1970. The essay presents it with the announcement that ``we'd need a new kind of verdict.'' The essay does not cite the literature it claims to replace.

In short: an essay whose main example shows science's verdict arriving too late rather than wrong, and which uses that gap to justify belief in the author's causes without making the positive case for any of them.
\end{afterword}
