\begin{afterword}
\summaryhead

The post offers an evolutionary explanation for why explanations fail. It argues that humans evolved in small hunter-gatherer bands without writing, where everyone shared the same background knowledge. Any new fact was at most one ``inferential step'' from what everyone already knew. In such a world, a person who asserted something without obvious support was a liar or a fool, and a person who failed to see something obvious was stupid or obstinate.

Modern knowledge is different: scientific conclusions can be a hundred steps from common knowledge. The author argues that we still expect short distances, and that this, together with the illusion of transparency, explains why scientists struggle to talk to lay audiences and to each other. The illustration is a biologist who defends evolution as ``the simplest explanation'' to a listener who does not share the scientist's respect for simplicity.

The advice is to build an argument step by step from what the audience already accepts, and not to let them see that you think you are far ahead of them.

\responsehead

The advice in this post is sound and easy to state: an explanation has to start from what the audience already knows, and an explainer usually underestimates how far back that is. The rest of the post is an account of where the underestimate comes from. That account is the post's own contribution, and it is the part the evidence does not support.

Its premise is that in the ancestral world ``all background knowledge is universal knowledge,'' and the only argument is the word ``period.'' The premise leaves out children. Every band has always contained members who began knowing nothing the adults knew: how to track, which plants are poisonous, who is related to whom, what the words mean. Each of them had to be taught, and the teaching took years. If any human activity is a long explanation across many steps, it is this one, and it is as old as the species. A mind shaped in such a world had every reason to expect some listeners to be far behind. The post's picture of ancestral life leaves out the one activity most relevant to its subject.

The account also explains nothing that the post's other causes do not. The paragraph that draws the conclusion names the illusion of transparency and self-anchoring, the habit of modelling other minds as slightly modified copies of one's own. Either would produce the failures the author reports. The ancestral story is added as a third cause, and the post gives no observation that would come out differently if the story were false. The evidence for the effect itself is the author's impression, introduced with ``I think'' and ``When I observe failures of explanation.'' The effect is real and had been studied: economists named it ``the curse of knowledge'' in 1989. The post does not mention that work, which is where a reader could have found evidence for how large the effect is and when it occurs.

None of this affects the advice. Starting from what the audience knows is good practice whatever its origin. But the post presents the advice as following from a theory of human evolution, and the theory depends on a premise that the teaching of children, an explanation across many steps, counts against.

In short: sound advice about explaining, presented as following from an evolutionary story that leaves out the teaching of children, the oldest case of explaining across many steps, and that explains nothing the biases the post names do not already explain.
\end{afterword}
