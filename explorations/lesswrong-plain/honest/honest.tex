\section*{A note before starting}

Each section retells one post in its author's voice. It follows the post from start to finish and says plainly what each part does: what it claims, what it shows, and what it only appears to show. Where a paragraph contributes nothing to the argument, the retelling says so.

These are parodies written by the editor of \textsc{LessWrong, Annotated}, not the authors' words. Words inside quotation marks are the authors' own and have been checked against the originals. Every other claim about a post, including its errors, rests on the notes and Responses in the annotated edition, where the evidence is given.

The satire is meant to be fair. Where a post gets something right, the retelling says that too.

\honest{The Lens That Sees Its Flaws}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

Light leaves the Sun, bounces off your shoelaces, enters your eye, and becomes the belief that your shoelaces are untied. I describe every step of this, because it sounds like physics and makes what follows sound like physics too.

My point is that you can understand this process and a mouse cannot. I ask you to marvel at it. Then I tell you about the mouse again, and ask you to marvel again, in case the first marvel did not take.

Science, I say, is this same understanding made systematic. The claim I actually need, that a mind can be understood and repaired the way a machine can, gets one sentence. I defend it by saying that the mind may be ``a little more complicated'' than a steam engine. That is a joke, and it is the whole defense.

Now my one example. Years ago, in a chatroom, I asked strangers whether they expected a nuclear war in the next twenty years, and asked the ones who said no to explain themselves. The ones who said yes were not asked to explain anything. One man said he expected no war for a hundred years, because ``All of the players involved in decisions regarding nuclear war are not interested right now.'' That is a reason about the world. I asked why he stretched it to a hundred years, and he said ``Pure hope.'' From those two words I work out his unhappiness and what his brain is doing.

To show that hoping for peace does not make peace likelier, I bring in parallel universes and a paper by Max Tegmark. Ordinary probability would have done the job. The principle is that wanting something to be true is not evidence that it is true. People call this wishful thinking, and have for a long time.

I conclude that a mind which knows its own flaws can apply a reflective correction, and that this makes it ``far more powerful.'' I do not say what the correction is, how large it should be, or how you would know it had worked. Five months ago I wrote that knowing about biases can make people reason worse. I leave that out.

In a footnote I say that a small effect should move your beliefs only a little, and that we must now bring in Bayes's theorem and measure it. Then I measure nothing. That footnote was the most useful part of the essay.

\honest{Politics Is the Mind-Killer}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

People think badly about politics. The reason, I say, is ``so obvious as to be worth belaboring'': in the ancestral environment, being on the wrong side of an argument could get you killed. Having said it is worth belaboring, I do not belabor it. No evidence follows, then or later.

My advice is this. When you want to teach a point about rationality, do not take your example from current politics; use Louis XVI. Politics is a terrible place to learn rationality, ``unless all the discussants are already rational.'' Any group that wants to argue about politics can decide it qualifies, so anyone can exempt themselves from the rule.

Next I explain that arguments are soldiers, and that sensible scientists turn into slogan-chanting zombies when a political issue comes up. I describe this as something other people do.

Then comes my example, a standard problem from artificial intelligence: all Quakers are pacifists, all Republicans are not, and Nixon is both. I state it with ``all'', which turns it into a plain contradiction. The problem was invented to show how to reason with rules that have exceptions, and the real version says ``usually''.

I spend two of my eight paragraphs asking why the problem's author, whom I do not name, chose Nixon. Was it to make Republicans feel unwelcome in courses on artificial intelligence? I decide it was to get in a dig at the other side. That is a dig at the author's motives, in an essay whose advice is not to do that. A footnote assures you that I am neither a Republican nor a Democrat.

Finally, the rule. I am not asking you to be apolitical, only to skip gratuitous jabs at a party, because some readers may belong to it. Whether the party really is at fault does not matter. What matters is ``the spiritual growth of the community.'' So the rule exists to keep the peace, and I say so. Keeping the peace and finding out what is true are different aims, and I put keeping the peace first.

My title says that politics kills minds. My essay says that politics is a poor place to practice. Readers will remember the title, and use it to end conversations. Seven years later I will say that I meant ``national politics as seen on TV.''

\honest{Strong Evidence Is Common}{Mark Xu}{2021}

Someone asked my name. I told them, and they believed me. I guess they would have happily bet at 20 to 1 on it. Before I spoke, the odds that a given person is called Mark Xu were about a million to one against. So my saying it multiplied the odds by some twenty million, about 24 bits of evidence.

This is true, and worth knowing. An ordinary sentence can carry a huge amount of evidence. I do not say why. The reason is that the claim is very specific, and people rarely lie about their names. Later I apply the idea to claims where that reason does not hold, and I do not notice the difference.

I add three more cases of the same kind: a Wikipedia page, someone who says they can juggle, a result from Mathematica. All are reliable testimony or reliable tools. I do not point that out either.

Now the part I actually care about: you. Perhaps you are in the top 1\% of traders. To be 50\% sure of that, I say, you need only 200 to 1 evidence, and that ``might be easy to get.'' I do not say what the evidence would be. A good year is weak evidence, since returns are noisy and the traders who failed have mostly stopped trading. On the way I also say that the efficient market hypothesis makes it hard to make money. It says it is hard to beat the market.

Perhaps you are also much less overconfident than most people. That takes only 50 to 1 evidence, and an hour of calibration practice might supply it.

So I have moved from my own name, where testimony is strongest, to your own excellence, where evidence is weakest and wishful thinking is strongest. I made the move without comment.

I close by saying that working through Bayes's rule by hand may push people toward timid estimates. I give no argument for this. Then I reverse Carl Sagan's saying that extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence: such evidence, I say, ``might be more common than you think.'' Sagan meant claims that contradict what we know, such as alien visits. My name is not one of those.
