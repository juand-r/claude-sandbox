\section*{A note before starting}

Each section retells one post in its author's voice. It follows the post from start to finish and says plainly what each part does: what it claims, what it shows, and what it only appears to show. Where a paragraph contributes nothing to the argument, the retelling says so.

These are parodies written by the editor of \textsc{LessWrong, Annotated}, not the authors' words. Words inside quotation marks are the authors' own and have been checked against the originals. Every other claim about a post, including its errors, rests on the notes and Responses in the annotated edition, where the evidence is given.

The satire is meant to be fair. Where a post gets something right, the retelling says that too.

\honest{The Lens That Sees Its Flaws}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

I start with how you come to believe that your shoelaces are untied. Light from the Sun bounces off them and enters your eye, and your brain turns the signal into a belief. I describe each step so that you see forming a belief as a physical process, one that can be understood.

My point is that you can understand this process and a mouse cannot. I ask you to marvel at this. Two paragraphs later I say the same thing about mice again, and ask you to marvel again.

I say that science is this same ability, used systematically. The claim my argument depends on is that a mind can be understood and corrected the way a machine can. I state it in one sentence. My only support is the remark that understanding the mind may be ``a little more complicated'' than understanding a steam engine. That remark is irony, and irony is not a reason.

Now my one example. Years ago, in a chatroom, I asked strangers whether they expected a nuclear war in the next twenty years, and asked those who said no to explain why. Those who said yes were not asked to explain anything. One man said he expected no war for a hundred years, because ``All of the players involved in decisions regarding nuclear war are not interested right now.'' That is a reason about the world. I asked why he extended it to a hundred years, and he said ``Pure hope.'' From those two words I conclude that the thought of nuclear war makes him unhappy, and that his brain rejects it for that reason.

To show that hoping for peace does not make peace more likely, I bring in parallel universes and a paper by Max Tegmark. Ordinary probability makes the same point without them. The point is that wanting something to be true is not evidence that it is true. This is called wishful thinking, and it has been known for a long time.

I conclude that a mind which knows its own flaws can ``apply a reflective correction,'' and that this makes it ``far more powerful.'' I do not say what the correction is, how large it should be, or how you would know it had worked. Five months earlier I wrote that knowing about biases can make people reason worse. I do not mention that here.

In a footnote I say that a small effect should change your beliefs only a little, and that we must now use Bayes's theorem to measure it. Then I do not do the calculation. That footnote contains the most useful idea in the essay.

\honest{Politics Is the Mind-Killer}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

People think badly about politics. The reason, I say, is ``so obvious as to be worth belaboring'': in the ancestral environment, being on the wrong side of an argument could get you killed. After saying it is worth belaboring, I do not belabor it. I give no evidence for it, here or later.

My advice is this. When you want to teach a point about rationality, do not take your example from current politics; use Louis XVI instead. Politics is a terrible place to learn rationality, ``unless all the discussants are already rational.'' Any group that wants to argue about politics can declare itself rational, so the rule lets anyone exempt themselves.

Next I say that arguments are soldiers, and that sensible scientists become slogan-chanting zombies when a political issue comes up. I give no evidence for this. I describe it as something other people do.

Then comes my example, a standard problem in artificial intelligence: Quakers are pacifists, Republicans are not, and Nixon is both. The problem exists to show how a program should reason with rules that are usually true but have exceptions. I state the rules with ``all,'' so they have no exceptions and simply contradict each other. The standard version says ``usually.''

I spend two of my eight paragraphs asking why the problem's author, whom I do not name, chose Nixon. Was it to make Republicans feel unwelcome in courses on artificial intelligence? I decide the author could not resist a dig at the other side. That is itself a dig at the author's motives, in an essay that goes on to advise against such digs. A footnote tells you that I am neither a Republican nor a Democrat.

Finally, the rule. I am not asking you to be apolitical, only to avoid needless jabs at a party, because some readers may belong to it. Whether the party really is at fault does not matter. What matters is ``the spiritual growth of the community.'' So the purpose of the rule is to keep the community peaceful, and I say so. That is a different aim from finding out what is true, and I put it first.

My title says that politics kills minds. The essay says only that politics is a poor place to practice reasoning. Members of the community later quoted the title to cut political discussions short. In 2014 I said that I had meant ``national politics as seen on TV.''

\honest{Strong Evidence Is Common}{Mark Xu}{2021}

Someone asked my name. I told them, and they believed me. I guess they would have happily bet at 20 to 1 that it was true. Before I spoke, the odds that a given person is called Mark Xu were about a million to one against. So my saying it multiplied the odds by about twenty million, which is about 24 bits of evidence.

This is true, and worth knowing: an ordinary statement can be very strong evidence. I do not say why. The reason is that the claim is narrow, and people rarely lie about their names. That reason matters later, because it does not hold for the claims I turn to next.

I add three more cases of the same kind: a Wikipedia page, someone who says they can juggle, and a result from Mathematica. All of them are reliable testimony or reliable tools. I do not say so.

Next I apply the idea to the reader. Perhaps you are in the top 1\% of traders. To be 50\% sure of that, I say, you need only 200 to 1 evidence, and that ``might be easy to get.'' I do not say what the evidence would be. A good year of trading is weak evidence, because returns vary a lot by chance, and the traders who lost have mostly stopped. In the same paragraph I say that the efficient market hypothesis makes it hard to make money. What it says is that it is hard to beat the market.

Perhaps you are also much less overconfident than most people. That takes only 50 to 1 evidence, and an hour of calibration practice might supply it.

So I have moved from my own name, where testimony is most reliable, to the reader's own excellence, where evidence is weakest and wishful thinking is strongest. I do not point out the change.

I end by saying that working through Bayes's rule by hand may push people toward timid estimates. I give no argument for this. Then I reverse Carl Sagan's saying: extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence, but such evidence ``might be more common than you think.'' Sagan meant claims that contradict what we know, such as visits by aliens. A person's name is not that kind of claim.
