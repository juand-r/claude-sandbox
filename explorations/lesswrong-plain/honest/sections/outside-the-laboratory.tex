\honest{Outside the Laboratory}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

``Outside the laboratory, scientists are no wiser than anyone else.'' Is the proverb true? ``Probably not in an absolute sense,'' I say, but it ``does appear true to some degree,'' and that should alarm us. I give no evidence for the second part. \nb{In 2009 a Pew survey found American scientists about half as likely as the general public to believe in God or a higher power: 51\% against 95\%. That fits both the premise and its hedge.}

A shepherd trained to count sheep who cannot count apples has not understood counting. An economist who buys a lottery ticket every week may not understand expected utility. \nb{Whether the ticket is a mistake depends on how much the anticipation is worth, which is what the defenders in ``Lotteries: A Waste of Hope'' argue.} Feynman's students in Brazil had memorized optics and did not recognize it in water.

Why do experiments? Not because journals demand them, but because to map a territory you must go and look: light from your shoelaces has to reach your eye.

Now take a scientist who believes in a spirit world and says that observation cannot settle the question. ``I cannot but conclude that this person literally doesn't know why you have to look at things.'' If the scientist says the spirits spoke in their heart, that is a causal interaction and counts as evidence, to be weighed by its likelihood ratio against other causes and against the prior. Gould's ``separate magisteria,'' I say, is an ``immortal blunder of a phrase.''

To me, a scientist who accepts that spiritual matters cannot be settled by observation understands experiment only as a social convention. The rules of addition are the same for sheep and apples, and Newton showed that the planets obey the same laws as falling apples. Reality is one process under simple laws, and ``Nor is Bayes's Theorem different from one place to another.''

So a scientist who is as open to ``wacky ideas'' as anyone else outside their field ``probably never did understand why the scientific rules work.'' They ``don't like to be constrained by evidence,'' and it makes me wonder whether to trust them even in their own field. \nb{The scientist behind this diagnosis is the imagined one of the earlier paragraphs. The post gives no evidence about how real scientists who hold religious beliefs reason, inside or outside their fields.}

Maybe we can do better, if we learn enough probability theory to know why the rules work. ``A witty saying proves nothing,'' as Voltaire said.
