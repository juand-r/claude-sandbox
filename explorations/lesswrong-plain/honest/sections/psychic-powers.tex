\honest{Psychic Powers}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2008}

I open by quoting my previous post: ``You can never get Bayesian confirmation for a hypothesis of irreducibility,'' because any prediction you can make could also be made by a reducible thing, your brain. Benja Fallenstein objected. No test could logically prove irreducibility, but an outcome could make it ``subjectively much more probable.'' Suppose, for example, that the universe allowed hypercomputers working on arbitrary real numbers, though our brains, simulable by an ordinary Turing machine, do not use them.

I promise a crushing reply, and it is: ``You're right.'' I get no modesty credit, since after publishing ``yesterday's post'' I found a similar flaw myself, concerning Occam's razor and psychic powers. \nb{LessWrong dates both posts 12 September 2008; in United States time the first went up the evening before.}

If beliefs were ontologically basic, a law linking minds directly would be much simpler than it is if beliefs are complex patterns of neurons. So information passing between brains with no material connection is ``probabilistically a privileged prediction of supernatural models.'' If naturalism is right, a reductionist model can match any concrete prediction a parapsychologist makes, and we can conceive of beliefs as fundamental only because our minds' reflective architecture shows us the ``belief'' class and hides the neurons behind it. The hope of psychic powers comes from treating beliefs and desires as fundamental enough to connect directly to reality. If beliefs are patterns of neurons, with inputs through eyes and outputs through muscles, and that accounts for all known mental powers, there is no reason to expect more. That is why reductionists expect no psychic powers. A naive Occam rule that counts ``belief'' as one basic entity favours psychic powers, since they then need only simple laws. The naturalistic rule does not. Discovering them would show that the naive rule was better calibrated, that what our minds show as a simple lever really is one, and that the naive dualists were right all along.

So telepathy, precognition and influencing events by wishing would be ``strong Bayesian evidence'' that beliefs are fundamental. ``Not logical proof.'' A science-fiction story with psychic powers can be written by a reducible author's brain, but finding the powers in fact would make it much more probable that something irreducible was happening. Non-reductionists ``own'' this prediction, since they put it forward against reductionist expectations, so if psychic powers are discovered, non-reductionism wins. \nb{The history supports this. F.~W.~H. Myers, a founder of the Society for Psychical Research, hoped telepathy would show mind communicating with mind apart from recognised channels, and so support survival after death.}

``I am therefore confident in dismissing psychic powers as a priori implausible, despite all the claimed experimental evidence in favor of them.'' I do not describe that evidence. \nb{It existed and was contested. Meta-analyses of ganzfeld experiments reported an effect in 1994, found none in 30 further studies in 1999, and reported one again, pooling 79 studies, in 2001.} The word ``confident'' links to my post ``I Defy the Data!'' \nb{That post's addendum allows defying the data of one experiment, not of many. Beyond that, it says, one must give up the theory or dismiss the data with reasons, such as a design flaw or a larger body of failed replications. The post gives no such reason.}
