\honest{Belief as Attire}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

In earlier posts I described three kinds of improper belief. Here is a fourth: belief worn as a badge of belonging, like a priest's vestments or a skullcap. Robin Hanson gave me the image, and I call it ``belief as attire.'' I give no test for telling a belief worn for belonging from a belief held for good reasons that one's group happens to share.

My example: the men who flew planes into the World Trade Center ``undoubtedly'' saw themselves as heroes fighting alien monsters, ``a la the movie \textsc{Independence Day}.'' I cite nothing they wrote or said. My picture of how the Enemy sees the world comes from a Hollywood film about an alien invasion.

Only an inexperienced nerd, I say, would say this in an Alabama bar. The American thing to say is that the terrorists ``hate our freedom'' and committed a ``cowardly act.'' \nb{The first phrase is President Bush's, from his speech to Congress nine days after the attacks.} I present it as what ordinary Americans say. In America, I say, you cannot call a suicide bomber's act heroic self-sacrifice. \nb{Within two weeks of the attacks, Bill Maher said on television that staying in the airplane was not cowardly, and Susan Sontag wrote in \textsc{The New Yorker} that ``they were not cowards.'' Both were attacked for it. So it could be said, at a price.}

My example has a second problem. The belief I chose, that the hijackers were cowards, is a moral judgment. By my own scheme, a moral judgment predicts no experience, so every moral judgment is improper belief, sincere or not. My example cannot tell attire from conviction, which is what it was chosen to do.

Next I explain why people can be passionate about improper beliefs. Religious people who no longer really expect their religion to be true work hard to convince themselves that they are passionate, ``But it's not the same fire they had as a child.'' This is my ``impression,'' and ``I confess I'm not an expert here.'' \nb{No observation could show this wrong, since any passionate believer can be described as desperate.} Five days ago I published a test for beliefs that predict nothing. This one fails it.

Belonging to a tribe, on the other hand, is easy and strong, like cheering for a sports team. ``People will die for it.'' The beliefs a tribe wears are then spoken with the full passion of belonging. My example, a sports team, involves no belief at all.

In a footnote I call Republicans versus Democrats a ``swindle'' and a false dilemma, ``a topic for another time.'' I name no swindler and no third option.

The people whose beliefs I describe as attire are Americans in an Alabama bar, religious people who have lost their fire, and the supporters of both parties. I do not ask whether any belief of mine, or of my readers, is worn this way.
