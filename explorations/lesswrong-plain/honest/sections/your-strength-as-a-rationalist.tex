\honest{Your Strength as a Rationalist}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

Long ago, in a chatroom, someone said that a friend of his had chest pains and called an ambulance, and the paramedics told the friend it was nothing and left. Now the pains were getting worse. What should the friend do? I warn you that my memory of this may be imprecise.

The story confused me. I remembered reading that paramedics in New York must take homeless people to hospital even on their 27th call, and that emergency rooms are legally obliged to treat anyone. So anyone reporting chest pains should have been ``hauled off by an ambulance instantly.'' I give no source for any of this, and what I remembered is wrong. A competent adult can refuse to go to hospital, and many do. The law requires hospitals to examine and stabilize patients, not to treat anyone for anything.

I also remembered that my own doctor never panicked about my symptoms, and was always right. So I told the stranger that if the paramedics said it was nothing, ``it must really be nothing.'' His friend's chest pains were getting worse. If the story had been true, my advice could have delayed treatment for a heart attack. I do not mention this again.

Later the stranger came back and said his friend had made the whole thing up. So I conclude that my confusion was a clue that the story was false, and that I threw the clue away. The only evidence that the story was false is the word of the same stranger who told it.

I say I should have known that a friend of a stranger in a chatroom is less reliable than a published journal article. My model did not come from a journal article. It came from something I remembered reading and from my own visits to the doctor.

Then the principle: a model is useful for what it cannot explain, and a model that forbids nothing tells you nothing. This is true, and it is Karl Popper's idea. It also does not describe my mistake. My model did forbid something: it forbade the story, which is why I was confused. My mistake was to ignore what it forbade.

Then the thesis: ``Your strength as a rationalist is your ability to be more confused by fiction than by reality.'' Nobody knows in advance which story is the fiction. A true report that clashes with a wrong model is just as confusing as a lie. A reader who takes confusion as a sign of fiction will dismiss the true report and keep the wrong model.

I end by wishing that the feeling of a forced explanation came as a glowing neon sign reading ``Either Your Model Is False Or This Story Is Wrong.'' In my case both were true. The story was made up, and my model of ambulances was false. My thesis tells you to pick the second.

Two footnotes. In the first I say that hospitals are closing their emergency rooms and that economists are being ignored. I give no source, and it has nothing to do with my argument. In the second I cite evidence that we believe what we hear by default: a blog post of mine from the day before.
