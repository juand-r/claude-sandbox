\honest{Chaotic Inversion}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2008}

I have always struggled to keep working hour after hour. I can stay with a problem for seconds or for years, but not for hours. Reading reams of advice, however reasonable it sounded, has mostly taught me that many other creative professionals have the same trouble and cannot beat it either.

Some friends asked what I do when I cannot work. I give the conversation as ``a very loose gist''. I browse the web or watch short videos, I said. If you know you cannot work for a while, they said, watch a film. I replied that I must do things that come in short units, because I might become able to work again at any time, and I cannot predict when.

Then I stopped. I had always treated my work cycle as chaotic. But my friends ``seemed to be implying'' that other people can predict when they will be able to work again. \nb{As reported, the friends said ``if you know'', which does not say that anyone does.} Perhaps I had been committing the Mind Projection Fallacy in everyday life. ``Maybe it wasn't that my productivity was unusually chaotic; maybe I was just unusually stupid with respect to predicting it.''

Chaos is ``what inverted stupidity looks like'': something hard to handle, grasp or guess, that you can do nothing with, not only in abstract matters like AI but in ordinary life. We do not think of the explanation ``I'm stupid'', I suspect, not because we think highly of ourselves, but because ``we don't think of ourselves at all.'' \nb{The only case given is the author's own.}

This may or may not help. ``Saying `I'm ignorant' doesn't make you knowledgeable.'' But it is a different path from saying ``it's too chaotic''.
