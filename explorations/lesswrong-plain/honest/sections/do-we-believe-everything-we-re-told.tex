\honest{Do We Believe Everything We're Told?}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

Early experiments tested whether keeping people busy, for example watching for the digit 5 in strings of numbers, increased anchoring. Most of them, I say, ``seemed to bear out the idea.'' Looking at such results, Daniel Gilbert asked: ``Do we believe everything we're told?''

Descartes thought we first understand a proposition, then consider it, then accept or reject it. Spinoza thought we accept a proposition in the course of understanding it, and only afterwards reject it if it fails. For centuries, I say, philosophers ``pretty much went along with Descartes.'' \nb{The 1990 paper by Gilbert, Krull and Malone, which the post cites, says that philosophers such as Reid and Russell questioned Descartes's view, and that it is ``modern psychology'' that continues to hold it. The post's footnote on this sentence cites a blog post by Robin Hanson about policy debates.}

If Spinoza is right, distraction should make people remember false statements as true, but not true ones as false. Gilbert, Krull and Malone ``bear out this result'': when people learning made-up words were interrupted, they identified true statements as well as before (58\% against 55\%) and false ones much worse (35\% against 55\%). \nb{The study had 35 students, each with two interrupted statements of each kind. Two years before the post, Hasson, Simmons and Todorov found the same pattern only for statements that tell you little when false, and concluded that ``comprehending a statement may not require believing it.'' The post does not mention them.}

In a follow-up, people read crime reports in which false statements were printed in a different color. Busy readers recommended 11.15 years in prison when the false statements made the crime worse and 5.83 years when they excused it. Readers who were not busy recommended 7.03 and 6.03 years. The paper was called ``You Can't Not Believe Everything You Read.''

So be careful with unreliable information, ``especially if we're doing something else at the time.'' And, I add, by a rumor I just made up, you will be less skeptical of this essay because of its color changes.
