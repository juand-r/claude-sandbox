\honest{The Design Space of Minds-In-General}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2008}

People ask me what AIs will be like. I tell them they have asked a trick question.

Animals, plants and bacteria, you and a petunia included, have essentially the same molecular machine, ATP synthase, one of three known cases where evolution invented a freely rotating wheel, unchanged for two billion years, because genes that many others depend on are hard to change. ``Any two AI designs might be less similar to each other than you are to a petunia.''

Humans form a natural class because we share one brain architecture. But ``AI'' names a far larger space of possibilities, so when people talk about AIs, ``we are really talking about minds-in-general,'' or optimization processes; a word for ``AI'' is like a word for everything that is not a duck. \nb{The post's own reason why humans form a class is a shared architecture. Whether AIs made by shared methods would share one too, the post does not take up; Quintin Pope later raised this.}

Then I draw my standard diagram. Humans are a tiny dot, since a sexually reproducing species cannot vary much. The dot sits in a transhuman region, minds perhaps much smarter than us but still people as we understand people. That region sits inside a posthuman region, everything a transhuman might grow into, which sits inside a sphere of minds-in-general, including AIs too odd even to be posthuman. The sphere floats in a larger space of optimization processes, with natural selection at the bottom, as it began in some tidal pool: mutate, replicate, sometimes die. Hutter's AIXI may be a powerful optimizer that we would not call a mind; for a mind of infinite power it is awfully stupid, unable even to recognize itself in a mirror. But that is for another day.

My moral is to resist generalizing over all of mind design space. \nb{Aaron Sloman proposed mapping the space of possible minds in 1984, in ``The structure of the space of possible minds''.} There are about two to the trillionth power minds that can be specified in a trillion bits, so a claim that all minds do something has that many chances to be false, and a claim that some mind does it has that many chances to be true. People make such claims by imagining themselves in the mind's place, which gives an anthropomorphic answer, true of at least one mind. Or they imagine a mind doing something and the reasons they would not do it, as if a ghost in the machine would read the source code and hand it back.

``Somewhere in mind design space is at least one mind with almost any kind of logically consistent property you care to imagine.'' So explain what a mind does from its particular makeup. And do not say that a mind which does things differently is ``by definition'' not a mind.
