\honest{The Importance of Saying ``Oops''}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

I have just read a history of Enron's collapse, \textsc{The Smartest Guys in the Room}, and I give it my award for ``Least Appropriate Book Title.''

Enron's executives, I say, never admitted a large mistake. When a disaster forced a change of policy, they said, ``Too bad that didn't work out,'' and asked how to hide the problem on the balance sheet. I made up that line to show self-deception. What it shows is people who knew about the problem and hid it. I note that after the bankruptcy Jeff Skilling told Congress that Enron had been a great company. I do not mention that he was convicted of fraud more than a year before I wrote this.

My thesis: every improvement is a change, and if you admit only small mistakes you will make only small changes. A big change needs a big admission. I give no evidence for this, and I do not ask whether many small corrections can add up to a big one.

I grew up on Heinlein and Feynman, and learned what I call Traditional Rationality: make bold theories that can be proved false, give up your ideas when the evidence goes against them, argue fairly, do not deceive yourself. I call these ``fuzzy verbalisms.'' Traditional Rationality, I say, teaches you to give in to the evidence eventually, but not quickly. I quote no one who holds it.

Then I made a large mistake, and switched to Bayes. What was the mistake? ``Well, it's a long story.'' I do not tell it. This is an essay about admitting large mistakes fully.

Looking back, I say, I had admitted my mistake in small, grudging pieces. I could have moved much faster by simply shouting ``OOPS!''

So admit a large mistake all at once. Do not be proud of admitting errors; it is better to be right the first time. ``Even hedonically, it is better to take one large loss than many small ones.'' ``The alternative is Enron.'' What Enron actually did was commit fraud.

Since then, I say, I have watched others concede each millimeter of ground. I list their excuses, such as ``I was right in principle'' and ``It could have worked,'' so that you will recognize them in other people. Nothing in the essay helps you find your own large mistake.

I end: ``Better to swallow the entire bitter pill in one terrible gulp.''
