\honest{Strong Evidence Is Common}{Mark Xu}{2021}

Someone asked my name. I told them, and they believed me. I guess they would have happily bet at 20 to 1 on it. Before I spoke, the odds that a given person is called Mark Xu were about a million to one against. So my saying it multiplied the odds by some twenty million, about 24 bits of evidence.

An ordinary sentence can carry a huge amount of evidence. \nb{This is true, and worth knowing. The post does not say why: the claim is very specific, and people rarely lie about their names. Later the post applies the idea to claims where that reason does not hold, without mentioning the difference.}

Three more cases of the same kind: a Wikipedia page, someone who says they can juggle, a result from Mathematica. \nb{All are reliable testimony or reliable tools, and the post does not point that out either.}

Then I turn to you. The efficient market hypothesis implies that making money on the stock market is hard; generously, perhaps only the top 1\% of traders profit. Perhaps you are among them. To be 50\% sure of that, you need only 200 to 1 evidence, and that ``might be easy to get.'' I do not say what the evidence would be. \nb{From prior odds of 1 to 99, evidence of 200 to 1 gives about 67\%; 99 to 1 would give 50\%. A good year is weak evidence, since returns are noisy and the traders who failed have mostly stopped trading.}

People are overconfident on average, but 12\% are not. To conclude that you are much less overconfident than average takes only 50 to 1 evidence, and an hour of calibration practice and the resulting calibration plots might supply it.

\nb{The post has moved from the author's name to the reader's own excellence, a subject on which people tend to flatter themselves, and it makes the move without comment.}

Last: running through Bayes's rule explicitly ``might produce a bias towards middling values.'' I support this only with the examples above. Then I accept the saying that extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence, and add that such evidence ``might be more common than you think.'' \nb{Sagan meant claims that contradict what we know, such as alien visits. A person's name is not one of those.}
