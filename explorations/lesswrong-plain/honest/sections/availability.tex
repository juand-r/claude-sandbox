\honest{Availability}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

The availability heuristic, I explain, is judging how frequent or likely an event is by how easily examples of it come to mind. \nb{The idea and the name come from Tversky and Kahneman (1973).}

My evidence is a 1978 study by Lichtenstein and colleagues. Its subjects thought accidents killed about as many people as disease, and that homicide was more common than suicide. In fact, I say, disease kills about sixteen times as many people as accidents, and suicide is ``twice as frequent as homicide.''

A 1979 follow-up found that the errors matched what two newspapers reported. I admit this does not show whether reporting shapes memory or vividness shapes both. ``But either way,'' I say, ``an availability bias is at work.''

Selective reporting is a major cause, and I say it is worse now than for our ancestors, who had ``usually at most one layer of selective reporting'' between them and an event. I give no source for that. You hear about Bill Gates far more than his numbers justify, and you may compare your success to his and feel worse. \nb{That is a point about comparison and mood, not about judging how often something happens.}

Availability also ``seems'' to make people treat what has never happened as impossible. People refuse flood insurance even when it is heavily subsidized. I quote Kunreuther and colleagues: men on flood plains are ``prisoners of their experience.'' \nb{Yudkowsky's paper on global risks, from which much of this post is taken, gives these words to Kunreuther and colleagues in its 2006 draft and to Robert Kates (1962) in the version published in 2008.} Dams prevent small floods, so people take fewer precautions, and the yearly damage rises.

From floods I draw a rule: a society protected against minor hazards ``takes no action against major risks.'' \nb{The paragraph before had said ``reduced precautions,'' which is less than no action.} I end: ``Memory is not always a good guide to probabilities in the past, let alone in the future.''
