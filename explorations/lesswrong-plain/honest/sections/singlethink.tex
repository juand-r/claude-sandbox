\honest{Singlethink}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

I remember the exact moment when my journey as a rationalist began. It was not while reading Feynman; what I read in books I simply accepted as obvious. It began when I saw a flaw in my own skills.

I was fifteen, and going over a ``pleasantly self-righteous'' memory from summer camp. I was six or seven, I think; the memory is vague. A teenage counselor lined us up for a game in which each boy crawled through our legs and we spanked him as he went. I refused, and was sent to sit in the corner. The memory had come to mean that even then I would not hurt others, and would not play a negative-sum game.

At fifteen I saw that this was untrue. I learned about the Prisoner's Dilemma early, but not at seven. I had refused ``simply because I didn't want to get hurt.'' \nb{The Prisoner's Dilemma point rules out only the negative-sum framing. A seven-year-old can refuse to hurt others without knowing any game theory. The post does not consider that motive.} And I had always known it: the real memory was always there, and I kept glancing at it and looking away. \nb{This sense of having always known is the only evidence for the new reading. The post has just shown that the author's reading of this memory could be confidently wrong, and does not ask whether the new one could be wrong too.}

I caught the feeling of shoving an unwanted truth into a corner, and resolved to notice it every time. I named the skill singlethink, after Orwell's doublethink: in doublethink you forget, and forget that you have forgotten; in singlethink you notice that you are forgetting, and remember, holding a single non-contradictory thought. \nb{The gloss matches Orwell's account in \textsc{Nineteen Eighty-Four}.} It was my first new skill, though I doubt it is original ``in the sense of academic priority.'' My fifteen-year-old self liked to name things.

Confirmation bias goes deep; not forever, since the brain is finite, but it feels like forever, and you keep finding new ways in which your brain pushes things aside. ``But my young self swept out quite a few corners with that first broom.'' I describe none of them.
