\honest{Conservation of Expected Evidence}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

In 1631 the priest Friedrich Spee described how accused witches were judged. A bad life proved guilt, and so did a good one, because witches pretend to be virtuous. Fear in prison proved guilt, and so did calm. Spee heard many confessions, so he could see every branch: whatever the woman did, it counted against her. This is a good example, and Spee saw its point without any probability theory.

It is for this reason, I say, that scientists write down their predictions in advance. In 2007, outside clinical trials, few did.

Then my law, which I name ``Conservation of Expected Evidence'': your expected belief after seeing the evidence must equal your belief before. I write out three lines, the third of which is the law of total probability from any first course in the subject. The step my name refers to, that the expected change in belief is zero, I leave for you to work out.

Then the best paragraph. A confident theory gains little when its prediction comes true, and loses a lot when the prediction fails. This is right. I end the paragraph: ``On average, you must expect to be exactly as confident as when you started out.'' Your expected probability for the theory stays the same, but you expect to end up more sure, one way or the other. That is what evidence is for.

Three applications follow. If no sabotage is evidence for a Fifth Column, then sabotage must be evidence against one. If a good life shows that a woman is a witch, then an evil life must show that she is not. Both are correct. Then: if God refuses to reveal Himself in order to test our faith, ``then the miracles described in the Bible must argue against the existence of God.'' My law pairs the two possible outcomes of a single observation. God's silence now and miracle stories written long ago are two different observations.

``For a true Bayesian, it is impossible to seek evidence that confirms a theory.'' Two paragraphs earlier I described how to do it: pick a test your theory will probably pass. What you cannot do is expect your belief to rise on average.

This, I say, should take a load off your mind, since you need not plan how to make the evidence confirm your theory. You can sit back and relax while you wait for the evidence. That is advice for an ideal reasoner. A human can reinterpret results to suit a theory, and often does.

``Human psychology is so screwed up.'' I end there, without returning to Spee or saying what his judges should have done.
