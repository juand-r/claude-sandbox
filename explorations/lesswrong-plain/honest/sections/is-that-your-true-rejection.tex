\honest{Is That Your True Rejection?}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2008}

Some people hear my ideas about superintelligence and Friendly AI and reject them. Asked why, they sometimes say, ``Why should I believe anything Yudkowsky says? He doesn't have a PhD!'' Some advise me to get one. There are good and bad reasons to get a PhD, I say, and this is one of the bad ones. I do not say why.

My explanation of my critics is this. The idea reminds them of science fiction or an end-of-the-world cult, and they reject it ``at the speed of perception.'' Afterwards they look for a respectable reason and land on the PhD. I give no evidence that any critic reasoned this way. I simply describe what happens in their heads.

The ``true reason,'' I explain, is whatever caused the rejection at the very first moment. I do not mention that people cannot reliably report causes that act that fast. So the question in my title asks people for a report that, by my own definition, they cannot give.

Nobody asks for my PhD, I note, when I write about human rationality. But a reader can check claims about reasoning against their own experience, and cannot check claims about superintelligence. A year ago, in ``Argument Screens Off Authority,'' I wrote that when a listener cannot evaluate an argument, the speaker's authority is what they have to go on.

If I had a PhD, I say, they would ask for tenure at a major university instead, and people do not believe Harvard professors who say weird things either. I suspect that credentials start to outweigh a first impression only at about the level of a Nobel prize. ``If that.'' This is a convenient threshold for someone without a doctorate.

My one example is Eric Drexler. People told him to come back when he had a PhD; he got one, and wrote a technical book. Did those people change their minds? ``Not so far as I ever heard.'' After the doctorate, his best-known critic was Richard Smalley, a Nobel laureate in chemistry, who argued in detail that Drexler's machines could not work. By my threshold, a laureate's word should count. I do not mention him.

The idea, I say, is useful in business: the reason a customer gives for saying no may not be the real one. That is true.

In disagreements, Robin Hanson and I believe that two rationalists should not agree to disagree. I list the hidden sources of lasting disagreement, from rare but sound knowledge and hard-to-state intuitions to habit, fear that a past mistake could be exposed, and self-deception for the sake of pride. I give no way to tell which one applies in a given case, and the person accused cannot disprove any of them.

Then the advice: ask yourself whether this is your true rejection. Openly psychoanalyzing the other person, I warn, makes a conversation go bad ``very fast.'' I have spent most of this post doing that to my critics. In the last paragraph I decide that asking the other person is ``fair game'' after all, if done humbly, as long as the most embarrassing possibilities are left unspoken. I have just listed them.

Two days later I asked Robin Hanson, in public and ``in all humility,'' whether this was his true rejection.
