\honest{The Failures of Eld Science}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2008}

This is a story. No robes this time: Jeffreyssai teaches a class of five in the Conspiracy, Brennan among them, admitted for political advantage rather than promise, and knowing it. \nb{The book has already shown Brennan's initiation, in ``Initiation Ceremony,'' and Jeffreyssai, in ``The Ritual.''}

Jeffreyssai asks how the scientists of the Eld era, ours, failed ``for over thirty years'' to see something about quantum physics: ``Not one of them saw it; not Einstein, not Schrödinger, not even von Neumann.'' I do not name what they missed. \nb{The students' talk of a ``universal law'' points to many-worlds, which Yudkowsky had defended the day before in ``Many Worlds, One Best Guess''; the notes on that post assess the case. Schrödinger came close: in a Dublin lecture in 1952 he said that the alternatives his equation describes are ``not alternatives but all really happen simultaneously.''}

The students try answers. Hiriwa notes this was 250 years after Newton, so the idea of a universal law was known. Taji answers that knowing the universal law of gravity is not understanding the concept of a universal law. Hiriwa wonders whether a religious injunction stopped them, and is told to think of cognitive errors instead. ``Mistakes don't travel alone; they hunt in packs,'' says Taji.

Styrlyn says only one in a hundred thousand could have written Schrödinger's equation, a failure to concentrate forces, and is told off for propaganda: would calling in the whole species have helped? ``Comparative advantage.'' Taji lists grant proposals and tenure. Yin replies that Schrödinger ``retired to a villa for a month, with his mistress to provide inspiration, and emerged with his eponymous equation.'' \nb{The usual account agrees: a mountain cabin at Arosa, December 1925.} Taji calls administrative burdens a generic obstacle. Jeffreyssai asks what kind of system would burden its own people.

Brennan says ``Lack of pragmatic motivation'': the Manhattan Project had a weapon to build and its scientists were spared grant proposals. Quantum theory had no such use, and a use would have shaped their curiosity and defined success.

The teacher's answer is mine: Eld science thought it ``acceptable'' to take thirty years. Its social process reached the truth eventually, discarding a wrong theory once a new generation grew up with the replacement, and work expands to fill the time allotted.

The teacher then drills Brennan. How long to dodge a knife? ``Less than a second.'' How long to judge which of two attackers is more dangerous, or which of two girlfriends you love? ``Less than a second.'' How long to change your mind when an argument shows your theory flawed? ``Less than a second.'' ``WRONG!'' Brennan must stop answering by pattern and think; pressed, he refuses to answer before he has finished thinking, and is praised. His realistic answer: notice at once, suspend judgment, re-gather the evidence, and if attached to the theory use the crisis-of-belief technique, so five minutes to an hour. In the Eld days, the teacher adds, a grant agency might spend six months on a proposal. \nb{True: that was the National Science Foundation's target at the time.}

Brennan has just learned something in under a minute; how long should a major problem take? He guesses a year, and thinks a month unrealistically short. A month holds 43,200 minutes, Hiriwa computes, or 28,800 waking ones; at five minutes an original insight, that is 5,760 insights. The teacher admits 5,760 insights is not realistic, but says a problem needs fewer, and ``yes, it has been done.'' I give no example.

Jeffreyssai judges all the answers as reasonable as his own except Styrlyn's; even Hiriwa's had something, since proposing new theories was once a duty reserved for the high-status.

Then he seats Brennan in front of the class with five minutes to say something brilliant while everyone gazes expectantly. Brennan decides how to think, then thinks, and speaks at four and a half minutes.

A wise woman told him to regard his past selves as fools beyond redemption, since respecting the past prevents a clean break. Looking at Eld scientists that way, though they were not fools, he says they were trained to use known science, not to resolve a major confusion, which was ``something you did once per lifetime if you were lucky.'' Open problems were rare and published as soon as solved, so no one could be trained on them. Scientists used to a neat, agreed body of knowledge may have seized unsatisfactory answers out of the fear of not knowing. So the founders, though not unintelligent, were ``amateurs,'' ad-libbing a paradigm shift. Hiriwa adds: ``And no probability theory,'' so anyone who succeeded could not explain what they had done. Styrlyn adds that a handful of people making a handful of discoveries each could not sustain a community, so each had to rediscover the rules. \nb{Einstein had resolved major confusions twice, with special and general relativity, and quantum mechanics grew out of twenty-five years of the confused ``old quantum theory.'' Jeffreys's Bayesian \textsc{Theory of Probability} appeared in 1939.}

The teacher accepts this and leaves the class 480 minutes to find one more flaw. \nb{The numbers fit: five students, five minutes an insight, 480 insights in all, 96 in series.} I do not say what the flaw is.
