\honest{Humans Are Not Automatically Strategic}{Anna Salamon}{2010}

I am replying to a post by Lionhearted, who asks why a man who wants to be the best comedian ever would study by watching reruns of \textsc{Garfield and Friends}. The comedian is my only worked example.

My answer starts with a child. An eight-year-old fails a calculus test because most possible answers are wrong and nothing guides the child to the right ones. Failure is the default, and needs no special explanation.

I apply this to adults. Most ways of pursuing a goal are ineffective, and no evolutionary or cultural force has pushed us toward the few that work. I give no evidence for the part about evolution and culture. The other examples go in a footnote: people train for a career without first comparing salaries, type with two fingers for years, and never check which of their hobbies they enjoy.

I do not consider that the adult, unlike the child, could look things up and may have chosen not to. \nb{Looking things up takes time. Stopping at good enough is often sensible; Herbert Simon called it satisficing. A two-finger typist near retirement may be right not to learn.}

I grant that we have goals in three weak senses: we tell stories about them, we do things that fit the role, and we feel glad or disappointed at the result. Then I list eight things we do not do automatically. We do not ask what we want, ask how we would know we had got it, feel curious about how to get it, gather that information, test different methods, focus on what works, make sure the goal ``is really our goal,'' or arrange our surroundings to keep us motivated.

I come close to a simpler explanation of the comedian: perhaps he likes cartoons more than he wants the hard work, and ``becoming a comedian'' is partly a story he tells. I have just said that we tell stories about our goals, and asked whether a goal is really our goal. I never draw the conclusion. I treat the gap between what people say they want and what they do as a lack of skill.

Why do we not do these things? Because, I say, ``humans are only just on the cusp of general intelligence.'' I give nothing to support this, and the rest of the argument does not need it. Perhaps 5\% of people, I add, can even understand these methods once they are pointed out. I give no source for the figure. Then comes the real explanation. Knowing that exercise is good for you does not make you want to exercise. I know that a glass floor high up in a building is safe, and I still have to walk across it many times before the rest of my brain agrees.

I say I am keen to train. There ``seem to be clear avenues'' for becoming more strategic, I say, and I do not name one. I also say, in half a sentence, that having goals in this fuller sense is part of what ``rational'' should mean. \nb{That widens the word a good deal.} I end by asking readers how they have trained themselves.

\nb{Two years later Salamon co-founded the Center for Applied Rationality, which teaches this kind of training in paid workshops.}
