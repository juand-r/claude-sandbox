\honest{Something to Protect}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2008}

In Japanese fiction, I say, power comes from having someone to protect. In Western comics, the hero ``gets bitten by a radioactive spider'' and then needs something to do with his powers, ``so he decides to fight crime.'' That hero is Spider-Man. He fights crime because he let a burglar escape and the burglar killed his uncle. His motive is the loss of someone he loved, which is my Japanese pattern.

Unhappy Westerners, I add, are told to pick an altruistic cause that suits them, ``like picking out nice living-room drapes.'' This is a good joke.

``Of course I'm not saying all this to generalize from fictional evidence.'' I say it so that you will not confuse my idea with the Western version.

My idea is that a rationalist must value something more than rationality. Science beat authority, I say, ``because it displayed greater raw strength in the form of technology, not because science sounded more reasonable.'' I cite no historian. The economic historian B. Zorina Khan, studying Britain's great inventors from 1750 to 1930, found that ``scientists, engineers or technicians were not well-represented among the British great inventors until very late in the nineteenth century.'' To people who say truth should matter more than usefulness, I say: ``Forget for a moment'' that people ``in pretty much that frame of mind defended the Bible.'' I ask you to forget the comparison, and I do not take it back. Then I explain that my love of truth is ``more complicated,'' and that I like ``declaring that I value mere usefulness above aesthetics,'' which is ``almost a contradiction, but not quite.''

My one argument is a dilemma. Option 1 saves 400 lives for certain. Option 2 saves 500 with probability 90\% and none otherwise. If your daughter is one of the 500, option 1 saves her with probability 80\% and option 2 with 90\%, and love may push you to ``shut up and multiply.'' But from her side neither option is certain. The ``comforting feeling of certainty'' that love was supposed to overcome is gone before love arrives. And 80 against 90 is the same ratio as 400 against 450, the expected number saved, so caring about her and counting everyone give the same answer because I chose numbers that make them agree. If she were certain to be among the 400, love would pick option 1. Nor do I give evidence that love makes people better with probabilities. In 2001 the psychologists Rottenstreich and Hsee predicted, and reported experiments supporting, that when an outcome stirs strong feeling people are ``less sensitive to intermediate probability variations.'' 80\% against 90\% is such a variation.

Standing alone against a crowd, I say, is ``much scarier than a mere threat to your life, according to the revealed preferences of teenagers who drink at parties and then drive home.'' The teenagers accept a small chance of death, not death.

Then my own history. I grew up an ``above-average Traditional Rationalist a la Feynman and Heinlein, and nothing more,'' and grew further only once I had ``something terribly important'' to do. I do not say what it was. A few paragraphs later I state, with this one case as evidence: ``No one masters the Way until more than their life is at stake.'' You cannot just pick a cause, I warn; but ``Learn how to multiply, and perhaps you will recognize a drastically important cause when you see one.''

Early in the post I say that the Art of rationality ``must have a purpose other than itself.'' I end by telling you to appreciate its beauty ``for its own sake.''
