\honest{Faster Than Science}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2008}

I sometimes say, I begin, that science piles up so much evidence that even scientists cannot ignore it. Max Planck was gloomier: new truths win because their opponents die. I am ``much tickled'' by this, since it means that science rests ``on the good taste of grad students.'' I quote Planck without a source. \nb{Studies that tested his remark, by Hull and colleagues in 1978 and by Azoulay and colleagues in 2019, found mixed results.}

Physicists, I say, are gradually coming to accept the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, which ``suggests'' that some of them need a large enough pack before they will convert. I give no survey and no numbers. \nb{Seventeen years later, a \textsc{Nature} survey of more than 1,100 physicists found 15\% favoring many-worlds, well behind the older Copenhagen interpretation. One survey shows a share, not a trend.}

Scientists are moved by prejudice and herd behavior, I say, and each unjustified shift must be undone with extra evidence, so academic science is ``a highly inefficient processor of evidence.'' \nb{The standard of comparison, as the post's ``more charitable'' version says, is an ideal reasoner, not any institution that exists.} The collapse of the wavefunction, I add, has no experimental support, and one argument against it is that it violates relativity. \nb{Roderich Tumulka had published a version of collapse consistent with relativity two years earlier.}

Then an accusation. In some fields debates drag on for decades because ``one side keeps throwing up more and more ridiculous objections'' from ``an entrenched position of academic power.'' I am thinking of evolutionary psychology, I say. I name no one, no paper and no objection.

Can individuals reach the truth faster than science? My answer is about where hypotheses come from. Science tests ideas but does not say where they come from. \nb{That is true, and philosophers have discussed it since Hans Reichenbach in the 1930s.}

Next, I say that when the possible answers are many, finding the right one takes more evidence than confirming it. Among four billion equations, getting one of them up to 10\% takes far more evidence than taking it from 10\% to 90\%. \nb{This is correct, and Charles Peirce made the same point in 1903. Much of the work that narrows the field is done by the scientist's training, which comes from the slow public process.}

Suppose, I say, that a robot-controlled Ouija board kept producing hypotheses that passed their tests. Science would not blink, but ``The pure ideal essence of Bayes would burst into flames and die.'' \nb{A Bayesian could update on the board's record like any other. What would fail is the claim that finding a hypothesis requires evidence.} ``Compared to Science,'' I add, ``Bayes is falsified by more of the possible outcomes.''

At the frontier, I say, scientists see things that are not yet confirmed. A scientist who holds the right hypothesis at 10\% ``must, in fact, have done'' most of the work, or he would never have thought of it, though he ``may not know'' that he has. So in the interval before the experiment, he ``rationally knew'' something science did not. \nb{At 10\%, he expects to be wrong nine times in ten. Many scientists reason the same way and guess wrong; the post counts only the one who guessed right.} Science knows it too, I say, once he ``performs the experiment, publishes the result.''

I end by saying that all this is ``more true for non-routine science than for routine science.'' I add: ``It is much easier to train people to test ideas, than to have good ideas to test.''
