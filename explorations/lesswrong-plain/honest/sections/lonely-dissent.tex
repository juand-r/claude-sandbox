\honest{Lonely Dissent}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

Asch's experiment, I say, showed that a single ally makes dissent easy, and that being the first to dissent is ``a lot harder.'' Both are true. Asch also found that even subjects facing the whole group alone gave the correct answer about two times in three, and that a quarter of them never gave in at all. I leave that out.

Lonely dissent, I say, feels like going to school in a clown suit, not like going to school dressed in black. ``If there's one thing I can't stand, it's fakeness,'' and everyone wants to be an iconoclast. I do not say how I know.

Vegetarians show some courage, I expect, but people understand them. Wearing black to school is a standard rebellion that everyone recognizes. Real courage means facing people who simply find you weird. My example is explaining cryonics, the freezing of the dead in the hope of reviving them later, which I support. The people who hear the explanation get no reasons, only noises: ``Huh? What? Are you crazy?'' I do not consider that some of them may simply think it will not work.

Then an evolutionary story, which I call post facto and then use as fact. A small group could leave the tribe together, but a person driven out alone probably died. So ``the fear of thinking really different is stronger than the fear of death.'' My evidence is that ``many more of us go skydiving than sign up for cryonics.'' A skydive is one ticket with a known result. Cryonics costs tens of thousands of dollars, and no one has ever been revived. I do not mention either difference.

The highest courage, I say, belongs to the first person to say a new thing, such as Robert Ettinger, who founded cryonics. From him I move to scientific revolutionaries, with every qualification: revolution is not the only route to greatness, you cannot get there by trying, and you need all existing knowledge plus luck. I never argue that cryonics belongs next to scientific revolution. I just put it there.

Then come the two sentences that matter most. Not every dissenting idea is good, and ``Most of the difficulty in having a new true scientific thought is in the `true' part.'' So differ only when you have an overwhelmingly good reason. In a footnote I cite Robin Hanson, whose post argues that careless dissenters are the main obstacle to good new ideas. I do not apply this to cryonics.

``Now me, you know, I really am an iconoclast.'' Everyone thinks they are, I say, with irony, but in my case it is true: I would ``totally'' have worn the clown suit. Then, seriously, I say that dissenting too easily is my own nature, and that I have to correct for it. ``You wouldn't want to end up as a free thinker.''
