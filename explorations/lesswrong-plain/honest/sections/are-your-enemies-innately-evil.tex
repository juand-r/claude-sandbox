\honest{Are Your Enemies Innately Evil?}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

We explain other people's actions by dispositions that match them. When someone offends us, ``I observe,'' the bias grows stronger, and we blame the Enemy's evil nature. \nb{The post cites no study for this.} As a matter of prior probability, we should ask what the Enemy believes about the situation.

On September 11th, 2001, nineteen hijackers flew four airliners on a suicide mission against the United States. Why? ``Because they saw the USA as a beacon of freedom to the world, but were born with a mutant disposition that made them hate freedom?'' \nb{No one is quoted holding this view. President Bush, who gave the ``hate freedom'' account to Congress nine days after the attacks, explained it by ``a fringe form of Islamic extremism,'' not by birth.}

Most people do not cast themselves as villains. ``Everyone is the hero of their own story.'' \nb{The post gives no evidence. A 1990 study of everyday quarrels by Baumeister and colleagues found that people who caused harm described it as ``meaningful and comprehensible,'' and their victims as ``arbitrary, gratuitous, or incomprehensible.''} If you imagine motives that make the Enemy look bad, you will be wrong about what goes on in the Enemy's mind. But politics is the mind-killer. An evil Enemy is an argument for your side, and every argument for your side must be supported, however silly. People compete in denunciation until the Enemy has horns and fangs, and anyone who denies a detail on factual grounds is a traitor. Few will see that such a person defends the truth, not the Enemy. If it took a mutant to do monstrous things, monstrous things would be rare. Or maybe we fear that understanding will lead to forgiveness. ``Die, vicious scum!'' is a better battle cry than ``Die, people who could have been just like me but grew up in a different environment!'', and killing people who are not pure darkness might make us feel guilty.

Wanting an evil enemy is wanting a policy with no downside. If a lunatic comes at you with a knife, killing someone who could have been your friend is a tragedy, and lying down to die is another. If someone has to die, better the one who started the violence, since that discourages future violence and minimizes total deaths. Understanding the Enemy's beliefs does not make them true or the acts justified. It means you may have to shoot someone who is the hero of their own story. A police officer makes that choice every day.

An accurate picture of the Enemy will make you sad, not righteous. More rarely it brings horror, as with ``true psychopaths'' or people whose beliefs have ``utterly destroyed their sanity (Scientologists or Jesus Campers).'' \nb{These two groups are named without evidence. The post blames their beliefs, not their birth, but it had just warned that construing motives which make the Enemy look bad will leave you ``flat wrong.''}

So the hijackers were not evil mutants, and they did not hate freedom. They died for what they believed was ``truth, justice, and the Islamic way.'' \nb{The post cites nothing they or their leaders said, as in ``Belief as Attire.'' Evidence that fits the claim existed: in 2004 Osama bin Laden called it ``Bush's claim that we hate freedom'' and asked why, if so, ``we don't strike for example -- Sweden?''} That does not make their beliefs true or their acts justified, and it does not mean the passengers of United Flight 93 should have stood aside. It means that, raised in a different environment, they might have been police officers, which is a tragedy. ``Welcome to Earth.''
