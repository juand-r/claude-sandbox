\honest{Toolbox-thinking and Law-thinking}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2018}

There are two styles of thought, I say. Toolbox thinkers keep many methods and choose among them by context. Law thinkers hold that some truths govern every case. I introduce the toolbox style by its suspicion of other people, and the law style as it works ``done correctly.''

My occasion is an exchange on Twitter. David Chapman defined rationalism as the belief in ``one weird trick'' to correct thinking, and added that some rationalisms ``insist there must be one, but that it is not currently knowable.'' Julia Galef replied that rationalists hold an ideal which no single rule can match in practice. Chapman answered that this distinction was new to him, ``useful and maybe key,'' and that the real disagreement might be whether an ideal ``that can't be achieved, even in principle, is a good thing.''

``I'm now going to badly stereotype this conversation,'' I say, ``in the form I feel like I've seen it many times previously.'' And I do. My stereotyped toolbox thinker hears an ideal as if it were a recipe. Chapman, as I have just quoted him, did not; he asked whether the ideal was worth having. I never answer his question.

Then comes a long maze. Every maze has a shortest path. The fact that a straight path is never longer than a detour always holds, but it tells you which way to walk only if you know the paths and care only about distance, and not, say, about avoiding stairs because someone in your party uses a wheelchair. A walker with a poor memory may do better keeping one hand on the wall than taking a shortcut. Knowing that a shortest path exists shows that there is room to improve. All of this is clear. But the maze comes with an agreed measure, distance, and a map would show the shortest path. What the measure is for thinking, and whether its ideal can be reached even in principle, is what Chapman asked. The maze assumes both answers.

Next, the objections to law thinking. I write them myself, as sweeping claims: Msr.\ Law ``will inevitably forget'' the context, and it ``will also never occur'' to Msr.\ Law to ride a bicycle round the maze. Among them is the one real case in the post: law thinkers who ``assume that the behavior of mortgage-backed securities is independently Gaussian-random because the math is neater,'' and bring down their trading firm. My reply is, ``Well, yes, that happens some of the time.'' Since I wrote the objections as if these failures always happen, ``some of the time'' defeats them. I never discuss the mortgages.

My resolution is that both styles are ``metatools in the metatoolbox,'' to be used ``in ways that depend on context and circumstance.'' Earlier in the post I described this very answer as the toolbox answer, ``as a more Toolbox-inclined person might put it.''

I close with a remedy for toolbox thinkers only. The best examples of laws, I say, are plain facts: that a straight path is never longer than a detour, the limit on the efficiency of engines, and Bayesian updating, since no different update can get a higher expected score. That last holds only if the prior probabilities and likelihoods are right, and the mortgage models are an example of when they were not. Seeing Bayes this way, I say, is ``a deeper level of understanding.''

In a clause near the end I concede that for values, such as the ideal of a good universe, ``there isn't any deeper fact of nature.'' Chapman's definition was about correct thinking and acting. Acting needs values.
