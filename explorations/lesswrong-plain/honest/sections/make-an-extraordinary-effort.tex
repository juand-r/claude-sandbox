\honest{Make an Extraordinary Effort}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2008}

I open with two epigraphs from Japanese martial texts. The second says: ``In important matters, a `strong' effort usually results in only mediocre results.'' I have seen this ``over and over again.''

A level beyond ``I want to become stronger'' is isshoukenmei: ``make a desperate effort,'' as if your life were at stake. The word began, I say, as the loyalty a samurai offered in return for his position. \nb{Japanese sources agree in outline. The older form, isshokenmei, referred to the land a samurai would defend with his life, and the spelling with the long vowel developed from it. Dictionaries give both spellings the same everyday meaning, ``with all one's might.''} On this point Japan's culture scores higher than America's: it has a short phrase for the idea.

Why don't people who call themselves rationalists do much better in life? From my own history, the answer ``seems straightforward'': it takes a tremendous amount of rationality to stop making stupid mistakes. Robert Aumann proved that Bayesians with the same priors cannot agree to disagree, and he is a believing Orthodox Jew. Knowing the math is not enough to save him, I say; it takes isshoukenmei, a desperate effort to be rational. \nb{Yudkowsky had used this example twice before. In a 2005 interview Aumann described his religion as ``mainly an emotional and aesthetic'' experience; see ``Crisis of Faith.''}

But Japan, with its more studious students, does not lead the United States in science. In the West, ``The squeaky wheel gets the grease''; in Japan, ``The nail that sticks up gets hammered down.'' Entrepreneurship, risk-taking and leaving the herd, I say, ``would seem to count for at least as much as desperate efforts.'' \nb{The evidence is one comparison between two countries, which differ in many ways besides these.}

So there is a second virtue. A desperate effort can lift more weight than usual, but lifting a truck takes something out of the ordinary. ``This is not included in isshokenmei, or Japan would be a very different place.'' \nb{In ``Crisis of Faith,'' written three days later but placed earlier in the book, Yudkowsky glosses isshokenmei as ``the desperate, extraordinary, convulsive effort to be rational,'' joining the two virtues this post separates.} The second virtue is higher, and more dangerous. A creative idea gone wrong can blow up the truck, and an embezzler also goes to extraordinary lengths. As a youth I told my little brother's friend, who wanted to change a game's rules just to be different, to break rules only when there is ``an overwhelmingly good reason.''

Many people have told me that Friendly AI is futile because corporations or the military will build the first AIs. Others tell me to get degrees first, ``at least a ten-year detour.'' I plan instead for a small, privately funded project, which needs only a few funders to think past the default. ``The life or death of the human species,'' I say, depends on a few people doing something a little extraordinary. Then I leave ``the details of that debate'' aside. \nb{The premise is stated here, not argued. Whether the extraordinary course was right in these two cases, both from the author's own work, depends on it.}

Keeping things ordinary can be a useful heuristic, I grant. But you should also have a sense of the cost of ordinariness. People accept sad futures because they seem ordinary. As a smiling man once said, ``It's all part of the plan.'' \nb{Probably the Joker in \textsc{The Dark Knight} (2008), who says that nobody panics when things go ``according to plan,'' even when the plan is horrifying.}
