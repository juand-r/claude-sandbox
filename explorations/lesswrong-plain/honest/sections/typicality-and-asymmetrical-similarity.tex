\honest{Typicality and Asymmetrical Similarity}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2008}

Is a robin or an ostrich the more typical bird? Most people say the robin, and psychologists study this as ``typicality effects.'' People confirm faster that a robin is a bird than that a penguin is. I am ``reasonably sure'' my source for this is Lakoff, though my books are still in boxes.

Typicality may be a heuristic, so I look for a bias it produces. ``98 is approximately 100'' sounds more natural than ``100 is approximately 98,'' and I cite Sadock (1977). \nb{Sadock's paper does not contain this comparison. The experiment behind it is Rosch's work on reference points: people put the more typical item in the reference slot of phrases like ``X is virtually Y.''} People also rate Mexico as more similar to the United States than the reverse. \nb{In Tversky and Gati's table the ratings are 7.65 and 6.46 on a scale of 1 to 20. Across their 21 pairs of countries the average difference was 0.42, and six pairs went the other way.}

In Rips's study, people expected a disease to spread from robins to ducks more readily than from ducks to robins. Any difference that would slow a disease one way, I argue, would slow it the other way too. \nb{A Bayesian model by Heit disagrees: if atypical species are believed to have many features of their own, a new property of ducks is more likely to be theirs alone.} I warn you not to rationalize answers from subjects ``who didn't even realize there was a comparison going on.'' The simpler explanation, I say, is that people use similarity as a stand-in for probability, and similarity is asymmetric. \nb{Rips measured similarity symmetrically and found a separate effect of how typical the species in the premise was. On Rips's analysis the asymmetry comes from typicality, not from asymmetric similarity.}

People also seem to treat Kansas as close to everything and Alaska as far from everything. \nb{A 1980 study by Sadalla and colleagues found the same asymmetry in judged distances between reference points and other places.}

So Aristotle's categories are a poor model of how people think, and ``Statements of set membership can be more or less true.'' \nb{Typicality does not show this. People rate 3 as a more typical odd number than 4,284,647, though both are plainly odd.}
