\honest{Making Beliefs Pay Rent (in Anticipated Experiences)}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

I open with the old riddle: if a tree falls in a forest and no one hears it, does it make a sound? One person says yes, because it makes vibrations in the air. The other says no, because no one hears it.

The foundational skill of rationality ``might be'' the ability to spot in your own head whether you have a map of something or not. The test is to ask what each side expects to experience. Would the two arguers expect to see the tree fallen in different places, hear anything different on a recorder left beside it, or see a different trace on a brain monitor? No. So their maps of the world do not differ. \nb{They do disagree about something: what the word ``sound'' means. The post treats an argument about a word as the same failure as a belief with no content, until near the end, where it separates the two.}

It would be tempting to allow only beliefs about direct sensations. But atoms and floors are real, though we never sense them directly. We see light from the floor, processed by the eye and brain. Inferring the floor is a step back to an unseen cause, however short the step.

Then an example. You drop a bowling ball from the top of a building and ask on which tick of the clock you will hear it land. To answer, you need two beliefs: that gravity is 9.8 meters per second per second, and that the building is about 120 meters tall. These are sentences, not anticipations of any sensation. Together, though, they predict one. If the second hand is on the 12 when you drop the ball, you expect it on the 1 when you hear the crash. To anticipate experience precisely, we must use beliefs that are not themselves anticipations.

Humans can model what they cannot see, and can also believe in what is not there. My example is the alchemists' belief that phlogiston caused fire. It predicted nothing in advance. The link from phlogiston to the fire was always drawn after the fire, never before. \nb{Phlogiston was a theory of chemists, not alchemists.}

Next, an English professor tells you that a writer called Wulky Wilkinsen is a ``retropositional author,'' because his books show ``alienated resublimation,'' and each term is defined only by the other. What should you expect from his books? ``Nothing,'' I answer. But you had better remember that he has both attributes, so you can repeat them on the quiz. I made up both terms.

I call beliefs that are connected only to each other ``floating'' beliefs, and I call them, with no evidence, ``a uniquely human flaw among animal species.''

Empiricism means constantly asking what experiences a belief predicts or, better, forbids. My example of a belief that forbids nothing is élan vital, a life force that nobody believes in any more. If you cannot name anything a belief rules out, it permits anything to happen to you, and it floats. \nb{The post's examples are phlogiston, élan vital, a made-up author and the tree. It does not apply the test to any belief that anyone holds today.}

If you cannot find a difference in what the two sides expect, you are ``probably'' arguing either about labels or, worse, about floating beliefs.

Above all, do not ask what to believe; ask what to anticipate. ``Every question of belief should flow from a question of anticipation.'' \nb{Not every claim in mathematics or ethics predicts a sensation, and the bowling ball showed that beliefs predict in groups.} And: ``If a belief turns deadbeat, evict it.''
