\honest{Two Cult Koans}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2007}

I tell two stories about Ougi, a master of rationality, in the manner of Zen koans.

In the first, a friend tells a novice that all his listening to his master and talk of ``rational'' this and that means he has ``fallen into a cult.'' The novice lies awake, then asks Ougi whether the teachings are ``only dogma.'' Ougi answers with a riddle: if you keep a hammer and drive nails with it, ``who can doubt its worth?'' The novice complains about ``mysterious Zen replies.'' Ougi promises plain arguments if the novice will first wear a huge cowboy hat, and the novice puts it on.

Then the plain version. How long will you repeat my words and ignore their meaning? Disordered thoughts begin as attachment to preferred conclusions, and the novice is too anxious about his image as a rationalist. He came for reassurance; if he had been truly curious, he would have thought of ways to settle his doubts. His need for reassurance made him willing to wear the hat, and ``If I had been an evil man, I could have made you pay a hundred silver coins.'' Work on a real question, and the worth of your understanding will show. Stop glancing away to see who is laughing. The novice later becomes a master and forbids his students to quote him: ``Use the techniques and do not mention them.'' \nb{Nine days later, in ``Cultish Countercultishness,'' Yudkowsky states this lesson in plain prose, with the same swordsman.}

In the second, a novice says the dojo seems ``a little cultish,'' what with the robes and the hood, like ``the bloody Freemasons.'' It seems ``terribly irrational.'' Ougi hands him a wizard's hat: since he is ``so concerned about the interactions of clothing with probability theory,'' he must need a special hat to understand. \nb{The novice's worry was about cultishness. ``Every Cause Wants To Be A Cult,'' nine days earlier, traced cultishness to ordinary in-group feeling, not to faulty reasoning. The master answers as if the worry were about reasoning, and does not ask whether shared robes feed that feeling.}

That novice took the name Bouzo and would discuss rationality only in a clown suit. The story ends there, without comment.
