\honest{Science Doesn't Trust Your Rationality}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2008}

Scott Aaronson compared the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics with libertarianism: both start from premises most educated people accept, and reach conclusions most of them reject. I quote that much. \nb{In the same post Aaronson listed cases where very smart people reasoned their way to false conclusions, and said that in those cases you ``still would've been wise to doubt their conclusions'' even without spotting a flaw. The post leaves that out.}

I have argued before that Science rejects many-worlds and Bayes accepts it. ``Science'' has a capital S here: it means ``the idealized form of Science, not just the actual social process of science.'' \nb{The post describes that ideal itself, so no scientist's practice can easily contradict what it says Science says.}

My own analogy is between libertarianism and Science. Both, ``it furthermore seems to me,'' distrust reasonable-sounding arguments, try to build systems more trustworthy than their members, and use human flaws to drive the system.

Then a paragraph of politics. When a person or group gets enough power to do everything they think is a good idea, ``history says'' you get Revolutionary France or Soviet Russia. So regulate as little as possible, because you cannot trust lovely theories. Markets, I add, turn selfishness into production ``With violence restrained and contracts enforced.'' As for market failures, ``I'm not going to go into'' them. The argument, I say, is not that libertarianism works in a perfect world, but that it ``degrades gracefully into real life.''

Science, I say, began as a rebellion against trusting Aristotle, and it lasted because its founders said, ``Let us trust no one! Not even ourselves!'' We do not try to choose impartial judges, I say. \nb{Journals appoint editors and referees for exactly that purpose.}

Instead, Science harnesses each scientist's stubborn wish to prove his own theory: make a new prediction, do the experiment, and if the result is replicated, you win. \nb{The comparison of science with a market is Michael Polanyi's, from 1962, and Philip Kitcher had argued in 1990 that scientists' baser motives can serve their community. A scientist who wants to prove a theory is also motivated to design experiments that the theory will pass, as the post admits a few paragraphs later.}

The system, I say, is ``somewhat more trustworthy than its parts.'' Both systems secretly rely on most people being decent, like tipping at a restaurant you will never visit again.

Then a rule. Successful predictions belong to the theory that made them first, and a later theory that makes no new prediction cannot take them. This is ``an important feature.'' \nb{The day before, in ``The Dilemma: Science or Bayes?'', Yudkowsky had written that many-worlds ``doesn't seem to make any new predictions relative to the old theory.'' By this rule, Science is right to withhold its credit.}

So Science, I say, ``is not easily reconciled with probability theory.'' A correct probability calculation, I say, gives ``the rational answer.'' Letting Bayes override Science ``is not a trivial step!'' Science assumes that you are ``too stupid and self-deceiving'' to reason perfectly on your own.

I end: ``So, are you going to believe in faster-than-light quantum `collapse' fairies after all? Or do you think you're smarter than that?'' \nb{The essay has just explained why Science is right not to trust how smart you think you are.}
