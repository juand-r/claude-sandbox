\honest{Dissolving the Question}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2008}

Earlier, I say, I did not answer the riddle of the falling tree. I explained how the mind processes words, and I hope that at the end ``there was no question left---not even the feeling of a question.''

Many philosophers, I say, especially amateur and ancient ones, share ``a dangerous instinct: If you give them a question, they try to answer it.'' Some weigh the arguments about free will and publish a verdict. Wiser ones define the term first. Wiser still, they argue that the idea is self-contradictory or meaningless, and publish that. I name no philosopher. \nb{Wittgenstein wrote that the clarity he aimed at meant that philosophical problems ``should completely disappear.''}

Then I point out that people's feeling of free will is a fact about human minds, and that rejecting the idea does not explain the feeling. And mistakes reveal how a mind works.

My explanation is a network with a central unit that stays undecided even after every observable feature is known. That unit, I say, feels like an unanswered question. I add: ``(Metaphorically speaking. Human neurobiology is surely far more complex.)'' I give no evidence that brains contain such a unit.

Once you understand ``in detail'' how your brain produces the feeling of the question, ``then you're done,'' with no lingering confusion. Any vague dissatisfaction is a warning that you are not done.

An evolutionary story, such as that believers in free will outbred other hunter-gatherers, only explains why people have the belief, not how. I call my example of such a story ``a completely bogus explanation.'' My central unit is also a story about mechanism, told without evidence.

Do not stop too early, I say: ``at least 20\%'' of a skilled rationalist's effectiveness comes from that. I give no basis for the figure.

When you are really done, ``you'll know you're done.'' It is ``an unmistakable feeling.'' Then: ``When you're done, you'll know you're done, but unfortunately the reverse implication does not hold.'' So the feeling can mislead you, and it is the only test I give.

The homework: describe the mental algorithm that produces the debate about free will. I spend four paragraphs on what the homework is not. Do not argue that people have free will or that they do not, and do not argue ``that free will is compatible with determinism, or not.'' \nb{Three months later, in ``Timeless Control,'' Yudkowsky gave his own answer, and it argued that determinism is compatible with real control: one side of the debate he had put out of bounds.}
