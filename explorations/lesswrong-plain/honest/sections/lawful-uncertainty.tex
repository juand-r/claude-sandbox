\honest{Lawful Uncertainty}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2008}

I quote Robyn Dawes on an old kind of experiment. Seventy per cent of the cards are blue, in random order. Always predicting blue is right 70\% of the time. Subjects instead predict blue about 70\% of the time, which is right only 58\% of the time (right 70\% of the time on blue cards and 30\% on red, so .7 × .7 + .3 × .3). Paid a nickel per correct guess over a thousand trials, they predicted blue 76\% of the time. \nb{The arithmetic is right. The 76\% could not be checked here.}

This ``compactly illustrates the most important idea in all of rationality.'' I do not argue that it is the most important.

Subjects ``just keep guessing red.'' Dawes says that despite a thousand trials of feedback they cannot believe they cannot predict. They could have bet blue every time and tested their hunches in private, switching only if a hunch came true. I would not fault them for inventing hypotheses, but I would for betting on them after hundreds had failed.

I suspect that the all-blue strategy ``just didn't occur'' to them. Seeing mostly blue cards with some red, people assume the best bets must be mostly blue with some red. \nb{Experiments by Koehler and James, published in 2010, support this suspicion: most people prefer always betting on the likelier option once both strategies are described to them.}

Three times I call something ``a counterintuitive idea'': that the best strategy does not look like the cards, that one should behave lawfully amid randomness, and that one should think lawfully under uncertainty. It seems your behaviour should be as unpredictable as the world, but ``A random key does not open a random lock just because they are `both random.'\,'' You fight fire with water, not fire, and that takes an extra step of thought. On every round, your partial knowledge says blue is the better bet. Bet red every round and you are 100\% stupid. Bet red 30\% of the time and you are 30\% stupid. ``Randomizing your actions takes you further from the target, not closer.'' \nb{Two days later, in ``The Weighted Majority Algorithm,'' Yudkowsky gave the exception: against an opponent who can predict your method, ``it is wise to choose randomly.'' That post is not in this book.}

``And so there are not many rationalists, for most who perceive a chaotic world will try to fight chaos with chaos.'' \nb{The evidence is the card task, and the behaviour there gives way to practice and pay. In a 1961 study of 1000 trials, subjects ended up choosing the likelier option 83\% of the time. In experiments by Shanks and colleagues in 2002, about 70\% of subjects given large payments, regular feedback and long training came to choose it on every trial for at least fifty trials in a row.}

Some say, ``Rationality works fine for dealing with rational people, but the world isn't rational.'' But against an irrational opponent, throwing away your own reason does not help. Decision theory still finds the best response. Every bet on red is an expected loss, ``and so too with every departure from the Way in your own thinking.'' I end by asking how many \textsc{Star Trek} episodes, and how many theories of AI, this refutes.
