\honest{What Do We Mean By ``Rationality''?}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2009}

I mean two things by rationality. ``Epistemic rationality'' is making your beliefs more accurate. ``Instrumental rationality'' is getting what you value, which I sometimes call ``winning.'' I give no source for the pair. \nb{It is standard in philosophy.} The obvious question is what to do when a false belief would get you what you value. I do not ask it.

In a parenthesis I add that truth does not mean certainty. We can only make our beliefs more probably accurate.

Then I raise an objection myself. People say ``X is rational!'' when they mean ``I think X is true'' or ``I think X is good.'' So why have the word at all? My answer is that we need it for general statements, and my example is that rational agents maximize expected utility. \nb{That is not a fact that anyone discovered about rational agents. It is how decision theorists define a rational agent.}

Next, the psychologists. People rate ``Bill is an accountant who plays jazz'' as more likely than ``Bill plays jazz.'' I leave out the description of Bill that the subjects were given. \nb{That description is what made ``accountant'' tempting, so without it the subjects look stranger than they were.} I say the judgment breaks a law of probability.

Then two admissions. First, no one can actually calculate and obey the mathematics I have just named as the gold standard. So the gold standard is not something a person can follow, and I do not say what a person should follow instead. I say there is a further art, and list its parts: learn your flaws, overcome your biases, get into good emotional shape, ``et cetera, et cetera.'' Second, the mathematics itself is disputed in some puzzles, such as Newcomb's problem.

I say that it is futile to settle a dispute by defining ``rational'' so that your answer wins. Four paragraphs later I do exactly that. If you say ``It's (epistemically) rational for me to believe X, but the truth is Y,'' I say you are probably using the word to mean something else. But I have already said that truth is not certainty. So a well-reasoned belief can turn out false, and saying so is not a confusion about words. I rule it out by definition. I do the same for action: if the rational thing and the right thing differ, you are almost certainly using one of the two words in some other sense.

Then I admit that I use the word ``normatively, to pick out desirable patterns of thought.'' So ``rational'' means good thinking. At the start I observed that people often use ``rational'' as a more strident way of saying ``true'' or ``good.'' I now use it that way myself.

As an example of plain and specific language, I offer this: causal decision theory says you should take both boxes in Newcomb's problem, ``but I'd rather have a million dollars.'' That presents taking one box as plain common sense. It is one side of a dispute that my own cited source, an article by Jim Holt, calls unsettled. \nb{In the 2020 PhilPapers survey, more philosophers chose two boxes than one.} I define rationality as winning, and then I say which choice wins.

I suggest that you replace ``rational'' with ``foozal'' throughout the essay and see whether the connotations change. \nb{It is a good test.} I do not apply it to ``rationalist,'' the name I give my readers in other posts.

I end: do not overuse the word. ``One receives no points merely for pronouncing it loudly.''
