\honest{What Do We Mean By ``Rationality''?}{Eliezer Yudkowsky}{2009}

I mean two things by rationality. ``Epistemic rationality'' is making your beliefs more accurate. You look around a room and place the bookcase against the wall; if your eyes or brain go wrong, your model shows a bookcase where there is none, and you are disappointed when you go for a book. A belief is a map, and its correspondence with the territory is what I call truth. ``Instrumental rationality'' is steering the future toward outcomes you rank higher, which I sometimes call ``winning.'' Winning does not mean winning at others' expense, since what you value includes other people. I give no source for the pair. \nb{It is standard in philosophy. The post does not ask the obvious question about it: what to do when a false belief would get you what you value.}

(Truth does not mean certainty. We can only make our beliefs more probably accurate.)

Then I raise an objection myself. People say ``X is rational!'' when they mean ``I think X is true'' or ``I think X is good.'' So why have the word at all? My answer is an analogy with ``true.'' We never need to say ``it is true that snow is white,'' but we need the word for general statements such as ``True models usually produce better experimental predictions than false models.'' In the same way, ``It's rational to eat vegetables'' just means useful, but ``Rational agents make decisions that maximize the probabilistic expectation of a coherent utility function'' needs the concept.

Next, the psychologists. People rate ``Bill is an accountant who plays jazz'' as more likely than ``Bill plays jazz.'' I leave out the description of Bill that the subjects were given, and point back to earlier essays for it. \nb{That description is what made ``accountant'' tempting, so without it the subjects look stranger than they were.} The judgment breaks a law of probability. The ``gold standards'' are probability theory and decision theory. Probability theory governs belief the same way whether you are locating a bookcase or estimating the hairs on Julius Caesar's head, and decision theory governs action whatever your goals. Beliefs that fit a coherent probability distribution, and choices that maximize the expected value of a coherent utility function, are called Bayesian. That is not acting like Mr.\ Spock, which does not help ``one hair''; a footnote extends rationality to ``urges, hunches, perceptions, and wordless intuitions.''

Then two admissions. First, no one can actually calculate and obey the mathematics I have just named as the gold standard. So the gold standard is not something a person can follow. There is a further art, and I list its parts: learn your flaws, overcome your biases, get into good emotional shape, ``et cetera, et cetera.'' Second, the mathematics itself is disputed in some puzzles: the rules of probability, in problems where the number of observers is uncertain, and the rules of decision theory, in problems such as Newcomb's, where another agent predicts your decision before you make it. Where the formal tools fall short we can fall back on plain ``truth'' and ``winning.'' A scientist first studying fire does better to point at a campfire and say ``that orangey-bright hot stuff'' than to define fire by phlogiston, and a bias hits no less hard because we cannot define irrationality.

It is futile to settle a dispute by defining ``rational'' so that your answer wins; that only raises the question why anyone should accept your definition. I care about Bayesian updating, with a prior that favors simple hypotheses, not because Laplace handed it down but because I expect it to bring beliefs closer to accuracy. Words exist to carry thoughts from one mind to another. If you understand the concept I am getting at, the question of definition is finished, and what is left is what counts as a good way to think. If you say ``It's (epistemically) rational for me to believe X, but the truth is Y,'' you are probably using the word to mean something else: rationality should be consistent under reflection, so that looking rationally at the evidence and looking rationally at how your mind handles the evidence give the same answer. I do the same for action: if the rational thing and the right thing differ, you are almost certainly using one of the two words in some other sense.

I use the word ``normatively, to pick out desirable patterns of thought''; that is, ``rational'' means good thinking.

Where people disagree about the word, use more specific language: ``The self-benefiting thing to do is to run away, but I hope I would at least try to drag the child off the railroad tracks.'' Or: causal decision theory says you should take both boxes in Newcomb's problem, ``but I'd rather have a million dollars.'' \nb{The example takes a side. A few paragraphs earlier the post listed Newcomb's problem among the puzzles where the mathematics itself is disputed, and in the 2020 PhilPapers survey more philosophers chose two boxes than one.}

I suggest that you replace ``rational'' with ``foozal'' throughout the essay and see whether the connotations change.

Do not overuse the word. ``One receives no points merely for pronouncing it loudly,'' and if you speak too much of the Way, you will not reach it.
