# Report: mysterious-answers-to-mysterious-questions

## 1. The argument in three sentences

Vitalism, illustrated by three passages from Lord Kelvin, answered the mystery of life with
a mysterious substance, "Élan vital", which, like phlogiston and Molière's "dormitive
potency", predicts nothing and only stops curiosity; its holders also revered their
ignorance. The deeper error is to think that an answer can be mysterious: mystery is a fact
about a mind's state of knowledge (the map), not about the phenomenon (the territory), which
is why mysterious questions keep turning out to have non-mysterious answers. The post names
such theories "mysterious answers to mysterious questions" and lists four signs: the
explanation stops curiosity, has no moving parts, is cherished by its holders, and leaves the
phenomenon as mysterious as before.

### Kelvin passage 1 (post's footnote 1)

"On the Dissipation of Energy", article in The Fortnightly Review, March 1892 (so stated in the
volume's contents: "Being article in "The Fortnightly Review," March, 1892."), reprinted in
Popular Lectures and Addresses, vol. II, pp. 451ff. Read at
https://archive.org/download/popularlecturesa02kelvuoft/popularlecturesa02kelvuoft_djvu.txt
(`kelvin_popularlecturesa02kelvuoft.txt`; two other scans, `kelvin_popularlectures00kelvgoog.txt`
and `kelvin_popularlectures07kelvgoog.txt`, agree). Passage on pp. 462 to 464:

> It seemed to me then, and it still seems to me, most probable that the animal body does not
> act as a thermodynamic engine in converting heat produced by the combination of the food with
> the oxygen of the inhaled air, but that it acts in a manner more nearly analogous to that of
> an electric motor working in virtue of energy supplied to it by a voltaic battery. According
> to either view, however, the mechanical effect achieved by an animal in walking up-hill [...]
> is a part equivalent for the oxidation of the food; the rest of the equivalent being animal
> heat. Joule estimated [...] It is, however, conceivable that animal life might have the
> attribute of using the heat of surrounding matter, at its natural temperature, as a source of
> energy for mechanical effect, and thus constituting a case of affirmative answer for Carnot's
> last thermodynamic question. The influence of animal or vegetable life on matter[footnote] is
> infinitely beyond the range of any scientific inquiry hitherto entered on. Its power of
> directing the motions of moving particles, in the demonstrated daily miracle of our human
> free-will, and in the growth of generation after generation of plants from a single seed, are
> infinitely different from any possible result of the fortuitous concourse of atoms; and the
> fortuitous concourse of atoms is the sole foundation in Philosophy on which can be founded the
> doctrine that it is impossible to derive mechanical effect from heat otherwise than by [...]

The post's words match; the ellipsis replaces the text from "in converting heat" to
"thermodynamic question." Footnote on "matter": "About twenty-five years ago, I asked Liebig if
he believed that a leaf or a flower could be formed or could grow by chemical forces. He
answered, "I would more readily believe that a book on chemistry or on botany could grow out of
dead matter by chemical processes."" Page 465: "But the real phenomena of life infinitely
transcend human science: and speculation regarding consequences of their imagined reversal is
utterly unprofitable."

### Kelvin passage 2 (post's footnote 2)

Read as reprinted in Sir William Thomson, Mathematical and Physical Papers, vol. I (Cambridge,
1882), Art. LVIII, pp. 505 to 510, footnote: "From the Proceedings of the Royal Society of
Edinburgh, February, 1852."
https://archive.org/download/mathematicalphys01kelv/mathematicalphys01kelv_djvu.txt
(`kelvin_mpp_mathematicalphys01kelv.txt`; two further scans agree). I did not see the Proc. RSE
printing itself. Matched (p. 507): "The question, "Can animated creatures set matter in motion in
virtue of an inherent power of producing a mechanical effect?" must be answered in the negative,
according to the well-established theory of animal heat and motion, which ascribes them to the
chemical action (principally oxidation, or a combustion at low temperatures) experienced by the
food." (p. 508): "A curious inference is pointed out, that an animal would be sensibly less warm
in going up-hill than in going down-hill, were the breathing not greater in the former case than
in the latter." (pp. 508 to 509): "Certainty regarding the means in the animal body by which
external mechanical effects are produced from chemical forces acting internally, cannot be
arrived at without more experiment [...]. Whatever be the nature of these means, consciousness
teaches every individual that they are, to some extent, subject to the direction of his will. It
appears, therefore, that animated creatures have the power of immediately applying, to certain
moving particles of matter within their bodies, forces by which the motions of these particles
are directed to produce desired mechanical effects." The post's words match; it drops
"Whatever be the nature of these means,", which is what "they" refers to.

### Kelvin passage 3 (post's footnote 3)

Silvanus P. Thompson, The Life of William Thomson, Baron Kelvin of Largs, vol. 2 (Macmillan,
1910), https://archive.org/download/lifeofwilliamtho02thomuoft/lifeofwilliamtho02thomuoft_djvu.txt
(`thompson_lifeofwilliamtho02thomuoft.txt`). Pp. 1097 to 1098: "A course of lectures on
"Christian Apologetics" was given at University College, London, in May 1903, the first of the
course being on Present Day Rationalism, by the Rev. Professor Henslow. A vote of thanks was moved
by Lord Kelvin [...] The following is The Times report, as corrected by Lord Kelvin's own hand"
... "Modern biologists are coming once more to a firm acceptance of something beyond mere
gravitational, chemical, and physical forces; and that unknown thing is a vital principle." Exact
match. Kelvin's letter of "May 2 [1903]" refers to "yesterday evening", hence 1 May. P. 1101:
"Sir E. Ray Lankester denied the statement that any modern biologist showed signs of coming to a
belief in the existence of a vital principle." P. 1094: "his doctrine had little in common with
the old crude vitalism which postulated a vital force as amongst the natural forces, so-called,
which operate on matter. [...] No supposed vital force could maintain the animal heat [...] True,
the body did not work as a thermodynamic engine, it more nearly resembled an electric motor [...]
But all this required only that some vital principle should direct or organize those
transformations of energy which would otherwise be conducted as a purely inorganic, that is,
unorganized process". The post cites the 2005 AMS reprint of this book.

## 6. Judgment calls for the editor

1. The first-passage cfact says "Cut, the sentence can read as a denial that the body works like
   a machine". This describes the effect of the cut; "can" is deliberate. I avoided "misquotes":
   the ellipsis is marked, and the post's words are Kelvin's.
2. The vitalism cpara says, on the authority of Thompson, that the theory the post defines "was
   not Kelvin's theory". Kelvin did assert a "vital principle" and "Directive Power", so he was a
   vitalist of a kind; the note limits itself to Thompson's contrast with "the old crude vitalism
   which postulated a vital force". A defender might say the post's "Élan vital ... caused it to
   move as consciously directed" fits Kelvin's directing will. That is partly true; the note's
   point is about vital force as a source of heat and motion, which Kelvin denied.
3. The Bergson/Élan vital cfact may be judged a naming nitpick (STANDARDS 2.4(2)). I kept it
   because the post attributes to "Élan vital" a chemical role and a date ("Wöhler's later
   synthesis") that do not fit the term's origin. Cut it if the editor disagrees; the Response
   mentions it in one clause.
4. The "equally good at explaining all outcomes" clogic uses the post's own description of
   vitalism against the post's claim. I used "sits uneasily with" in the Response rather than
   "contradicts": a defender could say the chemical prohibition was a separate auxiliary claim,
   not the "Why? Élan vital!" answer; but the post itself makes the prohibition part of what
   "Élan vital" does.
5. The Jaynes cpara is an "old idea without credit" point. The post does not claim novelty. I
   limited the note to saying the author later credited the idea, with the matched sentence.
6. I checked and dropped a planned note that the post's "infinitely beyond the reach of
   science" drops Kelvin's "hitherto entered on": Kelvin wrote a page later that life
   "infinitely transcend[s] human science", so the post's paraphrase is fair. The cpara on that
   paragraph now says so.
7. Reserved words: none. "Fair" once, as credit (Kelvin's phrase).
