# Report: to-spread-science-keep-it-secret

## 1. The argument in three sentences

Because of the scarcity effect, people value what seems forbidden, and especially secret
information; the author thinks people ignore science because it seems freely available and
so unimportant, and join cults that keep their truths secret. If science were guarded by
initiation rituals, people would try hard to get in, be satisfied when they did, and laugh
at rival cults; it might also be more fun. The proposal is disclaimed as not serious; the
real advice is to imagine science as secret knowledge, so as not to undervalue it for
being unprotected, while demanding data from any group that does use robes and masks.

## 6. Judgment calls for the editor

1. Genre: the post is judged as a partly joking proposal. Notes on the jokes (Purloined
   Letter, hot coals, Scientology, $100,000) only explain them. The critical notes are on the
   one claim the post makes seriously ("I think" people assume free information is
   unimportant) and on its support.
2. The objection paragraph: my first draft said the answer "restates the claim". On
   rereading, the objection in the post is "why doesn't difficulty count as scarcity?", and
   the answer (scarcity works on appearances) fits the scarcity effect. The note now says so,
   and makes the narrower point that difficulty and cost, which the post itself grants, are
   plain reasons not to study science that the post does not weigh against its own. These are
   the post's own premises, so I do not count this as an unsourced alternative explanation;
   the editor may disagree.
3. Aronson and Mills is given as support for the post (Book II lesson: prior work that
   supports the post is credit). It concerns liking for a group after joining, not wanting to
   get in; the note says only "satisfaction".
4. Pythagoras: the note does not fault the joke; it tells the reader how far Pythagorean
   secrecy is attested.
5. Reserved words: "never" and "false" only paraphrase the post.
6. Considered and left out: "People seem to have holes in their minds" sits beside "Is
   Humanism", which called the assumption that nonbelievers have "a hole in their mind that
   wants filling" the view of "gloating religionists". The holes differ (esoteric knowledge
   vs religion) and the post hedges with "seem"; a nitpick. Also left out: The Sacred
   Mundane (placed just before) celebrates "the sacred mundane", while this post calls
   science's presentation "false mundanity"; the two uses of "mundane" differ in sense.
7. Consistency with the Scarcity afterword (written in parallel by another agent, read at the
   end, not edited): it reports that Cialdini's first cause of the scarcity effect is a
   shortcut ("things that are hard to get are typically better"), and that censorship by an
   attractive expert "did not raise interest" in a disliked speech. Neither conflicts with
   my note, which describes only the studies the Scarcity post reports. The Cialdini shortcut
   is the same inference as this post's "if the information is freely available, it must not
   be important"; I have not read Cialdini, so I did not add it as credit. The editor may
   want a pointer.
