# Report: absolute-authority

## 1. The argument in three sentences

People who treat knowledge as a matter of authority read science's admission that it may
be wrong as a confession that it cannot be trusted; the author guesses (with "I suspect",
"I fear") that the habit comes from religion and from school, and offers four short lines
for a public debate. In private, the first step is to see that one can make moral and
factual distinctions without certainty: the "Argument from the Argument from Gray" (without
certainty the moral relativists win) fails because certainty is sufficient but not necessary
for choosing, and because the relativist chain breaks where all grays become one shade.
The learner also needs a culture where admitting error is not shameful, the habit of looking
rather than being preached at, and probability as calibration: people's "absolute"
certainties are often wrong, 99.9999% is already one error in a million statements, and
probability 1 is infinite certainty, so scientists might say "not infinitely certain".

## 3. Arithmetic

- Counts: "unenlightened ones" 2 times, "not-fully-enlightened ones" 1 (grep). "gravity"
  occurs once in the post (first paragraph only), basis for "gravity is not mentioned again".
- 99.9999% = one expected error in 10^6 statements: 10^6 x 10^-6 = 1. "A solid year or so":
  with Infinite Certainty's assumptions (one statement every 20 s, 16 h a day),
  16 x 3600 / 20 = 2,880 a day; 10^6 / 2,880 = 347 days. Holds. (Independence is not needed
  for the expected count; no note, nitpick.)
- "Thou shalt not win the lottery" at 99.9999%: a 2008 US jackpot at odds of about 1 in
  146-176 million gives P(not win) = 1 - 6e-9, above 0.999999. Holds for the jackpot; not
  checked against a source, no note.

## 6. Judgment calls

- First \cpara and \clogic: "does not take up the argument from history". The fair defender
  would say the opponent's line also says "you can't know for certain", and the post answers
  that. The note says so. The first debating line ("The power of science comes from having
  the ability to change our minds") touches the history, but as a virtue, not as a reply to
  the inference; I judged "does not take up" accurate. Editor should check.
- \cstyle on "the unenlightened ones": a tone note. It admits the irony. Cut if the editor
  thinks it a jab (STANDARDS 2.2.9); I kept it because the post's own subject is how to
  teach these people.
- Priors \cpara: "does not engage it". The post does state the worry (someone might argue
  with your prior) and mocks it with "gasp!"; the note quotes that. It is a parenthesis
  "in passing", which the note also says.
- "Reversed stupidity" \cpara: says the post's wording is loose but its diagnosis right. Low
  stakes; could be cut to its first sentence.
- Non-monotonic \clogic: the evidence (stated odds) is not the post's variable (emotional
  commitment); the note says so explicitly and uses "does not support", not "contradicts".
- Reserved words: "never" appears only in "gravity never comes up again" (Response; checked:
  one occurrence of "gravity" in the post) and in a quotation of the post ("never admits to
  error"). "wrong" in the Summary is the post's own sense.
