# Report: correspondence-bias ("Correspondence Bias", Yudkowsky, posted 2007-06-25)

## 1. The argument in three sentences (written before annotating)

People explain others' behaviour by matching dispositions ("an angry person") and their own by the situation, even when told that the situation explains it (Jones and Harris), although prior probability favours ordinary situations over rare unusual dispositions, and the author suggests essences are easier to think of than mechanisms. So to understand people, assume they see themselves as behaving normally and ask what situation they believe they are in. The author applies this to people who ask why the author wants to save the world: given the author's beliefs, the reaction is ordinary, and even people who do terrible things are not exceptional mutants.

## 3. Arithmetic

- Malle published November 2006; post 2007-06-25: seven months.
- "two to ten somatically expressed mutations" (hedged "If I recall correctly"): not recomputed. "Somatically expressed" is not a standard term. Current estimates put new (de novo) point mutations per person at several dozen, of which roughly one or two fall in coding sequence; I did not fetch a source, so nothing about this is in the notes. The post's point (any given DNA location is unlikely to be hit) does not depend on the number.

## 5. Items not verified

- Jones and Harris (1967) itself, including how the side was assigned (debate coach vs coin toss) and whether any condition used speeches rather than essays (the post says "speaker ... speech"; G&M say essays).
- The drinking example ("Drinking students considerably overestimate ... nondrinkers considerably underestimate") has no source in the post. The college-drinking literature I glimpsed in search results reports that students in general overestimate peers' drinking, which may sit uneasily with "nondrinkers ... underestimate", but I did not read a source that measures abstainers' estimates of the fraction who drink, so the note says only "given without a source".

## 6. Judgment calls for the editor

- The Malle note is the strongest charge. It is scoped: "Stated as a general rule"; it reports that the asymmetry held for negative events, which covers the post's own example. The Response says "the example survives; the general rule is not shown".
- The prior-probability clogic ("set against an extreme disposition"). A defender could say the post's observer infers a "naturally angry person" who "lashed out without any provocation", i.e. an extreme. The note says only that the milder reading exists and the late-bus comparison does not settle it. Cut if it seems speculative; it is a logical point, not an unsourced empirical one.
- The Castro topic note may count as a nitpick (STANDARDS 2.4(2)); the argument does not depend on the topic. I kept it because it misdescribes the post's one cited experiment, and the note also records the coin-toss vs debate-coach discrepancy.
- The essences-cause note says the cause "is not among" G&M's four. That is my reading of the four mechanisms (lack of awareness, unrealistic expectations, inflated categorizations, incomplete corrections); none is "essences are more available than mechanisms".
- "false" appears only in "false consensus" (a term).
