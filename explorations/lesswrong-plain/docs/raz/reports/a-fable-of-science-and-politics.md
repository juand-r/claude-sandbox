# Report: a-fable-of-science-and-politics

## 1. The argument in three sentences

The Blues and Greens of the Roman Empire, who killed each other for generations, were
chariot-racing fans, so factions can grow hostile over almost nothing. In an invented
underground society, two factions formed over whether "cerulean" means blue or green, and
each has since collected a package of positions on taxes, marriage and cosmology. When one
member of a tourist party reaches the surface and sees a blue sky, the fable gives six
possible reactions (triumph, despair, suppression, painful conversion, cynicism, curiosity)
and, without stating a moral, presents the last one, delight in finding out, as the right one.

## 3. Arithmetic

None. The only count in a note: six branches (Aditya, Barron, Charles, Daria, Eddin, Ferris),
counted. "Each side is right once" on cosmology: Blues say spherical (right) and at the centre
(wrong); Greens say flat (wrong) and circling a Sun (right).

## 6. Judgment calls

- The paragraph-2 note calls "They were sports fans" "one side of a debate", on the strength of
  a Wikipedia article and Gibbon's next sentence. The note also credits Procopius's support. The
  Response repeats this in brief. Editor may judge this a quibble with a fable's framing; I kept
  it because the opening paragraph is presented as history, not fiction.
- The Ferris note and the Response say the model response "is given to the one character with
  no side to lose" and that the fable "does not show how a partisan could reach it". This is an
  observation about the text (Ferris has no faction or pronoun; Daria is the only partisan who
  changes), not a motive reading. The claim that Ferris is the model response rests on the
  book's own later text.
- The Response's point that the sky settles facts but not the value questions in the packages
  (taxes, marriage) is my main criticism. A defender could say the fable is about reactions to
  truth, not about politics in general; the Response says exactly that the fable is narrower
  than its title, and does not claim the fable says otherwise.
- Reserved word "wrong" appears only in descriptions of the characters ("accepts that she was
  wrong") and "does not say whether hiding the sky is wrong". Pronoun flags are for characters
  and Procopius only.
- No sentence-level notes: I found nothing in the fable proper that a fair note could fault.
