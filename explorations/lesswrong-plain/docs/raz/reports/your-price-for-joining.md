# Report: your-price-for-joining ("Your Price for Joining", Yudkowsky, posted 2009-03-26)

## 1. The argument in three sentences (written before annotating)

In the Ultimatum Game people reject low offers even at real cost, and the post suggests an
analogue in joining group projects: a would-be member withholds support until the group meets
some demand, and people in the "atheist/libertarian/technophile/sf-fan" cluster, it seems to the
author, set that price far too high. The post's explanation is hunter-gatherer instinct tuned to
bands of about 40, which underestimates the inertia of large groups, large strategies and
unseen past negotiations, plus akrasia and a wish for status. Its remedy is a rule of thumb
and group norm: if joining still does net good and the problem is not worth fixing yourself,
join anyway; withhold only when the group blocks a competent person from fixing it, while
conceding that some pressure on a project's leadership is useful.

## 2. Sources checked (outside quotations and figures)

All saved in `data/sources/6c_your-price-for-joining/`.

- **Wikipedia, "Ultimatum game", revision 274902470 of 2009-03-04** (the last revision before the
  post; the post links the article), via the API (`wp_ultimatum_2009.txt`). Matched: "In many
  [[culture]]s, people offer "fair" (i.e., 50:50) splits, and offers of less than 20% are often
  rejected."; "even in a 100 USD game played in [[Indonesia]], where average 1995 per-capita
  income was 670 USD. Rejections are reportedly independent of the stakes at this level, with 30
  USD offers being turned down in Indonesia, as in the [[United States]], even though this equates
  to two week's wages in Indonesia." It credits "studies by Cameron and Hoffman et al.". The post
  keeps the article's typo "two week's wages". The current article says "less than 30%"
  (`wp_ultimatum_now.txt`); I used the 2009 text.
- **Oosterbeek, Sloof and van de Kuilen (2004)**, Experimental Economics 7(2):171-188, RePEc
  abstract (`oosterbeek.html`). Matched: "We find that on average the proposer offers 40% of the pie
  to the responder."; "On average 16% of the offers is rejected."
- **"About half" of offers below 20% rejected**: not quoted in the note. Secondary source only:
  JEPS article "Fairness or anger in ultimatum game rejections?" (`jeps.html`): "experimenters
  consistently find that offers of under approximately 20% are rejected about 50% of the time, and
  proposers tend to make offers of 40-45%." I could not reach Camerer and Thaler (1995) (Cloudflare

- **Cameron (1999)**, Economic Inquiry 37(1):47-59, RePEc abstract (`cameron_repec.html`). Matched:
  "raise the stakes to three times the monthly expenditure of the average participant";
  "responders become more willing to accept a given percentage offer at higher stakes, but proposer
  behavior is largely invariant to stake changes." Not used in a note (see section 5).
- **Henrich et al. (2005)**, Behav Brain Sci 28(6):795-815, PubMed abstract PMID 16372952
  (`henrich2005_abstract.txt`). Matched: "the canonical model - based on self-interest - fails in all

- **Wikipedia, "Exit, Voice, and Loyalty"** raw (`wp_Exit,_Voice,_and_Loyalty.txt`). Matched: "they
  can ''exit'' (withdraw from the relationship); or, they can ''voice'' (attempt to repair or improve

- Wikipedia, "Holdout problem" (`wp_Holdout_problem.txt`): read and not used (it concerns bond

## 3. Arithmetic

- $30 against $670 a year: 30/670 x 52 = 2.33 weeks, so "two week's wages" is right given the
  article's income figure. The 670 USD figure is the article's; I did not check Indonesia's 1995
  income (World Bank series not fetched). Not noted.
- 50 players x 20% = 1000% of the pot (cpara on the 50-player paragraph: "ten times the pot").
