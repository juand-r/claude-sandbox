# Report: the-futility-of-emergence

## 1. The argument in three sentences

"Emergence", as commonly used ("Intelligence is an emergent phenomenon!"), is a present-day
mysterious answer like phlogiston and vitalism: defined as high-level behaviour arising from
many low-level interactions, it fits every phenomenon above quarks and so explains nothing.
The verb "emerges from" is acceptable when a specific model follows, but the noun used as an
explanation meets every sign of a mysterious answer, as two exercises show: deleting
"emergent" from a sentence changes nothing, and replacing it with "magical" conveys the same
knowledge. The word is popular because it is "the junk food of curiosity", and people with
some science education use it the way their ancestors used magic.

## 6. Judgment calls for the editor

1. Reserved word "false" appears three times in the phrase "a claim that can turn out false"
   (note, Response, n.b.). It describes falsifiability, not a verdict on the post. Replace with
   "can be refuted" if the editor prefers the checker silent.
2. The central criticism (the post tests the loosest definition) must pass fair-defender 6: the
   post says "Taken literally, that description" and later narrows its target to the noun "as
   commonly used". The note begins "True of the one-line summary the post quotes", and the
   Response credits the narrowed point as fair. The criticism that remains is that the exercises
   are presented as showing the word adds nothing, while in the defined senses it adds a claim.
3. "In C. D. Broad's usage (1925), 'Life is an emergent phenomenon' says ... that life needs no
   special substance": Broad did not write that sentence; the note infers it from Broad's
   "Emergent form of Vitalism which does not assume entelechies". Strong emergentism also denied
   deducibility, which the post might count as mysterious; the note does not claim emergentism
   was a good theory, only that the word ruled something out.
4. The Bedau-magic clogic ("The post applies to the word in general a comparison that its own
   source made for one sense"): a defender could say the post never read that section. The note
   claims only that the page it links contained it.
5. "Fair" appears twice as credit (the narrowing; the Response's first line), which STANDARDS 2.1
   allows.
6. I dropped a planned note that "emergent" in condensed-matter physics (Anderson 1972,
   Laughlin and Pines 2000) names specific claims: I had not read those papers in this session.
