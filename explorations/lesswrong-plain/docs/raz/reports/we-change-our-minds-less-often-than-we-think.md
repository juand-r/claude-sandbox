# Report: we-change-our-minds-less-often-than-we-think ("We Change Our Minds Less Often Than We Think", Yudkowsky, posted 2007-10-03)

## 1. The argument in three sentences (written before annotating)

Griffin and Tversky found that colleagues choosing between job offers gave their predicted choice an average probability of only 66%, yet 23 of 24 chose it; the author concludes that once you can guess your answer, you have in all probability already decided, and that this usually happens within half a second of hearing the question. By the principle of the bottom line, only the real causes of a belief or decision matter, so the plan of deciding by non-rational means and then checking the justification fails, because we change our minds much less often than we think. We remember the times we changed our minds and not the times we did not, and a list of biases (hindsight, fake causality, positive bias, anchoring, confirmation bias) means that an idea, once in your head, will probably stay there.

## 3. Arithmetic

- 23/24 = 95.8%, the quoted "96%".
- If the colleagues were calibrated at an average 66% confidence, the expected number choosing the less likely option is 24 x 0.34 = 8.2, "about 8". Observed: 1. (Strictly, calibration is per person; with an average of 66% the expected count is 24 x (1 - 0.66) regardless of spread.)

## 6. Judgment calls for the editor

- The cpara on the second paragraph calls "in all probability, already decided" "the post's inference, not the paper's" and says the authors' account "fits the same numbers with no early decision". A defender could say "already decided" and "a small advantage generally leads to the choice" are the same thing. I think they differ: the paper's account locates the error in the stated probability, not in the decision process, and says nothing about when the choice was fixed. Please check that the note's wording is not stronger than that.
- The clogic "The source does not support the general form" rests on the paper's own sentence about overconfident self-prediction and on the Vallone abstract.
- The last cpara cites belief perseverance as the closest evidence for the post's claim. This is support for the post, not a fault; the note says only that the post does not name it.
- Reserved words: "false" appears only in "false feedback" (the study's design). "never" was in a clogic I later cut as a wording nitpick ("no amount of argument will alter the truth-value").
