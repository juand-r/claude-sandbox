# Report: uncritical-supercriticality

## 1. The argument in three sentences

Arguing whether atheism or Communism is a "religion" loses the real question, which is why
large groups of people have been slaughtered in the name of an idea. The author's suggested
ur-mistake is the rule of Deuteronomy 13, which Stalin and Hitler also set: do not listen to
the critic, destroy him; when it feels wrong to argue against any positive claim about the
Great Idea, the affective death spiral goes supercritical, and this can happen with or
without supernaturalism, so religion is not special enough to be the key problem. Faith
(Sam Harris's target) makes the spiral easy to start, but it turns deadly when criticism
becomes a sin, gaffe or crime, and the one unconditional rule is that no argument is beyond
criticism and bad argument gets counterargument, never violence.

## 3. Arithmetic

- The two definitions: under "a probability below 10% or above 90% for some God counts as
  religious", an atheist who gives Zeus under 10% counts as religious; under "over 90% for some God", Stalin does not. The
  post's conclusion checks.
- "less than 1.0 additional nice-sounding claims, on average": the standard subcritical
  condition; the expected total cascade from one claim is 1/(1-k), finite for k < 1.

## 6. Judgment calls

- \clogic on the ur-mistake: says the evidence shows co-occurrence, not that the rule came first
  and caused the killing, and keeps the hedge ("offers the claim as a suggestion"). The \cpara
  on the thesis paragraph says the hedge is dropped there; the Response makes the same point.
  A defender may answer that the post's claim is "deadlier", a comparative, which does not need
  the rule to come first. I think the charge still holds: "turns much deadlier after criticism
  becomes a sin" is a causal claim, and no comparison is given.
- \cfact on Stalin and Hitler is mainly credit ("bears this out in substance"). I avoided the
  word "invented" (reserved) for the Morozov story and used the source's "fictional".
- The first \cpara: I first wrote that "Purpose and Pragmatism" put the point "more cautiously"
  ("suggests that you may be playing loose with your purposes"). On rereading, that post also
  says the dilemma arises "only when" a question has no use, so the comparison failed the
  fair-defender test (7). The note now just describes the earlier post's example.
- Carrier note: neutral identification, not a charge; the post signals the idea is borrowed
  ("the best distinction I've heard").
- Considered and dropped: a \clogic on "Supernaturalist claims ... always turn out to be wrong"
  (a universal claim resting on the footnote's "See, for example" pointer; an aside, argued
  at length in "Excluding the Supernatural"); the US legal cases that treat atheism as a
  religion for First Amendment purposes as a counterexample to "nearly always" (a different
  setting from the bar-room quarrel; not checked).
- Pronouns: "him"/"his" for Carrier, Morozov, and Deuteronomy's generic tempter.
- Reserved words: "wrong", "false", "never" occur only in paraphrases of the post's own claims.
