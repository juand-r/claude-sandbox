# Report: serious-stories

## 1. The argument in three sentences

Starting from the writing manuals' rule that stories need conflict and pain, and Orwell's
observation that utopias are places no one would want to live, the post considers the
argument that a world without pain would have no stories worth reading and so would not be
worth living in; it grants that a story and a life are optimized by different criteria, but
takes the bad feeling of stories where everything goes right as a reason to ask whether a
life without pain would feel the same. It then argues that human pain is far easier to
produce and far more intense than pleasure, and guesses that pain, hedonic adaptation and
the "new floor" problem could be engineered away, so that David Pearce's abolition of
suffering is "very probably" feasible. Against complete abolition it sets a "path of
courage" (remove the pain that destroys minds, make people stronger, keep lesser sorrows),
admits this appeals to the author partly from conservatism and perhaps from fear, and ends
without deciding: "I don't know."

## 3. Arithmetic

- Loss aversion: the post says 2 to 2.5; the meta-analytic mean is 1.955, 2 per cent below
  the bottom of the range. Under the threshold for a number note; given as support.
- Utility rescaling: for a constant c, E[u(X) + c] = E[u(X)] + c for every lottery X, so the
  ordering of lotteries is unchanged; the same holds for u -> a u + c with a > 0. The post's
  "literally the same utility function, as a mathematical object" is loose (u and u + c are
  different functions representing the same preferences); judged a nitpick, not noted.

## 6. Judgment calls

- The main critical point is the Pearce paragraph (cpara, Response, honest n.b.): "very
  probably right" rests on three stated guesses. The post's hedge "So far as I know or can
  guess" is quoted in the note and the Response. I think the point survives the fair-defender
  test because "very probably" is stronger than the guesses, but the editor should check.
- The Card and Bickham paragraphs: I wrote "I could not find these exact words" and "a close
  paraphrase" rather than "misquotes", since the meaning is the same.
- The great-stories list note: "illustrates the claim rather than tests it" plus Aristotle.
  A defender could say the list was obviously illustrative; the note says so rather than
  criticizing it.
- The loss-aversion clogic ("Stated as fact, without evidence or argument") on "we'd go
  broke": a side remark; the editor may judge it a nitpick.
- The circuits cfact ("the neuroscience is mixed") and the congenital-analgesia cpara are
  information that qualifies two facts the post uses; neither is presented as refuting the
  post. The Response says they "need qualification".
- The "no book is named" in the Elder Author note is descriptive; could be cut.
- The child-abuse phrase in the "broken people" paragraph ("No child sexual abuse that turns
  out more abusers") is a restrictive clause; the cycle-of-abuse evidence point was made in
  Detached Lever Fallacy (batch 5a) and is not repeated here.
- Reserved flag "never" is a paraphrase of the post. Pronouns: Orwell, Aristotle keep theirs;
  "He's likely to act" and "his goal" are in quotations about characters.
