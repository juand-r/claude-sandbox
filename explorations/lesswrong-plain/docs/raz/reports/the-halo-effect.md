# Report: the-halo-effect ("The Halo Effect", Yudkowsky, posted 2007-11-30)

## 1. The argument in three sentences (written before annotating)

The halo effect, the social-psychology form of the affect heuristic, makes one good trait
raise judgments of others; a long passage from Cialdini reports that attractive people are
judged more talented, kind, honest and intelligent, get more votes, better jobs and pay,
lighter sentences and more help, without the judges noticing. Attractiveness has no
legitimate link to honesty, so this is plainly bias, though traits like honesty and
intelligence may really go together. By extension, readers should suspect a halo among
kindness, intelligence and other virtues in people they admire, and should be a little
more skeptical of attractive candidates.

## 3. Arithmetic

- Stewart: avoided jail 22/41 = 53.7% (attractive), 6/26 = 23.1% (unattractive); ratio 2.32,
  so "twice as likely" is right.
- Hamermesh-Biddle: a gap between good-looking and plain of premium + penalty, roughly 5-10%
  plus somewhat less, is consistent in size with Cialdini's 12-14%. ("broadly in line")

## 6. Judgment calls

1. The Eagly cfact says the review "does not support two of the four traits". The review found
   the effect "near zero" for integrity and concern for others; I map honesty to integrity and
   kindness to concern for others. That mapping is mine, though it is the natural one.
2. The Thorndike cfact ("What the post offers as a suspicion was the original finding") and the
   matching Response paragraph and "In short" clause. A fair defender could say the post only
   tells readers to apply the halo idea to people they know, and that not citing Thorndike is
   what any essay does (STANDARDS 2.2 test 4). I kept it because the post frames the existence
   of such a halo as a conjecture ("there may be"), and the reader should know it is the
   established core of the effect; the note also credits the post's distinction as Thorndike's
   own. The editor may prefer to drop it from the "In short" line.
3. The Efran note says only that the source is unpublished and unchecked, and adds later
   evidence in the same direction, so that the note does not suggest the effect is doubtful.
4. The Stewart note: "The ratio is right ... but it is a raw comparison." The confound is the
   author's own observation (seriousness correlated with attractiveness).
5. Pronouns: Thorndike gets "his"/"he" (historical figure, well documented). raz_check flags
   them.
6. No reserved words used.
