# Report: crisis-of-faith

## 1. The argument in three sentences

Some people keep beliefs a fresh ten-year-old could see through, and even a Nobel laureate
fluent in Bayesian mathematics (Robert Aumann, an Orthodox Jew) did not doubt his faith
successfully, so any long-held, emotionally loaded belief of one's own may be equally wrong
without one knowing it. Rehearsing one's standard justifications, including the rationalist's
"Occam's Razor!", is the same pattern that keeps theists in their religion; a true crisis of
faith, one that could go either way, requires the desperate effort a theist would need to
leave a religion: blocking cached replies, seeking the most painful objections, and
visualizing the alternative honestly. The post lists warning signs that call for such a
crisis, gives practical conditions for staging one (rest, uninterrupted hours, quiet), and
collects the earlier essays that supply its component techniques.

## 6. Judgment calls for the editor

1. The Aumann cpara: "follows only if his belief is false, which is the premise of the first
   paragraph, not something this case shows". A fair defender will say the post openly takes
   religion to be false. The note does not dispute the premise; it says the case cannot add
   evidence for the danger beyond it. The Response puts it as "shows the danger only to readers
   who already share the verdict". I think this passes test 1, but it is the note the author
   would most dispute.
2. The two Aumann cfacts. The post hedges ("I feel reasonably confident in venturing"); the
   notes do not say the guess is wrong, only that Aumann's public account differs from the
   post's picture and that the post does not cite it. "Let the test decide" (Bible codes) is my
   characterization; the committee's result and his conclusion are sourced.
3. Footnote-4 note (many-worlds): says the footnote does not ask of the author's formulation
   what the paragraph asks of the reader's. That is an observation about the text, not a
   motive reading. Brief, with pointers back (STANDARDS 2.5).
4. The Descartes note: "The setting has a famous precedent ... It names no precedent." The
   post claims no originality for the setting; the note gives credit and difference.
5. The isshokenmei note: could be seen as a word nitpick (2.4(2)). I kept it because the post
   presents the word as a concept and glosses it ("lifelong") with a sense the dictionary does
   not give. Cut if the editor disagrees.
6. The camel note is an allusion I infer; the post's hedge ("I wonder") is kept, and the
   retraction came after the post (said in the note).
7. Reserved words: "false" appears only in the post's own sense (false belief). "never" was
   removed from the In short line ("does not show at work").
8. Praise: the Response credits the post twice (turns the test on its readers' own replies;
   the reframing "is the right question"). Both seemed needed for accuracy.
9. Layout: two overfull boxes (41pt, 132pt) at the post's footnote 3, caused by the bare URLs
   in the post text (known issue from batch 1).
