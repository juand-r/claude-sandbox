# Report: are-your-enemies-innately-evil ("Are Your Enemies Innately Evil?", Yudkowsky, posted 2007-06-26)

## 1. The argument in three sentences (written before annotating)

The correspondence bias grows stronger when someone offends us, so we explain enemies by an evil disposition, when prior probability says we should ask what they believe about their situation; most people see themselves as heroes, and the political habit of treating arguments as soldiers makes any factual correction look like treason. Understanding enemies does not mean excusing them: when you must defend yourself, killing someone who might have been your friend is a tragedy, but the alternative is a worse tragedy, and wanting a non-tragic option is wanting a one-sided policy debate. An accurate picture of the enemy should make you sad rather than righteous: the 9/11 hijackers were not evil mutants who hated freedom but heroes of their own stories, who might in another environment have been police officers.

## 5. Items not verified

- The hijackers' own statements (for example the "last night" letter or the martyrdom videos). I used bin Laden's 2004 statement, and the note says he was not one of the nineteen.
- The "correspondence bias redoubles" premise: I did not search for studies. Malle (2006, see the Correspondence Bias report) found the actor-observer asymmetry held for negative events, which is related but not the same claim, so I did not cite it here.
