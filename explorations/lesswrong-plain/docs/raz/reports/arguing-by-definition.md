# Report: arguing-by-definition ("Arguing \"By Definition\"", Yudkowsky, posted 2008-02-20)

## 1. The argument in three sentences (written before annotating)

Arguments "by definition" start from visible features that fit a dictionary definition, but
the visible features are rarely what the dispute is about; the legitimate move from visible to
hidden properties is a probabilistic inference from the cluster structure of the world, which no
definition changes. Saying "by definition" amounts to refusing to condition on further evidence
(Socrates seen with herbologists, the coin already seen to show heads), and people reach for the
phrase exactly when the default inference has been called into doubt, or when X does not resemble
the central members of Y and a connotation of Y is to be sneaked in ("atheism is, by
definition, a religion"). By the author's informal tally over thirteen years, "by definition"
outside mathematics is among the strongest signals of a flawed argument, so readers should drop
the phrase.

## 6. Judgment calls

1. clogic on "Which is to say: People insist ...", with Kaufman. This is the main criticism and
   the one the task's note warned about (legal and political examples). I kept it as a scope
   point: the post's account fits the debater's argument it quotes, and does not cover
   definitions that are rules. A fair defender could say the post is about debate, not law; the
   note says exactly that ("This covers the debater's argument above"). The case is used only as
   a checkable instance of the post's own example classified by a legal definition; no view on
   the case is taken. The MTSU article adds a bracketed "[only]" to another court sentence; I did
   not use that quotation.
2. Afterword: "The herbologists are a hypothetical" and "hypothetical cases" (not "invented",
   per test 3): the point is only that the claim about when people use the phrase is supported by
   the author's tally and by cases the author constructed.
3. "shows none of them" (Feynman and later correct uses): a plain description; kept as one
   sentence in the Response.
4. cstyle on "legal": reading aid, so that a reader does not take "legal" as law (STANDARDS 2.2
   test 6). It also keeps the Kaufman note from seeming to answer the post's word "legal".
5. "The inference is hedged (``probably'') and it is sound": the one praise word, kept because
   the paragraph's argument is correct and a bare description would undersell it.
