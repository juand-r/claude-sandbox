# Report: if-many-worlds-had-come-first

## 1. The argument in three sentences

After a short history of Everett's 1957 proposal and its neglect, the post asks whether the
order of discovery drives the popularity of interpretations: if many-worlds had come first and
collapse had been proposed only in 1957, would collapse now be gaining ground? It answers with
a satire in which Huve Erett presents a collapse postulate to Biels Nohr, who rejects it as
unphysical (non-linear, non-local, informally specified, untestable, unsupported by evidence,
and no explanation of the Born statistics). The joke's reversal is that Huve's imagined
"absurd" world, where physicists knew quantum mechanics for thirty years and never thought
there might be more than one world, is our own.

## 3. Arithmetic recomputed

- GRW rate: f = 10^-16 per second per particle; 1/f = 10^16 s = 3.2 × 10^8 years, of the
  order of the SEP's "every hundred million years". The note gives the SEP's wording.
- Dates: Everett's defense job spring/June 1956; paper July 1957; Copenhagen visit March–April
  1959 (three years after the job). QNR posted 2008-05-08, this post 2008-05-10 (two days);
  Collapse Postulates 2008-05-09 (the day before).

## 6. Judgment calls

- The GRW notes (on "the only informally specified ... law" and on "I can't construct a test")
  are the main charge: the satire's collapse is the textbook postulate, and the published,
  specified collapse theories answer several of Nohr's charges. The second note also credits
  Nohr: no test has found a collapse. The Response calls Huve's version "the least specified
  form of its rival" rather than "weakest".
- The Born-rule symmetry note gives both sides from the SEP (Wallace's "arguably the most
  serious difficulty" and Papineau's "no disadvantage"). The full assessment is left to Many
  Worlds, One Best Guess.
- The von Neumann and Schrödinger notes: the post's line is fiction ("That's right ... He
  wouldn't"), but the satire's reversal depends on the claim that in our world no one asked
  whether the laws were universal, so I treated it as presupposed history. A fair defender
  might say "not one person says 'Hey, I wonder if these laws happen to be universal'" means
  "no one proposed that they are", which Schrödinger 1935 did not do. The notes say only that
  the founders took up the question and judged the answer absurd.
- "Crushed, Everett left": the note says "The order is not the post's". The post does not say
  explicitly that Everett left because of Bohr, but "Crushed" following Bohr's rejection
  implies it. Wheeler's "disappointed, perhaps bitter" supports "crushed" in part, and the
  note quotes it.
- Lagrange note: possibly a nitpick under STANDARDS 2.4(2); kept as one short cfact because it
  is a factual claim about a real person that readers would repeat. The editor may cut it; it
  is also in the Response's last paragraph and in one honest n.b. sentence.
- Wheeler–DeWitt note: labelled "A slip, though the point survives it", kept out of the
  "In short" line per the Book III lesson.
- The last cpara ("the joke equates declining cryonics with stupidity") describes the text
  literally; no motive reading.
- Reserved-word flags: "never" twice in paraphrase of the post, once in the Response
  describing what the ending mocks.
