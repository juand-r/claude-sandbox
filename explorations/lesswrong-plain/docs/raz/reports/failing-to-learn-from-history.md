# Report: failing-to-learn-from-history

## 1. The argument in three sentences

The author's most critical youthful mistake was not realizing that solving a mystery should
make it feel less confusing; the young author thought the chosen phenomenon was a new and
stranger kind of mystery, unlike the stars, matter and life that science had already
explained. The lesson history seemed to teach was that astrologers, alchemists and vitalists
had a character flaw, when in fact vitalism had seemed reasonable at the time and the
universe's answer, that life is mundane, was a surprise. Reading history is not living it;
had the author personally lived through those refutations, the new mysterious answer would
have been recognized at once.

## 6. Judgment calls for the editor

1. Kelvin note (last paragraph), repeated in the Response and an n.b., and pointed to from
   Making History Available. Fair defender: the post's counterfactual is about someone who
   personally "postulated" mysterious answers and then saw them refuted; Carnot's theory of heat
   is not a "mysterious answer" in the post's sense. My note says only that Kelvin "lived
   through a scientific revolution in his own field" and gave up Carnot's theory for Joule's,
   which the source supports, and does not call Carnot's theory mysterious. The editor should
   judge whether the case is close enough to the post's hypothetical.
2. The first \cpara's "works in one direction" point: the post claims only a necessary
   condition, so the note does not say the rule is wrong, only what it cannot catch. It also
   touches our Say Not Complexity note's point about feelings; I made it a different point (what
   the rule misses) rather than repeating that note's circularity charge.
3. Pronouns: Kelvin is a historical figure with documented pronouns (STANDARDS 2.6); raz_check
   flags "he/his" for him. No pronoun is used for the author.
