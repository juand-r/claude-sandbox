## 1. The argument in three sentences

Dictionaries record past usage and do not legislate it (except for bodies like the IEEE that
negotiate their terms), but there is a real case for common usage: shared words are a public
good that makes communication possible. When both parties already know what the other means,
as Albert and Barry do, an appeal to common usage cannot be about communication, so a mere
naming conflict should be settled by coining two words. When a categorization carries hidden
inferences or moral stakes, the dispute is real, and common usage or a dictionary cannot
settle it, since definitions used for inference can be wrong and are wiser or more foolish.

## 6. Judgment calls for the editor

- Nix v. Hedden (clogic): a boundary condition on "once any empirical proposition is at stake ... you can no longer appeal to common usage." A fair defender would say the post means propositions about the things, not about what a word meant. The note says exactly that ("The rule holds for questions about the things named") and names the exception; it is in the In short line as "needs one exception". If the editor judges the post's context (clustering for inference) to make the scope obvious, the In short line should drop it and the note stay.
- OED note (cfact): says the OED and the post use "authoritative" in different senses. Not a charge of error; it points out a verbal difference of the kind the post warns about. Could be read as a gentle jab; I kept the wording neutral.
- Académie française and Johnson: information that qualifies "dictionary editors ... are historians" and shows the IEEE is not the only lawgiving dictionary. The post says "an example of proper usage", not "the only", and my text now says so (I corrected an earlier draft of the Summary that said "the one proper").
- Reserved words: "wrong" appears only as a paraphrase of the post ("definitions can be *wrong*"); "invented" describes the post's characters.
