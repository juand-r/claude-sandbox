# Report: priming-and-contamination

## 1. The argument in three sentences

Seeing one word speeds recognition of related words, an automatic effect of spreading
activation, and anchors work partly the same way: comparing a temperature to 5°C or 20°C
speeds recognition of cold or hot words. More generally, uninformative or irrelevant
information "contaminates" estimates and decisions, and the author says modern research
attributes most anchoring to this rather than to adjustment. Because any idea primes
compatible thoughts, contamination is a form of confirmation bias built into the brain, so
one quick flash can already decide the conclusion.

## 6. Judgment calls

- The Wansink note. It states that no retraction was found, and that the misconduct finding
  concerned other work. I kept it because "shown to work" rests on that one paper, and a
  reader would want to know. The editor may judge it guilt by association and cut it; if so,
  also cut the honest n.b. on Wansink.
- The first cpara and the last \cfact separate semantic priming (held up) from social or
  behavioural priming (failed replications). The brief named priming as "the obvious case";
  the priming this post describes is not the kind that failed, so I did not say it failed.
- "The detail of stopping 'at one end of a confidence interval' is not in the 1974 paper":
  an attribution point. It may count as a nitpick under 2.4(2); I kept it because the post
  presents the confidence-interval mechanism as Tversky and Kahneman's.
- The \clogic on "most": attacked as hedged ("Offered with 'seems'").
- The cpara "Revises ... without saying that it does": a descriptive cross-post note; the
  full criticism is in the anchoring note.
