# Report: the-proper-use-of-doubt ("The Proper Use of Doubt", Yudkowsky, posted 2007-08-06)

## 1. The argument in three sentences (written before annotating)

Told that Jesuit novices are made to doubt their faith (a report the author could not confirm and treats as possibly hypothetical), the author grants they would not be "fleeing from doubt" but finds the practice suspicious, since to an ideal rationalist doubt should not be scary. A rational doubt arises from a specific reason, implies an investigation, and exists to end either in the destruction of its target belief or in its own death; an unresolved or uninvestigated doubt does nothing. Doubting is admired as a display of modesty, but displaying doubt without a reason or an investigation is only professing, like wearing a lab coat.

## 6. Judgment calls

- Peirce uncredited: the post does not claim novelty outright, but says the point "is not
  widely understood". I judged that enough to name the old source; the note says "Peirce is
  not mentioned" rather than "presented as new".
- The null-op `\clogic` ("Overstated, by the author's own earlier example"): a defender could
  say the engineer's reason ("Historically, it's not unheard-of for an engineer to be damn sure
  a new machine won't fail, and then it fails anyway") is general, not the "specific
  justification" the post requires of a rational doubt. But the rule "An unresolved doubt is a
  null-op" is stated of doubts in general, and the engineer's doubt, unresolved, changes the
  design. The note says only that the rule is overstated and "fits doubts that can be
  settled", not that it is false.
- The courage/desensitization `\clogic` uses "sits uneasily" (not "contradicts"): the post
  can reply that its suspicion concerns motive, not the practice's usefulness.
- Pronoun "he" for Peirce (historical figure, documented) and inside Peirce's own words.
- I did not note the bullet "you can justly be proud when you have just finished tearing a
  cherished belief to shreds" against "Do not indulge in drama and become proud of admitting
  errors" in "The Importance of Saying Oops"; the two can be reconciled and the note would be
  close to a contrived objection.
