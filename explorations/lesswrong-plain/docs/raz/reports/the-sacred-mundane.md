# Report: the-sacred-mundane

## 1. The argument in three sentences

Adam Frank's book, of which the author read about half, holds that the experience of the
sacred is shared by science and religion, and (following William James) that it is private
and individual; the author reads the stress on privacy as a trick of "Dark Side
Epistemology", since a private experience cannot be criticized. Religion, the post argues,
distorts the experience of the sacred through five habits that grew up to shield its
original lie (mysteriousness, faith, experientialism, separation, privacy), so it should
not be salvaged even as "spirituality": the wise start over from scratch. The capacity to
admit being entirely wrong is what separates scientific from religious experience, and the
post ends with the sacred as it should be: believable, real, knowable, shared, and made of
the same stuff as everything else, "the sacred mundane".

## 6. Judgment calls for the editor

1. James note (paragraph 8) and Response: the post says James "admired" solitude "as the
   core of religious experience, as if loneliness were a good thing". I credit "core" (James:
   "the primordial thing") and say only that "loneliness" reads a claim about where religion
   begins as praise of being alone. James's "in their solitude" does literally mean "alone";
   a defender could say the jab is fair. I kept it because James's reason for putting the
   individual first is stated and is not privacy-as-shield.
2. "Neither James nor Frank is shown using privacy that way": true of the post; I could not
   read Frank's book, so I say "is shown", not "does".
3. The Genetic Fallacy note is a cross-post tension, stated with the balancing sentence that
   the post's per-habit costs are reasons independent of origin. The main charge kept in the
   Response is the scope one: no spiritual practice is examined.
4. Cut after checking: a note that the tie of faith to the sacred predates archaeology
   (John 20:29 "blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed"). A fair defender
   answers that the verse blesses belief on testimony, and the post itself says early
   religions "staked their reputation upon the testimony of miracles". Tertullian's "certum
   est, quia impossibile" is disputed in meaning (Wikipedia, Credo quia absurdum). Dropped.
5. Cut: a note that "No religion can absorb that capacity" is an uneven comparison
   (religion judged whole, science by its theories). The post's point is that wonder at the
   stars does not depend on any theory, so the comparison is fair on its own terms. A
   Dalai Lama quotation on revising Buddhism by science could not be verified (NYT paywalled,
   only a quotation site found), so it is not used.
6. Reserved words: "wrong" appears only in paraphrase of the post's "entirely wrong".
7. The Keats note does not say "misquotes"; the sense of "mundane" and "common" is close.
   It is kept because the changed word is the post's own title word inside a quotation.
