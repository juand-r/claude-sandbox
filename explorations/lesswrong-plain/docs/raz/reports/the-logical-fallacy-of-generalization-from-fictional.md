# Report: the-logical-fallacy-of-generalization-from-fictional

## 1. The argument in three sentences

Using films and novels as starting points for thinking about the future is an error even
though no one believes them, because stories are shaped by storytelling needs, must follow one
detailed line of events, skip the hard work of locating which possibilities deserve attention,
and frame the debate in their own terms. People do not believe fiction, but it is available in
memory and recalled like a historical case, and seeing fictional examples builds automatic
categories (the Borg), as the author found in catching the author's own argument from a Vinge
character. Worst of all, in the author's estimation, borrowed imaginings stop people from
seeing for themselves.

## 3. Arithmetic

- 100-bit paragraph: a 100-bit proposition has prior odds about 1 : 2^100. After 98 bits,
  odds 2^-2 = 1:4 (probability 0.2). After 5 more bits, 2^3 = 8:1 (about 0.89); after 7,
  32:1 (0.97). "A handful more ... near-certainty" is consistent. The note says so.

## 6. Judgment calls for the editor

1. cstyle on "logical fallacy": the name fits poorly with the post's own psychological
   account. Fair defender: "'logical fallacy' in ordinary use covers informal fallacies, and
   this is a kind of hasty generalization." The note rests on the post's own words ("The movie
   is not believed, but it is cognitively available") and the chapter's "mix of anchoring and
   availability", and concedes that the Vinge case is an argument. Could be judged a wording
   nitpick (2.4(2)); I kept it because the Response's structure uses it.
2. Framing note: "a superintelligence snapping nanotechnological fingers" treated as the post's
   own specific scenario. Fair defender: "I only contrasted two images; I did not assert
   mine." The note says the picture "arrives without the evidence" the post says a specific
   scenario needs, not that the post asserts it. The "In short" line says the framing argument
   is "not applied to the post's own picture of AI", which rests on this one note.
3. Green and Brock note is credit to the post's thesis combined with a criticism that the post
   cites nothing. I think leaving it out would make the "no evidence" point misleading.
4. Pirsig note: states facts only (Pirsig's student was blocked by what she "had already
   heard"; the author's "either way, it's true" two days earlier). I did not write the
   inference that the post relies on a story of unknown truth; the reader can draw it. The
   honest n.b. gives the same fact.
5. Bostrom note: "points out" versus a supposition. Small, but the chapter's own
   "hypothesized" makes it a fair comparison.
6. Final-line note: the hedge "in my estimation" is dropped in the last sentence. The last
   sentence is a flourish; fair defender 3 is possible.
7. Reserved-word flags ("wrong", "false") are in descriptions of the post's own claim ("one can
   go wrong without believing anything false"). Pronouns: Pirsig's student "she" (a
   character). "catching himself" was removed from the Summary.
8. Considered and dropped: scenario planning as a counterexample to "A story is never a rational
   attempt at analysis" (the post's claim is about one story; a set of scenarios is not a
   story); social-priming replication failures (no specific study to test); the author's later
   fiction ("That Alien Message", 2008) as self-application (a different use, and outside this
   post's scope); discovery versus justification (not needed).
