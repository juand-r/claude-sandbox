# Report: what-s-a-bias

## 1. The argument in three sentences

A cognitive bias is an obstacle to truth that comes not from costly information or limited
computing power but from the shape of our mental machinery (for example, the side effects
of a useful heuristic such as availability), and it is to be distinguished from mistakes
(errors of learned content), from brain damage, and from absorbed culture, since biases
come from universal machinery. Plato illustrates the line: ignorance of relativity is not a
bias, but a belief in philosopher-kings that came from a universal instinct for
self-promotion would be. The definition does not matter much: we fight biases not out of
duty but because they stand between us and the truth, which we need and are curious about.

## 6. Judgment calls for the editor

1. Three notes rest on the 2006 revision (opening paragraph; hedges dropped; "In cognitive
   science" added). This is a new kind of note for the project: comparing the text readers
   meet with the text first posted. I think it is fair (it is the author's own text, and the
   change is to hedges, which STANDARDS 2.2(2) treats as important), but the editor should
   decide whether revision history belongs in the notes at all. If it does, the brief could
   tell every agent to check `revisions` for 2006-2008 posts, since other posts may also
   have been revised for the book.
2. "which turns the author's usage into a claim about a field": this describes the effect of
   an edit, not a motive. I avoided saying why it was added.
3. The limited-computing-power note says "the line between the two causes is not drawn". A
   fair defender could answer: the shortcut exists because of limited computation, but which
   errors it makes depends on its shape. The note only says the post does not explain how to
   tell them apart, which is true; I did not write "contradicts".
4. The Plato note: the example is a hypothetical (fair-defender 3), and the note grants that
   ("shows what the definition means"). Its point is about applying the definition. Some
   editors may see this as faulting a definition for being a definition.
5. Pronouns "his" in the Plato notes refer to Plato (a historical figure; the post itself uses
   "he"), not to the author.
