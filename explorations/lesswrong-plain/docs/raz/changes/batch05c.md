That Omega ignores the reasoning and rewards only the choice does not meet the charge that the choice itself is irrational. Lewis, who put ``Why ain'cha rich?'' in a title, concluded in 1981 that on whether the one-boxers' riches prove anything, ``it's a standoff.'' The claim that Rachel envies only Irene's choice meets Joyce's own sentence that she wishes ``for Irene's options, not sanctioning her choice'' by restating the post's view. The cliff analogy shows a case where calling an advantage unfair would be absurd, but not that Newcomb's problem is such a case.

One objection the post answers is given without a source. Its causal decision theorist says one-boxers must believe their choice affects the box. Lewis's charge is different: the evidential rule seeks good news about matters the choice cannot affect. That charge needs no belief that the choice affects the box. The stakes argument goes from wishing that one-boxing were reasonable to redefining ``reasonable.'' Joyce's passage already grants the wish and denies the step.

The post grants the common view that verbal arguments for one-boxing are easy and that the hard part is a theory which does not fail elsewhere. Two smaller points are in the notes: the inference to an unbounded utility function needs a further premise, and ``Bayescraft'' does not by itself choose between the two decision theories.
