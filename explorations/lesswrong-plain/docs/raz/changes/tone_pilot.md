- Before: The post has two parts: a premise about human values, and an account of how simple value proposals are defended. The second part is the stronger.

Its answer to the proposers is sound. A process that maximized complexity would search for whatever is most complex; it would not stop at a mother's love because that love is complex and people like it. This is the test of the million-dollar laptop in ``Fake Justification,'' applied to a principle instead of a purchase. The post also states correctly what a real argument for one master value would have to show: that other goods are good only when they serve it. That is the same requirement the \textsc{Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy} states for utilitarian monists, and some monists, Mill among them, have tried to meet it. The post's target is the weaker proposer who argues from pleasant consequences instead.

The premise is asserted more firmly than it is argued. That human values are many and underived is called a descriptive ``known fact.'' The support is by reference to earlier posts on evolution and desire, chiefly ``Thou Art Godshatter,'' whose central step our notes on that post describe as an inference presented as history. No study of how people's values are related is cited. The corresponding view about what is valuable, value pluralism, is old in moral philosophy; W. D. Ross held it in 1930.

In short: a sound account of how simple utility functions are defended and what a real defence would need, resting on a premise about the many-valued nature of human wants that is called a known fact and argued only in earlier posts.

The \textsc{Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy} states the same requirement for utilitarian monists, and Mill tried to meet it. The post's target is not Mill. It is a proposer who argues from pleasant consequences.

That proposer never appears in person. The philosophers' normative cousin of the thesis, value pluralism, is old; W. D. Ross held it in 1930.

The stakes come from fiction, and the post labels them as such. The ``hyperexistential catastrophe'' links to a paper by Nick Bostrom in which the word does not occur.

In short: a sound demolition of arguments from pleasant consequences, aimed at opponents the reader never hears, and resting on a premise that arrives labelled ``known fact'' with only earlier posts behind it.

The post keeps both the allergy and this lesson, and leaves for later posts how one is to tell which kind of case one faces.

In short: a memoir that the author's writings of the 1990s bear out, joined to a defence of the childhood allergy to talk of risk whose record is one estimate about one regulator and a list of old controversies.

In short: a memoir the archive bears out, attached to a defence of the author's old allergy to talk of risk that rests on one regulator's record, apparently misreported by a factor of ten, and silent on the one kind of risk the post turns out to be about.

## annotated/afterwords/why-our-kind-can-t-cooperate.tex

Why: Tone pilot: original-52 voice. Restores, as fact, two points cut in 6b (the post reassembles earlier posts; title firmer than body), which the user's decision on redundancy and tone allows; all facts from the notes.

- Before: The post's question is a fair one, and its remedy has support. Its diagnosis rests on less.

The evidence for the diagnosis is one fundraising appeal, told from memory by the person who ran it, and the author's observations of the community. From these the post names ``the two major forces'' that keep the ``atheist/libertarian/technophile'' crowd from coordinating. The body is hedged (``Perhaps that is why''). For information, the outside record is mixed. In the year before the post, supporters of Ron Paul, a former Libertarian Party nominee, raised over \$4.2 million online in one day, and a British atheist bus campaign that had failed to gather pledges raised over \pounds100,000 in four days once it was relaunched. Such groups can coordinate giving at times. These cases do not test the post's account of its own appeal.

Two findings favour the post. Its remedy, public support voiced alongside objections, has experimental backing: in a field experiment on charitable giving, telling new donors what others had given raised their gifts. And many Raelians, members of the post's example of a group united by nonsense, call their creed an ``atheistic religion.'' That fits the post's view that the difference lies in group culture, not in belief.

A careful reader should note what the post does not claim. The paragraph saying the ``Light Side will always be divided and weak'' and that ``the future inevitably belongs to the Dark'' states a view the post goes on to reject. The objection raised early, that the critics of the appeal may have been right, is answered at the end: objections belong at another time, or beside public support.

In short: a diagnosis of the author's community drawn from one fundraising appeal and the author's own observations, with a remedy, public support beside public objections, that an experiment on giving supports.
