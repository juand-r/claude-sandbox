Machine learning · Evaluating classifiers

ROC curves
and AUC

A classifier gives a score. Where we cut that score decides which mistakes we make. The ROC curve shows every choice at once.

Today is about judging a classifier that outputs scores, before we commit to a threshold.

Today

From scores to decisions

We can trade off precision and recall (or TPR and FPR) by choosing how lenient or strict we are when we turn a model's score into a class label.

Each model we have seen gives a score
  • Nearest neighbours: the fraction of the k nearest neighbours of x that are positive.
  • Naive Bayes: the posterior probability that x is positive, P(+ | x), from Bayes' rule.
  • Decision trees: the fraction of training records in x's leaf that are positive.

In each case every point gets a score, and a threshold turns scores into decisions. So far we used 0.5, the majority class. But we can pick any threshold we want.

Every classifier we have met outputs a number before it outputs a label. With k-NN the label is a majority vote, which is the same as thresholding the positive fraction at 0.5. Naive Bayes picks the class with the larger posterior, which for two classes is thresholding P(+ | x) at 0.5. A tree leaf predicts its majority class, again a threshold of 0.5 on the leaf's positive fraction. Moving that threshold is the subject of today: a lower one catches more positives and raises more false alarms; a higher one does the reverse.

2 · Rates

Two rates

  • True positive rate
    TPR = TP / (TP + FN)
    Of the actual positives, the fraction we caught.
  • False positive rate
    FPR = FP / (FP + TN)
    Of the actual negatives, the fraction we wrongly flagged.
  • Each rate uses one row of the matrix, one true class. Neither depends on how many positives there are compared with negatives.
  • Lower t and both rates rise. The threshold buys TPR and pays in FPR.

0Raw counts depend on how many examples of each class we happen to have. Dividing within each class fixes that.

1TPR is also called recall or sensitivity. At t = 0.5, 8 of the 10 positives are caught, so TPR = 0.8.

2FPR: at t = 0.5, 4 of the 10 negatives are flagged, so FPR = 0.4. In medicine one reports specificity instead, which is 1 − FPR.

3If we doubled the number of negatives, drawn from the same distribution, both rates would stay where they are. This property comes back twice: once as a strength, once as a weakness.

4Drag the threshold down: both rise. Up: both fall. Anyone can get TPR = 1 by flagging everything; FPR is the price. So we must look at the two together. That is the ROC curve.

3 · The ROC curve

What the shape
tells you

  • Many examples now. Negatives score around 0, positives around d. The shaded tails right of t are FPR and TPR.
  • d = 0: the classes look the same, so TPR = FPR at every threshold. The diagonal is chance.
  • Larger d: the curve bows toward (0, 1), the perfect classifier.
  • d < 0: below the diagonal. The scores point the wrong way; reverse them and the curve flips above.

0To see shapes clearly, imagine infinitely many examples: negative scores follow a bell curve centred at 0, positive scores the same curve centred at d.

1d = 1. The green area to the right of t is the fraction of positives flagged, TPR. The purple area to the right of t is FPR. Drag t on the left; the point moves along the curve on the right.

2d = 0. The two classes have the same scores, so any threshold flags the same fraction of each: TPR = FPR, the diagonal. A coin that says "positive" with probability q also lands on (q, q). AUC = 0.5.

3d = 2.5. Less overlap, and the curve hugs the top-left corner, where we catch every positive with no false alarms. For this model AUC = Φ(d/√2), here 0.96.

4d = −1. Worse than chance. This is a useful classifier with its labels backwards. Reversing the scores turns every point (x, y) into (1 − x, 1 − y): the curve is rotated half a turn about the centre, and AUC becomes 1 − AUC.

4 · AUC

Area under
the curve

AUC is the area under the ROC curve. A perfect classifier has 1; chance has 0.5.

  • Another reading: pick one positive and one negative at random. AUC is the probability that the positive has the higher score.
  • Why: each (positive, negative) pair is one cell of the 10 × 10 grid. The cell is under the curve exactly when the positive outranks the negative.
  • Here 79 of the 100 pairs are in the right order, so AUC = 0.79. Ties count as half.

3Press "Check all 100": 79 cells under the curve, 21 above. This count is the Mann–Whitney U statistic, so AUC = U / (P·N). It can be computed by sorting the scores once, without drawing anything.

4 · AUC

What AUC
does not see

  • Only the order of the scores matters. Cube them or squash them: the curve and the AUC stay put.
  • So AUC says nothing about calibration, whether a score of 0.8 means an 80% chance.
  • Reverse the order and every won pair is lost: AUC = 1 − 0.79 = 0.21.
  • AUC averages over all thresholds, but we deploy one. Two models with equal AUC can differ a lot at the threshold we care about.

2After squashing, every score sits between 0.45 and 0.55. AUC is still 0.79, but as probabilities these numbers are useless. To check calibration, use a reliability diagram: bin the scores and compare each bin's average score with its fraction of positives.

3Press 1 − s. This function is decreasing, so the order reverses and AUC drops to 0.21.

4Two ROC curves can cross: one model better at low FPR, the other at high FPR, with equal areas. If only FPR below 1% is acceptable, as in screening, compare the curves in that region or at the operating point itself.

5 · A caveat

When positives
are rare

  • Fraud: 100 fraudulent transactions, 100,000 legitimate ones. A model at TPR 0.90, FPR 0.01 sits near the top-left corner.
  • It flags 90 frauds and 1,000 legitimate transactions. Only 8% of its alarms are real.
  • Precision = TP / (TP + FP) depends on the class ratio; TPR and FPR do not. When positives are rare, also plot precision against recall.

0The within-class property from slide 4, now as a weakness.

1On an ROC plot the point (0.01, 0.90) is close to the top-left corner. It looks excellent.

2But 1% of 100,000 is 1,000. Ninety real frauds are mixed into 1,090 alarms; an analyst finds a real case about once in twelve.

3Drag the slider: TPR and FPR are fixed, only the class ratio changes. Precision collapses as negatives multiply, and the ROC curve does not move at all. Precision–recall curves show this. When positives are rare and false alarms are expensive, look at both. The threshold itself should come from the costs of each kind of mistake.

Summary

Takeaways

Rates

TPR and FPR

TPR = TP/(TP+FN): positives caught. FPR = FP/(FP+TN): negatives wrongly flagged. Each is computed inside one class.

Curve

ROC

Every threshold gives one point (FPR, TPR). The diagonal is chance; the top-left corner is perfect.

Number

AUC

The area under the ROC curve. It equals the probability that a random positive outscores a random negative.

Limits

What it hides

Only the ranking counts: no calibration, no chosen threshold. With rare positives, check precision too.

Four cards, one per idea. If students keep one sentence: AUC is the chance that the model ranks a random positive above a random negative.
