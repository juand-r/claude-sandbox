Forward propagation

\[\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{a}^{(1)} &= \sigma(\boldsymbol{W}^{(1)} \boldsymbol{x} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(1)}) \\ \boldsymbol{z} &= \boldsymbol{W}^{(2)} \boldsymbol{a}^{(1)} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(2)} \\ \boldsymbol{p} &= \operatorname{softmax}(\boldsymbol{z}) \end{aligned}\]
  • Train by gradient descent:\[\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\, \nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\]

Backpropagation

The network is one function

  • Notation, for layer \(l\): each unit computes a weighted sum \(z^{(l)}\), the pre-activation, then its activation \(a^{(l)} = \varphi(z^{(l)})\). The inputs are \(a^{(0)} = \boldsymbol{x}\).\(w^{(l)}_{ji}\) is the weight from unit \(i\) of layer \(l - 1\) to unit \(j\) of layer \(l\); \(b^{(l)}_j\) is unit \(j\)'s bias.
  • A small network for regression: two sigmoid hidden units, one linear output. Written out in full:\[\begin{aligned} \hat{y} = {} & w^{(2)}_{11}\,\sigma(w^{(1)}_{11}x_1 + w^{(1)}_{12}x_2 + b^{(1)}_1) \\ {} + {} & w^{(2)}_{12}\,\sigma(w^{(1)}_{21}x_1 + w^{(1)}_{22}x_2 + b^{(1)}_2) + b^{(2)} \end{aligned}\]The two \(\sigma(\cdot)\) terms are the hidden activations \(a^{(1)}_1\) and \(a^{(1)}_2\).

0From now on, two names per unit: z for its weighted sum (before the activation) and a for its output (after it). Last time the hidden outputs were called h; a is the same thing. The superscript is the layer. The picture shows where each symbol lives.

1The network we will use on the next slides. Inside: each hidden unit is a sigmoid of a weighted sum of the inputs, with its own bias. Outside: the prediction ŷ is a weighted sum of the hidden activations, with weights w₁₁⁽²⁾, w₁₂⁽²⁾ and bias b⁽²⁾. No activation at the output, because ŷ can be any number. The loss of one training point is squared error, ℒ = ½(ŷ − y)², as in last year's slides and the practice exam. Put the first formula in for ŷ and the loss is one long expression in the nine weights. Nine weights, all inside one nested function, and we need ∂ℒ/∂w for each one. Differentiating this directly, weight by weight, is possible but messy, and it gets worse with every layer. The chain rule takes it apart one piece at a time, and the pieces can be reused.

Backpropagation

The chain rule

  • If \(y\) depends on \(u\), and \(u\) depends on \(x\), the derivatives multiply:\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}\]
  • Example: \(y = (3x + 1)^2\). Write \(u = 3x + 1\) and \(y = u^2\):\[\frac{du}{dx} = 3, \qquad \frac{dy}{du} = 2u\]
  • At \(x = 1\), \(u = 4\):\[\frac{dy}{dx} = 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 = 24\]Check: \(y = 9x^2 + 6x + 1\), so \(dy/dx = 18x + 6 = 24\).

0The rule from calculus. Each link of the chain contributes one factor.

1Break the formula into two simple pieces, and differentiate each piece on its own.

2Put in the value of u, then multiply. Expanding the square gives the same answer, but for a network there is nothing to expand: the chain rule is the only practical way. The nested function on the last slide is exactly such a chain, with one link per layer.

Backpropagation

When the chain branches

  • If \(x\) affects \(y\) along two paths, through \(u_1\) and through \(u_2\), add the paths:\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\partial y}{\partial u_1} \cdot \frac{du_1}{dx} + \frac{\partial y}{\partial u_2} \cdot \frac{du_2}{dx}\]
  • Example: \(u_1 = x^2\), \(u_2 = 3x\), \(y = u_1 u_2\):\[\frac{du_1}{dx} = 2x, \quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial u_1} = u_2, \quad \frac{du_2}{dx} = 3, \quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial u_2} = u_1\]
  • At \(x = 2\), \(u_1 = 4\) and \(u_2 = 6\):\[\frac{dy}{dx} = 6 \cdot 4 + 4 \cdot 3 = 24 + 12 = 36\]Check: \(y = 3x^3\), so \(dy/dx = 9x^2 = 36\).

0The chain rule with several variables. Each path is a chain, so it is a product; the paths add. The partial derivative ∂y/∂u₁ means: change u₁ and hold u₂ fixed.

1y is a product of two things that both depend on x.

2This is the product rule from calculus, derived from the chain rule. In a network, a hidden unit feeds several units in the next layer, so its weights reach the loss along several paths; we will see this once the example has two outputs.

Backpropagation

Derivatives we will use

  • Sigmoid:\[\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}, \qquad \sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\,\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr)\]
  • Logarithm:\[\frac{d}{dp} \log p = \frac{1}{p}\]
  • Softmax followed by cross-entropy (last class):\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_k} = p_k - y_k\]

Three facts we will use on the next slides. The sigmoid's derivative: σ′(z) = e^{−z}/(1 + e^{−z})², and 1 − σ(z) = e^{−z}/(1 + e^{−z}), so σ′ = σ(1 − σ). It is largest, 1/4, at z = 0. log is the natural logarithm. The last one was derived in the bonus slides of last class: the softmax and the cross-entropy fit together, and the error at output k is prediction minus label.

Backpropagation

Regression example

  • One training point: \(\boldsymbol{x} = (2, 1)\), target \(y = 2\). The weights are on the connections. The output is linear: \(\hat{y} = z^{(2)}\).
  • Hidden layer:\[\begin{aligned} z^{(1)}_1 &= -0.5 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 1 + 1 = 1 \\ a^{(1)}_1 &= \sigma(1) = 0.731 \\ z^{(1)}_2 &= 0.5 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1 - 0.5 = -0.5 \\ a^{(1)}_2 &= \sigma(-0.5) = 0.378 \end{aligned}\]
  • Output:\[\hat{y} = z^{(2)} = 2 \cdot 0.731 + 0.5 \cdot 0.378 - 1 = 0.651\]
  • Loss:\[\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12 (0.651 - 2)^2 = 0.910\]

0The network from the last slide, with numbers. A regression problem: the target is a number, here 2. z⁽²⁾ is the output unit's weighted sum; with a linear output, the prediction ŷ is z⁽²⁾ itself.

1Each hidden unit: weighted sum of the inputs plus its bias (the pre-activation z), then the sigmoid (the activation a). Three decimals is enough: these numbers were chosen so that rounding at every step gives the same answers as the exact computation.

2The output: the same weighted sum, with the hidden activations as inputs, and no activation.

3The network says 0.651; the target is 2. To lower the loss, ŷ should go up. Which way should each weight move, and by how much? That is what the gradient tells us.

Backpropagation

An output weight: \(w^{(2)}_{11}\)

  • \(w^{(2)}_{11}\) changes the loss along the highlighted path, \(w^{(2)}_{11} \to z^{(2)} \to \hat{y} \to \mathcal{L}\). The chain rule multiplies the derivatives along it:\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w^{(2)}_{11}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z^{(2)}} \cdot \frac{\partial z^{(2)}}{\partial w^{(2)}_{11}}\]
  • \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial \hat{y} = \hat{y} - y = 0.651 - 2 = -1.349\)
  • \(\partial \hat{y}/\partial z^{(2)} = 1\)The output is linear, \(\hat{y} = z^{(2)}\).
  • \(\partial z^{(2)}/\partial w^{(2)}_{11} = a^{(1)}_1 = 0.731\)Because \(z^{(2)} = w^{(2)}_{11} a^{(1)}_1 + w^{(2)}_{12} a^{(1)}_2 + b^{(2)}\).
  • Multiply. Keep the first two factors for later:\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{(2)}} &= -1.349 \times 1 = -1.349 \\ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w^{(2)}_{11}} &= -1.349 \times 0.731 = -0.986 \end{aligned}\]

0The same network. w₁₁⁽²⁾ is the weight from hidden unit 1 to the output. Only one path leads from it to the loss. Each link of the path contributes one factor.

1The derivative of ½(ŷ − y)² with respect to ŷ. The ½ is there so that this comes out without a 2.

2Trivial here, but keep it in mind: when the output has an activation, this factor is its slope.

3z⁽²⁾ is linear in w₁₁⁽²⁾, and w₁₁⁽²⁾ multiplies a₁⁽¹⁾. If a₁⁽¹⁾ were near 0, this weight would barely matter for this point.

4Negative: increasing w₁₁⁽²⁾ lowers the loss. ∂ℒ/∂z⁽²⁾ is the error at the output's pre-activation. Every weight further back goes through z⁽²⁾, so this number is reused. The bias b⁽²⁾ has ∂z⁽²⁾/∂b⁽²⁾ = 1, so ∂ℒ/∂b⁽²⁾ = −1.349.

Backpropagation

A hidden weight: \(w^{(1)}_{11}\)

  • The path is longer: \(w^{(1)}_{11} \to z^{(1)}_1 \to a^{(1)}_1 \to z^{(2)} \to \hat{y} \to \mathcal{L}\).\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w^{(1)}_{11}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{(2)}} \cdot \frac{\partial z^{(2)}}{\partial a^{(1)}_1} \cdot \frac{\partial a^{(1)}_1}{\partial z^{(1)}_1} \cdot \frac{\partial z^{(1)}_1}{\partial w^{(1)}_{11}}\]
  • \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z^{(2)} = -1.349\), already computed for \(w^{(2)}_{11}\). Reuse it.
  • The new factors:\[\begin{aligned} \partial z^{(2)}/\partial a^{(1)}_1 &= w^{(2)}_{11} = 2 \\ \partial a^{(1)}_1/\partial z^{(1)}_1 &= a^{(1)}_1(1 - a^{(1)}_1) = 0.731 \times 0.269 = 0.1966 \\ \partial z^{(1)}_1/\partial w^{(1)}_{11} &= x_1 = 2 \end{aligned}\]
  • Multiply:\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w^{(1)}_{11}} = -1.349 \times 2 \times 0.1966 \times 2 = -1.061\]With another activation \(\varphi\), use \(\varphi'(z^{(1)}_1)\) in place of \(a^{(1)}_1(1 - a^{(1)}_1)\).

0A weight in the first layer, from input 1 to hidden unit 1. The path goes through the output, so its first factors are the ones we just computed.

1This is why it is called backpropagation: we start at the output and work backwards, and each step reuses the one after it. Nothing is computed twice.

2One factor per link: w₁₁⁽²⁾ because z⁽²⁾ = w₁₁⁽²⁾a₁⁽¹⁾ + …; the sigmoid's slope at hidden unit 1; and x₁ because z₁⁽¹⁾ = w₁₁⁽¹⁾x₁ + w₁₂⁽¹⁾x₂ + b₁⁽¹⁾. The slope is written with four decimals: with three (0.197) the product would come out −1.063 instead of −1.061. Only the middle factor depends on the activation function; for ReLU it would be 1, since z₁⁽¹⁾ > 0.

3Also negative: increasing w₁₁⁽¹⁾ raises a₁⁽¹⁾, which raises z⁽²⁾ and ŷ, which lowers the loss. The other weights work the same way; for w₁₂⁽¹⁾ the last factor is x₂ = 1 instead of x₁ = 2.

Backpropagation

Update the weights

  • Gradient descent, with learning rate \(\eta = 0.1\):\[w \leftarrow w - \eta\, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w}\]
  • \(w^{(2)}_{11} \leftarrow 2 - 0.1 \times (-0.986) = 2.099\)
    \(w^{(1)}_{11} \leftarrow -0.5 - 0.1 \times (-1.061) = -0.394\)
  • Do the same for all nine weights, with every gradient computed from the old weights. Then the next training point.

0The update from class 1: step against the gradient.

1Both gradients were negative, so both weights go up.

2The new values on the network.

3Compute all the gradients first, then update; otherwise later gradients would use some new weights. After one step on all nine weights, the loss on this point goes from 0.910 to 0.489 (check_numbers.py).

Backpropagation

Classification example

  • Same network and point, but the target is now a class label, \(y = 1\). Put a sigmoid on the output, so it is a probability: \(p = \sigma(z^{(2)}) = \sigma(0.651) = 0.657\).
  • The log loss, as for logistic regression:\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L} &= -y \log p - (1 - y) \log(1 - p) \\ &= -\log 0.657 = 0.420 \end{aligned}\]
  • Only the start of the chain changes. The sigmoid's slope cancels:\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{(2)}} &= \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial p} \cdot \frac{\partial p}{\partial z^{(2)}} = \Bigl(-\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - p}\Bigr)\, p(1 - p) \\ &= p - y = 0.657 - 1 = -0.343 \end{aligned}\]
  • The rest of the chain is the same as for regression:\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w^{(2)}_{11}} &= -0.343 \times 0.731 = -0.251 \\ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w^{(1)}_{11}} &= -0.343 \times 2 \times 0.1966 \times 2 = -0.270 \end{aligned}\]

0A two-class problem, label 1 or 0. The network is the same up to z⁽²⁾; the sigmoid turns z⁽²⁾ into a probability, as in logistic regression. Nothing else in the forward pass changes.

1The loss from logistic regression and class 1 (cross-entropy for two classes). With y = 1 only the first term is left.

2∂ℒ/∂p = −y/p + (1 − y)/(1 − p), here −1/0.657 = −1.522; ∂p/∂z⁽²⁾ = p(1 − p) = 0.225. Multiplied out: −y(1 − p) + (1 − y)p; the y·p terms cancel and leave p − y, the prediction minus the label. The same is true for softmax with C classes: ∂ℒ/∂zₖ⁽²⁾ = pₖ − yₖ.

3Everything before z⁽²⁾ is unchanged, so each gradient is ∂ℒ/∂z⁽²⁾ times the same factors as before. If asked: one could use squared error on p instead. Then the factor p(1 − p) = 0.225 would not cancel: ∂ℒ/∂z⁽²⁾ = (p − y)p(1 − p) = −0.077, and the gradients would be −0.056 and −0.061, about 4.4 times smaller. That factor is small whenever p is near 0 or 1, even when the network is confidently wrong (at p = 0.01 with y = 1, −0.010 against −0.99), which is one reason classifiers are trained with log loss.

Backpropagation

Two outputs

  • Same input and first layer, so \(a^{(1)}_1 = 0.731\), \(a^{(1)}_2 = 0.378\). Now two outputs, a softmax and cross-entropy. The label is class 1: \(\boldsymbol{y} = (1, 0)\).\(w^{(2)}_{kj}\) is the weight from hidden unit \(j\) to output \(k\).
  • The output pre-activations:\[\begin{aligned} z^{(2)}_1 &= 2 \cdot 0.731 + 0.5 \cdot 0.378 - 1 = 0.651 \\ z^{(2)}_2 &= 1 \cdot 0.731 + 0.5 \cdot 0.378 - 1 = -0.080 \end{aligned}\]
  • The softmax and the loss:\[\begin{aligned} p_1 &= \frac{e^{0.651}}{e^{0.651} + e^{-0.080}} = \frac{1.917}{1.917 + 0.923} = 0.675 \\ p_2 &= 0.325, \qquad \mathcal{L} = -\log 0.675 = 0.393 \end{aligned}\]
  • At each output, as before, \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z^{(2)}_k = p_k - y_k\):\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{(2)}_1} = 0.675 - 1 = -0.325, \quad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{(2)}_2} = 0.325 - 0 = 0.325\]

0The network from class 1's softmax slides, at its smallest: two classes, one score each. Output 1 keeps the weights of the one-output example (2, 0.5 and bias −1), so z₁⁽²⁾ is the same as z⁽²⁾ before.

1Each output's pre-activation (its score) is a weighted sum of the hidden activations plus a bias.

2The softmax turns the scores into probabilities; they add up to 1. The loss is −log of the probability of the true class, class 1.

3The softmax version of p − y, from the last slide and from class 1's bonus slide. One number per output: the network gives class 1 too little probability (negative) and class 2 too much (positive). With two classes the two always add up to 0.

Backpropagation

Add the paths

  • \(w^{(1)}_{11}\) changes \(a^{(1)}_1\), and \(a^{(1)}_1\) feeds both outputs. So \(w^{(1)}_{11}\) reaches the loss along two paths.
  • Through \(z^{(2)}_1\):\[\begin{aligned} &(p_1 - y_1) \cdot w^{(2)}_{11} \cdot a^{(1)}_1(1 - a^{(1)}_1) \cdot x_1 \\ &= -0.325 \times 2 \times 0.1966 \times 2 = -0.256 \end{aligned}\]
  • Through \(z^{(2)}_2\):\[\begin{aligned} &(p_2 - y_2) \cdot w^{(2)}_{21} \cdot a^{(1)}_1(1 - a^{(1)}_1) \cdot x_1 \\ &= 0.325 \times 1 \times 0.1966 \times 2 = 0.128 \end{aligned}\]
  • Add them:\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w^{(1)}_{11}} &= \sum_k (p_k - y_k)\, w^{(2)}_{k1} \cdot a^{(1)}_1(1 - a^{(1)}_1) \cdot x_1 \\ &= -0.256 + 0.128 = -0.128 \end{aligned}\]

0Last year's slides showed the same picture: one hidden weight, two highlighted paths.

1The same four factors as the one-output example: the error at the output, the weight from hidden unit 1 to that output, the sigmoid's slope at hidden unit 1, and x₁.

2The other path. Only the first two factors change. It is positive: raising a₁⁽¹⁾ also raises z₂⁽²⁾, the score of the wrong class.

3When a variable affects the loss along several paths, the effects add (the chain rule with branches). Here the two paths pull in opposite directions, and the gradient is half of what the first path alone would say. In general, a hidden unit adds up the errors of all the units it feeds, each weighted by its connection. check_numbers.py checks this against numerical derivatives.

Backpropagation

The general rule

  • Every unit \(j\) of layer \(l\) works the same way:\[z^{(l)}_j = \sum_i w^{(l)}_{ji}\, a^{(l-1)}_i + b^{(l)}_j, \qquad a^{(l)}_j = \varphi(z^{(l)}_j)\]"Error" means \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z\): how the loss changes with a unit's pre-activation.
  • Any weight: the error at its end times the activation at its start.\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w^{(l)}_{ji}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{(l)}_j} \cdot a^{(l-1)}_i\]
  • A hidden unit: its slope times the errors of the units it feeds, each times its weight.\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{(l)}_j} = \varphi'(z^{(l)}_j) \sum_k w^{(l+1)}_{kj}\, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{(l+1)}_k}\]
  • An output unit: from the loss. \(\hat{y} - y\) for regression with squared error; \(p - y\) for sigmoid or softmax with cross-entropy.

0One notation for every layer, the one from "The network is one function". a⁽⁰⁾ is the input x.

1Because zⱼ⁽ˡ⁾ is linear in wⱼᵢ⁽ˡ⁾, with coefficient aᵢ⁽ˡ⁻¹⁾. The biases are the case where that activation is 1. This is what we did for w₁₁⁽²⁾ (error at z⁽²⁾ times a₁⁽¹⁾) and for w₁₁⁽¹⁾ (error at z₁⁽¹⁾ times x₁).

2The chain rule with branches: zⱼ⁽ˡ⁾ changes aⱼ⁽ˡ⁾, which feeds every unit k of the next layer through wₖⱼ⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾, and the paths add. φ′ is the slope of the activation: a(1 − a) for the sigmoid, 1 or 0 for ReLU.

3The loss decides only where the backward pass starts. Squared error on a sigmoid output would give (p − y)p(1 − p). With more layers, apply the hidden-unit rule once per layer, from the output back. For the example's hidden unit 1: 0.1966 × (2 × (−0.325) + 1 × 0.325) = −0.064, and −0.064 × 2 = −0.128, as on "Add the paths".

Backpropagation

The algorithm

  1. Forward pass: compute and store every \(z\) and \(a\), layer by layer.
  2. Loss: compute \(\mathcal{L}\) from the output and the label.
  3. Backward pass: start at the output with \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z^{(2)}_k\) (for softmax and cross-entropy, \(p_k - y_k\)). Then go back layer by layer with the hidden-unit rule.
  4. Gradients: each weight gets the error at its end times the activation at its start.
  5. Update every weight, \(w \leftarrow w - \eta\, \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w\), using the old weights throughout.

0Run the network on the training point as usual, but keep the values: the backward pass needs the activations (for the gradients) and the pre-activations (for the slopes).

1The loss of this training point: −log pₖ for the true class with softmax and cross-entropy, ½(ŷ − y)² for regression.

2The backward pass starts with the derivative of the loss with respect to each output unit's pre-activation, the error at the output: pₖ − yₖ for softmax with cross-entropy, ŷ − y for a linear output with squared error. This is the "Add the paths" step, done once per hidden unit instead of once per weight. In the example: (−0.325 × 2 + 0.325 × 1) × 0.1966 = −0.064 for hidden unit 1. In matrix form (not on the slides) this step is (W⁽²⁾ᵀ(p − y)) ⊙ a⁽¹⁾ ⊙ (1 − a⁽¹⁾).

3For w₁₁⁽¹⁾ in the example: −0.064 × 2 = −0.128, as on the last slide.

4Compute every gradient first, then update. If you updated the output weights before the backward pass, it would use the new weights, which is wrong. The backward pass costs about as much as the forward pass, so one gradient costs about two passes through the network, however many weights there are.

Putting it together

The training loop

0Forward propagation: run the training points through the network, keeping the hidden values.

1Total error: compare the predictions with the labels. The loss is one number for the whole batch.

2Gradients: backpropagation, from the output back to the first layer.

3Update: one gradient-descent step for every weight.

4Then do it again with the new weights, until a stopping condition holds (later today). Everything that follows is about making this loop work well: the step size, the optimizer, how much data per pass, and when to stop.

Training in practice

The learning rate

  • The update:\[\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\, \nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\]Too small: slow. Too large: it overshoots. (Last class.)
  • Large steps help early; small steps help near the end.
  • A learning-rate schedule lowers \(\eta\) as training goes on.

0η = 0.1, 1 and 20 on the hours data from last class: too slow, about right, bouncing.

1Far from the minimum the gradient points roughly the right way and big steps save time. Near the minimum they make it jump back and forth.

2Three common shapes, all starting at η₀: constant; step decay, which halves η every 30 epochs here; and 1/t decay, η₀ / (1 + t/20). The numbers are examples; like η itself, they are tuned.

Training in practice

Local minima

  • Gradient descent stops in the valley it starts in.
  • Restart from several starting points; keep the lowest loss.
  • Momentum can roll through a shallow valley.
  • It happens on real networks: the XOR network from a different start stalls.

Training in practice

How much data per step?

  • Stochastic: the gradient from one point at a time. Many cheap, noisy steps.
  • Batch: from all the training points. One step per pass.
  • Mini-batch: from a few points, here 5. In between.
  • An epoch is one pass through the training data.

Training in practice

How much data per step?

  • Stochastic gradient descent (SGD): the gradient from one point at a time. Many cheap, noisy steps.
    \[\text{pick one point } i\text{:} \qquad \boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\, \nabla \mathcal{L}_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})\]
  • Batch: the gradient from all the training points. One step per pass.
    \[\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\, \nabla \Bigl(\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathcal{L}_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})\Bigr)\]
  • Mini-batch: the gradient from a few points. In between.
    \[\text{pick a set } B \text{ of } b \text{ points:} \qquad \boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\, \nabla \Bigl(\frac{1}{b} \sum_{i \in B} \mathcal{L}_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})\Bigr)\]

0The three ways again, side by side. ℒᵢ is the loss on training point i; N is the number of training points.

1The update is the same in all three; only the loss whose gradient we take changes: one point, all N points, or a mini-batch of b points. Here the loss is averaged rather than added up (last class added it up). The average only rescales the gradient, and it keeps η comparable across batch sizes. In practice the points are shuffled once per epoch and taken in order, rather than drawn at random each step.

Training in practice

When to stop

  • A maximum number of epochs; the weights stop changing; the loss is low enough.
  • The training loss keeps going down.
  • The validation loss goes down, then up: the network starts to overfit.
  • Early stopping: keep the weights from the epoch with the lowest validation loss.
  • Patience: stop once the validation loss has not improved for a set number of epochs.

Summary

Neural networks: characteristics

Strengths

  • Very flexible: one hidden layer with enough units can approximate any continuous function on a bounded region.
  • They learn their own features, so less hand-made feature design.
  • They improve with more data and computing power, and handle many outputs at once.
  • Irrelevant inputs can be ignored: training can drive their weights to zero.

Weaknesses

  • Non-convex training: the answer depends on the start, with no guarantee of the best weights.
  • Many choices to tune: layers, units, \(\eta\), optimizer, epochs.
  • They need a lot of data, overfit small data sets, and are hard to interpret.
  • Training is time-consuming: many passes over the data, and many runs to tune the choices above.

Takeaways

Backpropagation

The chain rule, from the output back. At the output, \(p_k - y_k\); a hidden unit adds up the paths through every output it feeds. Each weight's gradient is the error at its end times the value at its start.

Optimizers

Learning-rate schedules and momentum.

Batches

Batch, mini-batch, stochastic. An epoch is one pass through the data.

Watch the validation loss; stop early.

Bonus

Every path from one weight

  • Three hidden layers of 3 units, two outputs. Which paths lead from \(w^{(1)}_{11}\) to the loss?
  • \(w^{(1)}_{11}\) changes only \(a^{(1)}_1\).
  • \(a^{(1)}_1\) feeds all 3 units of layer 2: 3 paths.
  • Each of those feeds all 3 units of layer 3: \(3 \times 3 = 9\) paths.
  • Each of those feeds both outputs: \(9 \times 2 = 18\) paths to \(\mathcal{L}\). The chain rule adds 18 products.
  • Backpropagation never lists the paths. The hidden-unit rule adds them up one layer at a time, so the work grows with the number of weights, not the number of paths.

0The same question as "Add the paths", one layer deeper on each side.

1The first link of every path.

2Each path through a different unit of layer 2 contributes its own product.

3The number of paths multiplies by the width of each layer.

4With L layers of width n, a weight in the first layer has about n^(L−1) paths to the loss: exponential in depth. Written out path by path, the derivative would be hopeless for a real network.

5The errors ∂ℒ/∂z of layer 3 already contain all the paths from layer 3 on; layer 2 reuses them, and layer 1 reuses layer 2's. Each weight is touched once in the backward pass, which is why it costs about as much as the forward pass.
