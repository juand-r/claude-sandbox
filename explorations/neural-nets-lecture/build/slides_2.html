Part 2: training a network.

While people settle in. Last class we built the pile: layers of linear algebra with a nonlinearity in between. Today is about the stirring: backpropagation tells us which way to move every weight, and the rest of the lecture is about doing that well.

Last class we built the network and trained it with gradient descent, taking the gradient as given. Today: where the gradient comes from, and how training is done in practice.

Last time

Forward propagation

\[\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{h} &= \sigma(\boldsymbol{W}^{(1)} \boldsymbol{x} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(1)}) \\ \boldsymbol{z} &= \boldsymbol{W}^{(2)} \boldsymbol{h} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(2)} \\ \boldsymbol{p} &= \operatorname{softmax}(\boldsymbol{z}) \end{aligned}\]
  • Train by gradient descent:\[\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\, \nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\]
  • We need \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w\) for every weight \(w\). Where does it come from?

The network from last time: a hidden layer of sigmoids, then a softmax. The loss is the cross-entropy, −log of the probability of the true class, summed over the training data. θ is all the weights and biases together. The XOR network had 17 of them; real networks have millions. One partial derivative per weight. Writing out the loss as one formula and differentiating it by hand does not scale. Backpropagation is a way to get all of them at once, for about the cost of one more pass through the network.

Backpropagation

The network is one formula

A small network for regression: two inputs, two sigmoid hidden units, one linear output. Written out in full:

\[\hat{y} = v_1\,\sigma(w_{11}x_1 + w_{12}x_2 + b_1) + v_2\,\sigma(w_{21}x_1 + w_{22}x_2 + b_2) + c\]

The loss of one training point, squared error:

\[\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12 (\hat{y} - y)^2\]

Nine weights, all inside one nested formula. We need \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w\) for each one.

0The network we will use on the next slides. Inside: each hidden unit is a sigmoid of a weighted sum of the inputs, with its own bias b₁ or b₂. Outside: the prediction ŷ is a weighted sum of the hidden units, with bias c. No activation at the output, because ŷ can be any number.

1Squared error, as in last year's slides and the practice exam. Put the first formula in for ŷ and the loss is one long expression in the nine weights.

2Differentiating this directly, weight by weight, is possible but messy, and it gets worse with every layer. The chain rule takes it apart one piece at a time, and the pieces can be reused.

Backpropagation

A worked example

  • One training point: \(\boldsymbol{x} = (2, 1)\), target \(y = 2\). The weights are on the connections. The output is linear: \(\hat{y} = z\).
  • Hidden layer:\[\begin{aligned} a_1 &= -0.5 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 1 + 1 = 1 \\ h_1 &= \sigma(1) = 0.731 \\ a_2 &= 0.5 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1 - 0.5 = -0.5 \\ h_2 &= \sigma(-0.5) = 0.378 \end{aligned}\]
  • Output:\[\hat{y} = z = 2 \cdot 0.731 + 0.5 \cdot 0.378 - 1 = 0.651\]
  • Loss:\[\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12 (0.651 - 2)^2 = 0.910\]

0The network from the last slide, with numbers. wⱼᵢ is the weight from input i to hidden unit j, as in class 1; b₁, b₂ and c are the biases. A regression problem: the target is a number, here 2. z is the output unit's weighted sum; with a linear output, the prediction ŷ is z itself.

1Each hidden unit: weighted sum of the inputs plus its bias, then the sigmoid. Three decimals is enough: these numbers were chosen so that rounding at every step gives the same answers as the exact computation.

2The output: the same weighted sum, with the hidden values as inputs, and no activation.

3The network says 0.651; the target is 2. To lower the loss, ŷ should go up. Which way should each weight move, and by how much? That is what the gradient tells us.

Backpropagation

An output weight: \(v_1\)

  • \(v_1\) changes the loss along the highlighted path, \(v_1 \to z \to \hat{y} \to \mathcal{L}\). The chain rule multiplies the derivatives along it:\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial v_1} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z} \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial v_1}\]
  • \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial \hat{y} = \hat{y} - y = 0.651 - 2 = -1.349\)
  • \(\partial \hat{y}/\partial z = 1\)The output is linear, \(\hat{y} = z\).
  • \(\partial z/\partial v_1 = h_1 = 0.731\)Because \(z = v_1 h_1 + v_2 h_2 + c\).
  • Multiply. Keep the first two factors for later:\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z} &= -1.349 \times 1 = -1.349 \\ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial v_1} &= -1.349 \times 0.731 = -0.986 \end{aligned}\]

0The same network. Only one path leads from v₁ to the loss. Each link of the path contributes one factor.

1The derivative of ½(ŷ − y)² with respect to ŷ. The ½ is there so that this comes out without a 2.

2Trivial here, but keep it in mind: when the output has an activation, this factor is its slope.

3z is linear in v₁, and v₁ multiplies h₁. If h₁ were near 0, v₁ would barely matter for this point.

4Negative: increasing v₁ lowers the loss. ∂ℒ/∂z is the error at the output. Every weight further back goes through z, so this number is reused. The bias c has ∂z/∂c = 1, so ∂ℒ/∂c = −1.349.

Backpropagation

A hidden weight: \(w_{11}\)

  • The path is longer: \(w_{11} \to a_1 \to h_1 \to z \to \hat{y} \to \mathcal{L}\).\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{11}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z} \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial h_1} \cdot \frac{\partial h_1}{\partial a_1} \cdot \frac{\partial a_1}{\partial w_{11}}\]
  • \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z = -1.349\), already computed for \(v_1\). Reuse it.
  • The new factors:\[\begin{aligned} \partial z/\partial h_1 &= v_1 = 2 \\ \partial h_1/\partial a_1 &= h_1(1 - h_1) = 0.731 \times 0.269 = 0.1966 \\ \partial a_1/\partial w_{11} &= x_1 = 2 \end{aligned}\]
  • Multiply:\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{11}} = -1.349 \times 2 \times 0.1966 \times 2 = -1.061\]With another activation \(\varphi\), use \(\varphi'(a_1)\) in place of \(h_1(1 - h_1)\).

0A weight in the first layer. a₁ is hidden unit 1's weighted sum before the sigmoid, so h₁ = σ(a₁). The path goes through the output, so its first factors are the ones we just computed.

1This is why it is called backpropagation: we start at the output and work backwards, and each step reuses the one after it. Nothing is computed twice.

2One factor per link: v₁ because z = v₁h₁ + …; the sigmoid's slope at hidden unit 1; and x₁ because a₁ = w₁₁x₁ + w₁₂x₂ + b₁. The slope is written with four decimals: with three (0.197) the product would come out −1.063 instead of −1.061. Only the middle factor depends on the activation function; for ReLU it would be 1, since a₁ > 0.

3Also negative: increasing w₁₁ raises h₁, which raises z and ŷ, which lowers the loss. The other weights work the same way; for w₁₂ the last factor is x₂ = 1 instead of x₁ = 2.

Backpropagation

Update the weights

  • Gradient descent, with learning rate \(\eta = 0.1\):\[w \leftarrow w - \eta\, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w}\]
  • \(v_1 \leftarrow 2 - 0.1 \times (-0.986) = 2.099\)
    \(w_{11} \leftarrow -0.5 - 0.1 \times (-1.061) = -0.394\)
  • Do the same for all nine weights, with every gradient computed from the old weights. Then the next training point.

0The update from class 1: step against the gradient.

1Both gradients were negative, so both weights go up.

2Compute all the gradients first, then update; otherwise later gradients would use some new weights. After one step on all nine weights, the loss on this point goes from 0.910 to 0.489 (check_numbers.py).

Backpropagation

Now predict a class

  • Same network and point, but the target is now a class label, \(y = 1\). Put a sigmoid on the output, so it is a probability: \(p = \sigma(z) = \sigma(0.651) = 0.657\).
  • Keep squared error:\[\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12 (p - y)^2 = \tfrac12 (0.657 - 1)^2 = 0.059\]
  • One new factor, the sigmoid's slope, in place of \(\partial\hat{y}/\partial z = 1\):\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z} &= \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial p} \cdot \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = (p - y) \cdot p(1 - p) \\ &= -0.343 \times 0.225 = -0.077 \end{aligned}\]
  • The rest of the chain is the same as for regression.

Backpropagation

Now with cross-entropy

  • Same classifier, same point, same forward pass: \(p = 0.657\). Only the loss changes:\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L} &= -y \log p - (1 - y) \log(1 - p) \\ &= -\log 0.657 = 0.420 \end{aligned}\]
  • So only the first factor changes:\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial p} = -\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - p} = -\frac{1}{0.657} = -1.522\]
  • Times \(\partial p/\partial z = p(1 - p)\), it simplifies:\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z} = -y(1 - p) + (1 - y)p = p - y = -0.343\]
  • The rest of the chain is the same as before.

0The classifier from the last slide. Cross-entropy from class 1, for two classes with one sigmoid output. With y = 1 only the first term is left. Nothing in the forward pass changes.

1The derivative of the log terms. With y = 1 it is −1/p.

2Multiply out: −y(1 − p) + (1 − y)p; the y·p terms cancel and leave p − y, the prediction minus the label. The sigmoid's slope p(1 − p) cancels. The same is true for softmax with C classes: ∂ℒ/∂zₖ = pₖ − yₖ.

3Every gradient is about 4.4 times larger here (1/0.225), because squared error keeps the factor p(1 − p) = 0.225. That factor is small whenever p is near 0 or 1, even when the network is confidently wrong: at p = 0.01 with y = 1, squared error gives ∂ℒ/∂z = −0.010, while cross-entropy gives −0.99. This is one reason classifiers are trained with cross-entropy.

Backpropagation

Two outputs

  • Same input and first layer, so \(h_1 = 0.731\), \(h_2 = 0.378\). Now two outputs, a softmax and cross-entropy. The label is class 1: \(\boldsymbol{y} = (1, 0)\).\(v_{kj}\) is the weight from \(h_j\) to \(z_k\).
  • The scores:\[\begin{aligned} z_1 &= 2 \cdot 0.731 + 0.5 \cdot 0.378 - 1 = 0.651 \\ z_2 &= 1 \cdot 0.731 + 0.5 \cdot 0.378 - 1 = -0.080 \end{aligned}\]
  • The softmax and the loss:\[\begin{aligned} p_1 &= \frac{e^{0.651}}{e^{0.651} + e^{-0.080}} = \frac{1.917}{1.917 + 0.923} = 0.675 \\ p_2 &= 0.325, \qquad \mathcal{L} = -\log 0.675 = 0.393 \end{aligned}\]
  • At each output, as before, \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z_k = p_k - y_k\):\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_1} = 0.675 - 1 = -0.325, \quad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_2} = 0.325 - 0 = 0.325\]

0The network from class 1's softmax slides, at its smallest: two classes, one score each. Output 1 keeps the weights of the one-output example (2, 0.5 and bias −1), so z₁ is the same as z before.

1Each score is a weighted sum of the hidden values plus a bias.

2The softmax turns the scores into probabilities; they add up to 1. The loss is −log of the probability of the true class, class 1.

3The softmax version of p − y, from the last slide and from class 1's bonus slide. One number per output: the network gives class 1 too little probability (negative) and class 2 too much (positive). With two classes the two always add up to 0.

Backpropagation

Add the paths

  • \(w_{11}\) changes \(h_1\), and \(h_1\) feeds both outputs. So \(w_{11}\) reaches the loss along two paths.
  • Through \(z_1\):\[\begin{aligned} &(p_1 - y_1) \cdot v_{11} \cdot h_1(1 - h_1) \cdot x_1 \\ &= -0.325 \times 2 \times 0.1966 \times 2 = -0.256 \end{aligned}\]
  • Through \(z_2\):\[\begin{aligned} &(p_2 - y_2) \cdot v_{21} \cdot h_1(1 - h_1) \cdot x_1 \\ &= 0.325 \times 1 \times 0.1966 \times 2 = 0.128 \end{aligned}\]
  • Add them:\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{11}} &= \sum_k (p_k - y_k)\, v_{k1} \cdot h_1(1 - h_1) \cdot x_1 \\ &= -0.256 + 0.128 = -0.128 \end{aligned}\]

0Last year's slides showed the same picture: one hidden weight, two highlighted paths.

1The same four factors as the one-output example: the error at the output, the weight from h₁ to that output, the sigmoid's slope at h₁, and x₁.

2The other path. Only the first two factors change. It is positive: raising h₁ also raises z₂, the score of the wrong class.

3When a variable affects the loss along several paths, the effects add (the chain rule with several variables). Here the two paths pull in opposite directions, and the gradient is half of what the first path alone would say. In general, a hidden unit adds up the errors of all the outputs it feeds, each weighted by its connection. check_numbers.py checks this against numerical derivatives.

Backpropagation

The recipe

  1. Forward pass: compute and store \(\boldsymbol{h}\) and \(\boldsymbol{p}\).
  2. Error at each output: \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z_k = p_k - y_k\).
  3. Backward pass: each hidden unit adds up the errors of the outputs it feeds, then multiplies by its slope:\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a_j} = \Bigl(\sum_k (p_k - y_k)\, v_{kj}\Bigr) \cdot h_j(1 - h_j)\]With another activation, use \(\varphi'(a_j)\). With more layers, repeat, layer by layer.
  4. Gradients: each weight gets the error at its end times the value at its start: \((p_k - y_k)\, h_j\) for \(v_{kj}\), \((\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a_j)\, x_i\) for \(w_{ji}\). A bias's start value is 1.
  5. Update every weight, \(w \leftarrow w - \eta\, \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w\), using the old weights throughout.

0Run the network on the training point as usual, but keep the hidden values: the backward pass needs them.

1Prediction minus label, one number per output (softmax and cross-entropy).

2This is the "add the paths" step, done once per hidden unit instead of once per weight. aⱼ is hidden unit j's weighted sum before the activation. In the example: (−0.325 × 2 + 0.325 × 1) × 0.1966 = −0.064 for hidden unit 1. In matrix form (not on the slides) this step is (W⁽²⁾ᵀ(p − y)) ⊙ h ⊙ (1 − h).

3For w₁₁ in the example: −0.064 × 2 = −0.128, as on the last slide.

4Compute every gradient first, then update. If you updated the output weights before the backward pass, it would use the new weights, which is wrong. The backward pass costs about as much as the forward pass, so one gradient costs about two passes through the network, however many weights there are.

Putting it together

The training loop

0Forward propagation: run the training points through the network, keeping the hidden values.

1Total error: compare the predictions with the labels. The loss is one number for the whole batch.

2Gradients: backpropagation, from the output back to the first layer.

3Update: one gradient-descent step for every weight.

4Then do it again with the new weights, until a stopping condition holds (later today). Everything that follows is about making this loop work well: the step size, the optimizer, how much data per pass, and when to stop.

Training in practice

The learning rate

  • The update:\[\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\, \nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\]Too small: slow. Too large: it overshoots. (Last class.)
  • Large steps help early; small steps help near the end.
  • A learning-rate schedule lowers \(\eta\) as training goes on.

0η = 0.1, 1 and 20 on the hours data from last class: too slow, about right, bouncing.

1Far from the minimum the gradient points roughly the right way and big steps save time. Near the minimum they make it jump back and forth.

2Three common shapes, all starting at η₀: constant; step decay, which halves η every 30 epochs here; and 1/t decay, η₀ / (1 + t/20). The numbers are examples; like η itself, they are tuned.

Training in practice

Momentum

  • A long, narrow valley: plain gradient descent zigzags across it.
  • Keep a running sum of past gradients:\[\boldsymbol{v} \leftarrow \beta \boldsymbol{v} + \nabla\mathcal{L}, \quad \boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\, \boldsymbol{v}\]
  • The zigzags cancel; the steady downhill part adds up.

Training in practice

How much data per step?

  • Batch: the gradient from all the training points. One step per pass.
  • Stochastic: from one point at a time. Many cheap, noisy steps.
  • Mini-batch: from a few points, here 5. In between.
  • An epoch is one pass through the training data.

Training in practice

When to stop

  • A maximum number of epochs; the weights stop changing; the loss is low enough.
  • The training loss keeps going down.
  • The validation loss goes down, then up: the network starts to overfit.
  • Early stopping: keep the weights from the epoch with the lowest validation loss.
  • Patience: stop once the validation loss has not improved for a set number of epochs.

Summary

Neural networks: characteristics

Strengths

  • Very flexible: one hidden layer with enough units can approximate any continuous function on a bounded region.
  • They learn their own features, so less hand-made feature design.
  • They improve with more data and computing power, and handle many outputs at once.
  • Irrelevant inputs can be ignored: training can drive their weights to zero.

Weaknesses

  • Non-convex training: the answer depends on the start, with no guarantee of the best weights.
  • Many choices to tune: layers, units, \(\eta\), optimizer, epochs.
  • They need a lot of data, overfit small data sets, and are hard to interpret.
  • Training is time-consuming: many passes over the data, and many runs to tune the choices above.

Where this goes

The family tree

0Rosenblatt's perceptron, 1958. The multilayer perceptron trained with backpropagation, popularized by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams in 1986. That is what we covered.

1Two branches that build structure into the network. Convolutional networks for images: LeCun and colleagues, 1989, the same small weights reused across the image. Recurrent networks for sequences: Elman, 1990, a hidden layer that feeds back into itself. LSTM, Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997, and the GRU, Cho and colleagues, 2014, make recurrent networks trainable on long sequences.

2Attention: Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio, 2014, an encoder–decoder built on GRU units for translation. The Transformer, Vaswani and colleagues, 2017, keeps the attention and drops the recurrence. Every Transformer layer also contains an MLP like ours. Next lecture. The tree is simplified: these ideas have more parents than shown.

Takeaways

Backpropagation

The chain rule, from the output back. At the output, \(p_k - y_k\); a hidden unit adds up the paths through every output it feeds. Each weight's gradient is the error at its end times the value at its start.

Optimizers

Learning-rate schedules and momentum.

Batches

Batch, mini-batch, stochastic. An epoch is one pass through the data.

Stopping

Watch the validation loss; stop early.
