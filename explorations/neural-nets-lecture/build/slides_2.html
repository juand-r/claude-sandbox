Part 2: training a network.

Last class we built the network and trained it with gradient descent, taking the gradient as given. Today: where the gradient comes from, and how training is done in practice.

Last time

Forward propagation

\[\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{h} &= \sigma(\boldsymbol{W}^{(1)} \boldsymbol{x} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(1)}) \\ \boldsymbol{z} &= \boldsymbol{W}^{(2)} \boldsymbol{h} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(2)} \\ \boldsymbol{p} &= \operatorname{softmax}(\boldsymbol{z}) \end{aligned}\]
  • Train by gradient descent:\[\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\, \nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\]
  • We need \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w\) for every weight \(w\). Where does it come from?

The network from last time: a hidden layer of sigmoids, then a softmax. The loss is the cross-entropy, −log of the probability of the true class, summed over the training data. θ is all the weights and biases together. The XOR network had 17 of them; real networks have millions. One partial derivative per weight. Writing out the loss as one formula and differentiating it by hand does not scale. Backpropagation is a way to get all of them at once, for about the cost of one more pass through the network.

Backpropagation

The network is one formula

A small network: two inputs, two hidden units, one output. Written out in full:

\[p = \sigma\Bigl(v_1\,\sigma(w_{11}x_1 + w_{12}x_2 + b_1) + v_2\,\sigma(w_{21}x_1 + w_{22}x_2 + b_2) + c\Bigr)\]

The loss of one training point, here squared error:

\[\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12 (p - y)^2\]

Nine weights, all inside one nested formula. We need \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w\) for each one.

0The same network we will use on the next slides. Inside: each hidden unit is a sigmoid of a weighted sum of the inputs, with its own bias b₁ or b₂. Outside: the output is a sigmoid of a weighted sum of the hidden units, with bias c.

1Squared error, with y = 1 or 0, as in last year's slides; we will redo the example with cross-entropy afterwards. Put the first formula in for p and the loss is one long expression in the nine weights.

2Differentiating this directly, weight by weight, is possible but messy, and it gets worse with every layer. The chain rule takes it apart one piece at a time, and the pieces can be reused.

Backpropagation

A worked example

  • One training point: \(\boldsymbol{x} = (2, 1)\), label \(y = 1\). The weights are on the connections. Sigmoid units; squared error \(\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12 (p - y)^2\).
  • Hidden layer:\[\begin{aligned} a_1 &= -0.5 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 1 + 1 = 1 \\ h_1 &= \sigma(1) = 0.731 \\ a_2 &= 0.5 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1 - 0.5 = -0.5 \\ h_2 &= \sigma(-0.5) = 0.378 \end{aligned}\]
  • Output:\[\begin{aligned} z &= 2 \cdot 0.731 + 0.5 \cdot 0.378 - 1 = 0.651 \\ p &= \sigma(0.651) = 0.657 \end{aligned}\]
  • Loss:\[\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12 (0.657 - 1)^2 = 0.059\]

0The network from the last slide, with numbers. wⱼᵢ is the weight from input i to hidden unit j, as in class 1; b₁, b₂ and c are the biases. The output is a sigmoid, so p is between 0 and 1, and the label is 1. We start with squared error, as in last year's slides and the practice exam; cross-entropy comes after.

1Each hidden unit: weighted sum of the inputs plus its bias, then the sigmoid. Three decimals is enough: these numbers were chosen so that rounding to three decimals at every step gives the same answers as the exact computation.

2The output: the same thing, with the hidden values as inputs.

3The network says 0.657; the label is 1. To lower the loss, p should go up. Which way should each weight move, and by how much? That is what the gradient tells us.

Backpropagation

An output weight: \(v_1\)

  • \(v_1\) changes the loss along the highlighted path, \(v_1 \to z \to p \to \mathcal{L}\). The chain rule multiplies the derivatives along it:\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial v_1} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial p} \cdot \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial v_1}\]
  • \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial p = p - y = 0.657 - 1 = -0.343\)
  • \(\partial p/\partial z = p(1 - p) = 0.225\)\(0.657 \times 0.343\). The sigmoid's slope.
  • \(\partial z/\partial v_1 = h_1 = 0.731\)Because \(z = v_1 h_1 + v_2 h_2 + c\).
  • Multiply. Keep the first two factors for later:\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z} &= -0.343 \times 0.225 = -0.077 \\ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial v_1} &= -0.077 \times 0.731 = -0.056 \end{aligned}\]

0The same network. Only one path leads from v₁ to the loss. Each link of the path contributes one factor.

1The derivative of ½(p − y)² with respect to p. The ½ is there so that this comes out without a 2.

2The derivative of the sigmoid is σ(z)(1 − σ(z)), which is p(1 − p). It is at most 0.25, at p = 0.5.

3z is linear in v₁, and v₁ multiplies h₁. If h₁ were near 0, v₁ would barely matter for this point.

4Negative: increasing v₁ lowers the loss. ∂ℒ/∂z is the error at the output, after the sigmoid. Every weight further back goes through z, so this number is reused. The bias c has ∂z/∂c = 1, so ∂ℒ/∂c = −0.077.

Backpropagation

A hidden weight: \(w_{11}\)

  • The path is longer: \(w_{11} \to a_1 \to h_1 \to z \to p \to \mathcal{L}\).\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{11}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z} \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial h_1} \cdot \frac{\partial h_1}{\partial a_1} \cdot \frac{\partial a_1}{\partial w_{11}}\]
  • \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z = -0.077\), already computed for \(v_1\). Reuse it.
  • The new factors:\[\begin{aligned} \partial z/\partial h_1 &= v_1 = 2 \\ \partial h_1/\partial a_1 &= h_1(1 - h_1) = 0.731 \times 0.269 = 0.197 \\ \partial a_1/\partial w_{11} &= x_1 = 2 \end{aligned}\]
  • Multiply:\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{11}} = -0.077 \times 2 \times 0.197 \times 2 = -0.061\]With another activation \(\varphi\), use \(\varphi'(a_1)\) in place of \(h_1(1 - h_1)\).

0A weight in the first layer. a₁ is hidden unit 1's weighted sum before the sigmoid, so h₁ = σ(a₁). The path goes through the output, so its first factors are the ones we just computed.

1This is why it is called backpropagation: we start at the output and work backwards, and each step reuses the one after it. Nothing is computed twice.

2One factor per link: v₁ because z = v₁h₁ + …; the sigmoid's slope at hidden unit 1; and x₁ because a₁ = w₁₁x₁ + w₁₂x₂ + b₁. Only the middle factor depends on the activation function; for ReLU it would be 1, since a₁ > 0.

3Also negative: increasing w₁₁ raises h₁, which raises z and p, which lowers the loss. The other weights work the same way; for w₁₂ the last factor is x₂ = 1 instead of x₁ = 2.

Backpropagation

Update the weights

  • Gradient descent, with learning rate \(\eta = 1\):\[w \leftarrow w - \eta\, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w}\]
  • \(v_1 \leftarrow 2 - (-0.056) = 2.056\)
    \(w_{11} \leftarrow -0.5 - (-0.061) = -0.439\)
  • Do the same for all nine weights, with every gradient computed from the old weights. Then the next training point.

0The update from class 1: step against the gradient. η = 1 keeps the arithmetic simple.

1Both gradients were negative, so both weights go up. Small changes: the gradients are small here.

2Compute all the gradients first, then update; otherwise later gradients would use some new weights. After one step on all nine weights, the loss on this point goes from 0.059 to 0.044 (check_numbers.py).

Backpropagation

Now with cross-entropy

  • Same network, same point, same forward pass: \(p = 0.657\). Only the loss changes:\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L} &= -y \log p - (1 - y) \log(1 - p) \\ &= -\log 0.657 = 0.420 \end{aligned}\]
  • So only the first factor changes:\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial p} = -\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - p} = -\frac{1}{0.657} = -1.522\]
  • Times \(\partial p/\partial z = p(1 - p)\), it simplifies:\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z} = -y(1 - p) + (1 - y)p = p - y = -0.343\]
  • The rest of the chain is the same as before.

0Cross-entropy from class 1, for two classes with one sigmoid output. With y = 1 only the first term is left. Nothing in the forward pass changes.

1The derivative of the log terms. With y = 1 it is −1/p.

2Multiply out: −y(1 − p) + (1 − y)p; the y·p terms cancel and leave p − y, the prediction minus the label. The sigmoid's slope p(1 − p) cancels. The same is true for softmax with C classes: ∂ℒ/∂zₖ = pₖ − yₖ.

3Every gradient is about 4.4 times larger here (1/0.225), because squared error keeps the factor p(1 − p) = 0.225. That factor is small whenever p is near 0 or 1, even when the network is confidently wrong: at p = 0.01 with y = 1, squared error gives ∂ℒ/∂z = −0.010, while cross-entropy gives −0.99. This is one reason classifiers are trained with cross-entropy.

Backpropagation

Several paths: add them

  • With two outputs (softmax and cross-entropy), \(h_j\) feeds both. A hidden weight reaches the loss along two paths.
  • Add the paths:\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{ji}} = \sum_k (p_k - y_k)\, v_{kj} \cdot h_j(1 - h_j) \cdot x_i\]
  • In matrix form, with \(\odot\) the entrywise product:\[\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} = \boldsymbol{p} - \boldsymbol{y}, \quad \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} = (\boldsymbol{W}^{(2)\top} \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)}) \odot \boldsymbol{h} \odot (1 - \boldsymbol{h})\]
  • Then:\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{W}^{(2)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} \boldsymbol{h}^\top, \quad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{W}^{(1)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} \boldsymbol{x}^\top\]The biases get \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\).

0Softmax with two outputs. vₖⱼ is the weight from hidden unit j to output k. A change in w₁₁ changes h₁, which changes both z₁ and z₂.

1The multivariable chain rule: when a variable affects the loss through several routes, the effects add.

2δ is the error signal at a layer: the derivative of the loss with respect to that layer's sums. δ⁽²⁾ has one entry per output; δ⁽¹⁾ has one per hidden unit. W⁽²⁾ᵀ sends the output errors back along the same edges, weighted by the same weights. h ⊙ (1 − h) is the sigmoid's slope at each hidden unit; with another activation φ it becomes φ′(a), with a = W⁽¹⁾x + b⁽¹⁾.

3Each weight's gradient is the δ at its end times the value at its start. These are for one training point; for the whole training set, add over the points. check_numbers.py compares these formulas with numerical derivatives; they agree to about 10⁻¹⁰.

Backpropagation

The recipe

  1. Forward pass: compute and store \(\boldsymbol{h}\) and \(\boldsymbol{p}\).
  2. Error at the output: \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} = \boldsymbol{p} - \boldsymbol{y}\).
  3. Backward pass: \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} = (\boldsymbol{W}^{(2)\top} \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)}) \odot \boldsymbol{h} \odot (1 - \boldsymbol{h})\).For sigmoid units. With another activation, replace \(\boldsymbol{h} \odot (1 - \boldsymbol{h})\) by \(\varphi'(\boldsymbol{a})\).
  4. Gradients: \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} \boldsymbol{h}^\top\), \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} \boldsymbol{x}^\top\), and the \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\)s for the biases.
  5. Update every weight, \(\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\, \nabla\mathcal{L}\), using the old weights throughout.

0Run the network on the training points as usual, but keep the hidden values: the backward pass needs them.

1Prediction minus label, one entry per class.

2Send the error back through W⁽²⁾, then multiply by the activation function's slope at each hidden unit (for the sigmoid, h(1 − h)). With more layers, repeat this step once per layer.

3Each weight: δ at its end times the value at its start.

4Compute every gradient first, then update. If you updated W⁽²⁾ before computing δ⁽¹⁾, δ⁽¹⁾ would use the new W⁽²⁾, which is wrong. The backward pass costs about as much as the forward pass, so one gradient costs about two passes through the network, however many weights there are.

Putting it together

The training loop

0Forward propagation: run the training points through the network, keeping the hidden values.

1Total error: compare the predictions with the labels. The loss is one number for the whole batch.

2Gradients: backpropagation, from the output back to the first layer.

3Update: one gradient-descent step for every weight.

4Then do it again with the new weights, until a stopping condition holds (later today). Everything that follows is about making this loop work well: the step size, the optimizer, how much data per pass, and when to stop.

Training in practice

The learning rate

  • The update:\[\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\, \nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\]Too small: slow. Too large: it overshoots. (Last class.)
  • Large steps help early; small steps help near the end.
  • A learning-rate schedule lowers \(\eta\) as training goes on.

0η = 0.1, 1 and 20 on the hours data from last class: too slow, about right, bouncing.

1Far from the minimum the gradient points roughly the right way and big steps save time. Near the minimum they make it jump back and forth.

2Three common shapes, all starting at η₀: constant; step decay, which halves η every 30 epochs here; and 1/t decay, η₀ / (1 + t/20). The numbers are examples; like η itself, they are tuned.

Training in practice

Momentum

  • A long, narrow valley: plain gradient descent zigzags across it.
  • Keep a running sum of past gradients:\[\boldsymbol{v} \leftarrow \beta \boldsymbol{v} + \nabla\mathcal{L}, \quad \boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\, \boldsymbol{v}\]
  • The zigzags cancel; the steady downhill part adds up.

Training in practice

How much data per step?

  • Batch: the gradient from all the training points. One step per pass.
  • Stochastic: from one point at a time. Many cheap, noisy steps.
  • Mini-batch: from a few points, here 5. In between.
  • An epoch is one pass through the training data.

Training in practice

When to stop

  • A maximum number of epochs; the weights stop changing; the loss is low enough.
  • The training loss keeps going down.
  • The validation loss goes down, then up: the network starts to overfit.
  • Early stopping: keep the weights from the epoch with the lowest validation loss.
  • Patience: stop once the validation loss has not improved for a set number of epochs.

Summary

Neural networks: characteristics

Strengths

  • Very flexible: one hidden layer with enough units can approximate any continuous function on a bounded region.
  • They learn their own features, so less hand-made feature design.
  • They improve with more data and computing power, and handle many outputs at once.
  • Irrelevant inputs can be ignored: training can drive their weights to zero.

Weaknesses

  • Non-convex training: the answer depends on the start, with no guarantee of the best weights.
  • Many choices to tune: layers, units, \(\eta\), optimizer, epochs.
  • They need a lot of data, overfit small data sets, and are hard to interpret.
  • Training is time-consuming: many passes over the data, and many runs to tune the choices above.

Where this goes

The family tree

0Rosenblatt's perceptron, 1958. The multilayer perceptron trained with backpropagation, popularized by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams in 1986. That is what we covered.

1Two branches that build structure into the network. Convolutional networks for images: LeCun and colleagues, 1989, the same small weights reused across the image. Recurrent networks for sequences: Elman, 1990, a hidden layer that feeds back into itself. LSTM, Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997, and the GRU, Cho and colleagues, 2014, make recurrent networks trainable on long sequences.

2Attention: Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio, 2014, an encoder–decoder built on GRU units for translation. The Transformer, Vaswani and colleagues, 2017, keeps the attention and drops the recurrence. Every Transformer layer also contains an MLP like ours. Next lecture. The tree is simplified: these ideas have more parents than shown.
