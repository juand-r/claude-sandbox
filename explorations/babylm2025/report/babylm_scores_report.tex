\begin{abstract}
\noindent This is a self-contained guide to interpreting BabyLM challenge scores, aimed
at someone preparing a 2026 entry who wants to know three things: which of the many
evaluation numbers actually carry signal, how a given score compares to the field, and
which of the concrete evaluation bugs reported in the 2025 papers have been fixed in the
2026 pipeline. The findings are drawn from a full reading of all 41 papers of the 2025
proceedings (\texttt{2025.babylm-main}) and from reading the actual source of the 2026
evaluation pipeline (\texttt{github.com/babylm-org/babylm-eval}). \textbf{Headline
conclusions.} (i)~Only two scores are trustworthy for ranking: \textbf{BLiMP} and
\textbf{(Super)GLUE}; the rest are at chance, at the noise floor, or were outright buggy.
(ii)~BLiMP spreads widely across systems ($\approx$49--80) and is the axis that separates
models; (Super)GLUE is compressed ($\approx$53--72) and barely reflects a $10\times$
difference in training data. (iii)~The 2026 pipeline fixes the two most damaging scoring
bugs (BLiMP tie/NaN inflation; age-of-acquisition mis-fit) and deletes the unreliable WUG
tasks, but the EWoK ``concepts-absent-from-training'' problem remains.
\end{abstract}

\section{Purpose, method, and how to read this}
\label{sec:intro}
The BabyLM Challenge asks entrants to pretrain a language model on a human-scale amount of
text --- 100M words or fewer (Strict), or 10M or fewer (Strict-Small) --- and evaluates it
on a fixed suite of linguistic and cognitive benchmarks. Because that suite reports on the
order of a dozen numbers per model, and because those numbers are then blended into a single
leaderboard rank, it is easy to over-read a score that is really noise. This report exists to
prevent that.

It answers three questions. \textbf{(1)~Which scores are meaningful?}
(Section~\ref{sec:meaningful}) --- i.e.\ reliable across seeds, discriminative between
models, construct-valid, and not stuck at chance. \textbf{(2)~What does the field look like?}
(Section~\ref{sec:spread}) --- the actual spread of the two trustworthy metrics, with
reference bands so a new score can be placed without the leaderboard.
\textbf{(3)~Which 2025 bugs did 2026 fix?} (Section~\ref{sec:bugs}) --- audited against the
real pipeline code, not a changelog (there is none).

\textbf{Method and sourcing.} The reliability verdicts come from a full reading of the 41
papers in \emph{Proceedings of the First BabyLM Workshop} (\texttt{2025.babylm-main.1}
through \texttt{.41}); the organizers' summary is \texttt{.28}. Individual papers are cited
by anthology number, e.g.\ (m.16) for \texttt{2025.babylm-main.16}; a full number$\to$identity
map is in Appendix~\ref{app:papers}, and a condensed set of the load-bearing quotes and
numbers is in Appendix~\ref{app:evidence}. The bug-fix status was established by cloning and
diffing the 2025 (\texttt{babylm/evaluation-pipeline-2025}) and 2026
(\texttt{babylm-org/babylm-eval}) pipelines. This report is intended to be read on its own.

\label{sec:setting}

\textbf{Evaluation philosophy.} Most of the suite is \emph{zero-shot}: the model assigns
(pseudo-)log-probabilities to candidate strings, and accuracy is whether it prefers the
correct one, exactly as in BLiMP minimal pairs. The organizers investigated replacing the
finetuned (Super)GLUE tasks with zero-shot prompting but concluded that robust in-context
learning does not emerge at this data scale (induction heads require billions of tokens), so
finetuning was kept. This philosophy carries into 2026, which is why the 2025 reliability
picture below is directly relevant to a 2026 entry.

\section{How a submission is scored}
\label{sec:scoring}
Every text-track model is reduced to two headline numbers and one aggregate.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{NLP score} --- an \emph{accuracy} average over the forced-choice / labelled
tasks: BLiMP, BLiMP-Supplement, EWoK, Entity Tracking, COMPS, and the finetuned
(Super)GLUE suite (BoolQ, MNLI, MRPC, MultiRC, QQP, RTE, WSC; MNLI/QQP subsampled to 10k;
CoLA, SST2, MNLI-mm, QNLI dropped as redundant).
\item \textbf{Human-likeness score} --- a \emph{correlation-to-humans} average over
reading-time prediction (surprisal vs human reading times), the two WUG morphology tasks
(model inflection preference vs the human distribution), and age-of-acquisition (model
word-learning curves vs WordBank).
\item \textbf{Macro Average} --- the leaderboard sort key.
\end{itemize}

The aggregation is a two-level unweighted mean: each task score is the mean of its subtasks,
and the macro is the mean across tasks. Checking against the organizers' results table pins
the exact structure down: \textbf{Macro $=$ mean(NLP score, Human-likeness score)} --- a flat
50/50 split (e.g.\ CLASS-IT 20.4/52.9$\to$36.6; MoEP 31.5/53.2$\to$42.3). \emph{This 50/50
split is the crux of Section~\ref{sec:meaningful}:} half the flagship number comes from the
least reliable half of the suite. (Note: some online summaries quote a 50\%/30\%/20\%
BLiMP/GLUE/MSGS weighting --- that is the \emph{2023} scheme; MSGS was not used in 2025.)

\textbf{What the field actually achieved.} Table~\ref{tab:winners} gives the 2025 track
winners against the strongest provided baseline. Two facts stand out and inform how to read a
rank. First, human-likeness and NLP were awarded separately because a system could win either;
and at the top of the largest track (Strict-Small) the two winners are near-opposites (MoEP
31.5/53.2 vs AMLM 8.4/58.3), i.e.\ the two halves are decoupled. Second, in the Strict track
\emph{no submission beat the strongest GPT-BERT baseline on the macro average} --- the awards
go to the best non-baseline entries.

\section{Which scores are meaningful?}
\label{sec:meaningful}
Table~\ref{tab:verdict} is the summary; the subsections give the evidence. ``At chance'' means
$\approx$0.50 for a two-alternative task. The verdicts are unusually well-supported because they
are \emph{convergent across many independent submissions} and, in the strongest cases, stated by
the winning teams and diagnosed mechanistically.

\subsection{The reliable core: BLiMP and (Super)GLUE}
\textbf{BLiMP} is the anchor. It rises smoothly with training --- ``BLiMP scores demonstrate a
gradual and consistent increase during training'' (m.34) --- and the Strict-Small NLP winner's
own audit, an appendix literally titled \emph{``Should we trust BabyLM Metrics?''}, concludes
that for BLiMP and (Super)GLUE the answer ``appears to be yes\,\dots\ Failed and untrained
models perform expectedly poorly on these'' (m.31). Three caveats keep it from being read at
fine granularity: a scoring artifact could inflate degenerate models in 2025
(Section~\ref{sec:bugs}); it collapses toward chance under weak compute (``BLiMP\,\dots\ hovers
44--52 across configurations,'' m.12; 48.7 for a heavily-quantized model, m.11); and it saturates
at the top, where the best models approach the human/large-LM reference.

The \textbf{BLiMP-Supplement} is weaker: several models sit near 0.50 on it (m.5), and one paper
traced a supplement ``advantage'' entirely to three subcategories containing speaker labels that
only one condition's preprocessing preserved --- a headline difference that was a data-cleaning
artifact, not model quality (m.22).

\textbf{(Super)GLUE} is the trustworthy secondary: accuracy-based, moves with model quality
(m.31), with MNLI/MRPC/RTE carrying the real signal (m.1). Its caveats are cost and patchy
sensitivity: it requires finetuning (the expensive, higher-variance stage); it barely reflects a
$10\times$ data difference (only two of seven Strict systems beat the best Strict-Small system on
GLUE); it is sometimes wholly inert (frozen at 57.7 across every configuration in m.36; $\approx$63.5
flat in m.41); and WSC/RTE carry 4--12-point seed variance, enough that m.19 dropped WSC from its
analysis. Read the aggregate, not the individual small tasks.

\subsection{Real but too noisy to rank: Entity Tracking}
Entity Tracking is the most \emph{method-sensitive} accuracy task --- multi-token prediction
(+4--5 pts, m.41), contrastive data (+7\%, m.2), and recombination (12.95$\to$19.65, m.40) all
move it --- but it is also the highest-variance metric on the board: seed SD of 6--9 points
(20.70$\pm$6.09; 31.68$\pm$8.75, m.22), mid-run collapse (``rises to 41.8 at 20M tokens before
collapsing to 13.4,'' m.34; ``drops sharply after the first epoch,'' m.41), and a barely-trained
model beating a strong GPT-BERT on it (m.36). Use it only as a seeded diagnostic for a specific
intervention, never as a ranking component.

\subsection{Broken at this scale: EWoK (and COMPS)}
\textbf{EWoK} is at chance in essentially every text-track paper that reports it: 52.7--56.7
across all sizes (m.1); 53.0--53.8 across all methods (m.2); 0.494--0.519 across 14 curricula
(m.26); ``none of the models perform better than a random guess'' (m.41). The outstanding paper
m.15 supplies the mechanism, and it is a \emph{data} problem: EWoK ``demonstrates no sensitivity
to changes in architecture or training strategy, with performance remaining around 50\%\dots'',
because in 37.7\% of EWoK items at least one tested concept appears fewer than 100 times in the
training corpus and in 13\% both concepts appear zero times --- ``the training dataset does not
properly support EWoK evaluation.'' Where EWoK \emph{does} move it is suspect: a morpheme tokenizer
pushes it to 67--71, which the authors themselves call ``surprising, as EWoK measures basic world
knowledge rather than a linguistic task'' (m.21), i.e.\ a segmentation/log-probability artifact.
\textbf{COMPS} shares the floor problem (at chance for weak models, m.14,m.22) though strong
baselines reach 56--60. Do not optimize either, and do not read small differences.

\subsection{Noise floor or buggy: the human-likeness half}
\textbf{Reading-time prediction} has near-zero magnitude and is insensitive to model quality:
the winners' audit found the numbers ``stay relatively similar, regardless of the model'' (m.31);
``the correlations of all models are below 0.1'' (m.32). Values sit at 0.0--0.1 partial-$R^2$,
flip sign when the tokenizer changes (m.41), and can perversely favour \emph{smaller} models
(m.30,m.17).

\textbf{WUG} (past-tense and adjective-nomination) is unreliable in several independent ways.
There was an outright evaluation bug --- ``a bug was discovered in the evaluation for the WuG
task\,\dots\ we therefore exclude this task'' (m.14). Under a morpheme tokenizer every configuration
scores \emph{exactly} 100.00, a normalization artifact (m.21). The past-tense correlation routinely
goes strongly \emph{negative} and sign-flips across near-identical models ($-20.7$ vs $+37.5$, m.14;
$-24$ to $+2$ with SD up to $\pm$10, m.22). And the adjective task is gameable: ``one could obtain
quite high scores on adjective nominalization, around 78, just from initialization, no training
required'' (m.31).

\textbf{Age-of-Acquisition} was, in 2025, the most dangerous number on the board --- it was
\emph{miscomputed}. m.29 inspected the pipeline: ``only very few data points (1--5 words) are
considered\,\dots\ due to an unpassed condition on the parameters of the fitted sigmoid function
\,\dots\ Limited data points lead to either a score of zero or a strong negative correlation; hence,
these results can be misleading.'' This explains the wild spread (mostly $-0.07$ to $0$ with lone
outliers, m.34; literal $0.00$ at $p{=}1.0$, m.40; $-79.6$, m.29). It was also inflated by
broken/untrained runs (m.31). \emph{This specific bug is fixed in 2026} (Section~\ref{sec:bugs}).

\subsection{Why the aggregate is not a safe ranking}
The individual-task problems would matter less if they averaged out. They do not, because the
macro weights the broken half at 50\% and the noisiest tasks have the widest ranges. The decisive
demonstration (m.31): a team accidentally trained a model with too-low a batch size, spiking the
loss; it ``performs expectedly poorly on\,\dots\ BLiMP and (Super)GLUE'' but ``remarkably well
on\,\dots\ EWoK, adjective nominalization, COMPS, and AoA,'' and its aggregate \emph{beat every
properly-trained model}. Their verdict: ``the final scoring metric seems to be unfairly skewed.''
Relatedly, one real award (Strict-Small human-likeness, MoEP, m.39) turned almost entirely on the
miscomputed AoA task: its AoA (53.70) was 4--14$\times$ any baseline's, while on BLiMP it was
\emph{below} every baseline. Run-to-run macro SD is $\approx$1--2 points (m.14) --- larger than many
ranking gaps --- and cross-metric non-transfer is pervasive (grammar and knowledge/reasoning move
independently or oppositely under the same intervention: m.1,m.2,m.5,m.11,m.12), so no single scalar
is honest. Collapsing the metrics ``would obscure these distinctions and reduce interpretability''
(m.13).

\textbf{BLiMP tie/NaN (fixed).} In 2025, \texttt{rank\_and\_evaluate} chose the argmax candidate with
\texttt{torch.max(...)[1]}, which returns the \emph{first} index on ties and lets NaNs create spurious
maxima; an order-invariant or degenerate model could therefore be scored as correct, and a dummy model
could report 100.0. The 2026 code first maps NaN to $-\infty$ and then breaks ties \emph{randomly}, so a
model that cannot distinguish the minimal pair now scores at chance. It does \emph{not} remove the 22
paradigms whose two sentences are permutations of one another --- they are still in the data snapshot;
they simply no longer inflate a degenerate model.

\textbf{Age-of-Acquisition (fixed).} The 2025 model-side sigmoid fit was unbounded and capped at
\texttt{maxfev=1000}; on many clean learning curves the shape parameter diverged and the word was
discarded, leaving the correlation computed over a handful of survivors --- exactly the ``unpassed
condition on the parameters of the fitted sigmoid'' that m.29 diagnosed. The 2026 fit is \emph{bounded},
uses $20\times$ more evaluations, averages surprisals within each checkpoint, corrects the chance-surprisal
ceiling, and gates on a $p$-value. One caveat: the 2026 strict \textsc{readme} reports AoA as an MSE-like
quantity computed server-side, so the exact headline number may differ from the repository's
Pearson-correlation code; only the repo-side fit could be verified here.

\textbf{WUG (removed).} Rather than repair the morphological WUG tasks, 2026 deletes them --- the scoring
routine, the task dispatch, and the collation entries are all gone. The ``WUG${=}100$'' artifact and the
sign-flipping correlations are therefore moot, but so is any WUG signal.

\textbf{EWoK (not fixed).} The reason EWoK sits at chance is that $\approx$13\% of its items use concepts
absent from the training corpus (m.15) --- a property of the \emph{data}, and the 2026 filter and
vocabulary files are byte-for-byte identical to 2025. Recent 2026 commits fixed how EWoK pairs are
\emph{constructed and scored} (two context-based minimal pairs; a prediction/target matching fix), which
improves correctness, but the coverage problem is untouched. Treat EWoK the same in 2026 as in 2025:
near-uninformative at this scale.

\textbf{Other changes worth knowing.} The 2026 strict zero-shot suite keeps BLiMP, BLiMP-Supplement, EWoK,
Entity Tracking, COMPS, reading-time and AoA, \emph{drops} both WUG tasks, and \emph{adds} GlobalPIQA
(length-normalized). Fine-tuning keeps the same seven (Super)GLUE tasks. A new Multilingual track
(English/Dutch/Chinese) adds MultiBLiMP, Hanzi structure/pinyin minimal pairs, and MECO reading-time.
The Macro Average is computed server-side in both years, so the exact 2026 weighting cannot be read from
the pipeline code; but the \emph{composition} of the two halves changed --- the human-likeness side lost
WUG, the accuracy side gained GlobalPIQA.

\section{What the BLiMP and (Super)GLUE spread looks like}
\label{sec:spread}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering

\caption{BLiMP versus (Super)GLUE for a cross-section of 2025 systems. \textbf{(A)}~Each point is one
system; colour is the track; the star is the official GPT-BERT baseline. \textbf{(B)}~The same values as
a one-dimensional spread per metric (black bar $=$ mean, whisker $=$ min--max). BLiMP ranges

\label{fig:spread}

Three things stand out, and they are what matter for judging a new score.

\textbf{BLiMP is the metric that separates systems.} Its range here is about 31 points (49 to 80),
standard deviation near 9.5. A one-point BLiMP difference low down is meaningful; near the top it is not,
because the strongest models approach the reference and the metric saturates.

\textbf{(Super)GLUE is compressed and scale-insensitive.} Its range is about 19 points (53 to 72),
standard deviation near 6. Only two of seven Strict (100M-word) systems beat the best Strict-Small
(10M-word) system on GLUE --- a metric that barely reflects a $10\times$ data difference cannot finely
rank models that differ by far less. Read GLUE as a coarse band, not a ranking.

\textbf{The two are weakly correlated, so ``good'' is metric-specific.} Systems with strong BLiMP and
mediocre GLUE (Once Upon a Time: 77.5/53) and the reverse (Batchwise-Convergent: 49/58) both exist. A
single system can look strong on one axis and ordinary on the other; the baseline (GPT-BERT) is the rare
point at the top of both.

\subsection{Reference bands for placing your own score}
Table~\ref{tab:bands} gives approximate, track-dependent anchors that let a new score be judged without the
leaderboard. To decide whether a submission is good, compare its \textbf{BLiMP} against the GPT-BERT
baseline for its track ($\approx$80 for Strict, high-60s to low-70s for Strict-Small) --- that is the
number that most cleanly says ``ahead of'' or ``behind'' the field --- and treat \textbf{GLUE} as a wide
competitive band. Do \emph{not} use EWoK or the human-likeness tasks to decide whether a model is good;
small movements there are noise.

\section{Recommendations for a 2026 entry}
\label{sec:rec}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Optimize BLiMP $+$ BLiMP-Supplement first}, via the levers that actually moved them in 2025:
training objective and architecture (diffusion MLM m.38, adaptive masking m.31, GPT-BERT-style dual
objective, multi-token prediction m.41). It is the most reliable, most monotone, cheapest (zero-shot) signal.
\item \textbf{Treat GLUE as the secondary target}; budget for finetuning variance; report the aggregate, not
WSC/RTE/MultiRC individually.
\item \textbf{Do not optimize EWoK, COMPS, or the human-likeness tasks for their own sake.} If you compete on
human-likeness, note the award can be captured by a targeted push on one idiosyncratic task, but any single
such score is fragile.
\item \textbf{Use Entity Tracking as a diagnostic, not a target}, and only with multiple seeds and confidence
intervals.
\item \textbf{Report per-task, with seeds and confidence intervals}, following the best-practice examples in
the field: m.2 (10 seeds, bootstrap CIs) and m.22 (4 seeds, per-task SDs). A single-seed macro number is not
evidence that one model is better than another.
\item \textbf{Verify the pipeline before trusting a number} --- the BLiMP tie-handling and AoA fit were both
buggy in 2025 and both are worth re-checking in the 2026 code you actually run.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Bottom line}
The trustworthy scores are BLiMP (read coarsely, compared to the track baseline) and (Super)GLUE (a coarse,
compressed band). EWoK is at chance, the human-likeness tasks are at the noise floor or were buggy, and the
2025 Macro Average was unsafe enough that a broken training run topped it. The 2026 pipeline is genuinely
cleaner: the two most damaging scoring bugs (BLiMP tie/NaN, AoA mis-fit) are fixed and the unreliable WUG
tasks are gone; the one substantive survivor is EWoK's data-coverage problem. Judge a submission by its BLiMP
relative to the baseline, corroborate with GLUE, and read everything else with the scepticism the 2025
submissions themselves recommended.

\section{Selected evidence (load-bearing quotes and numbers)}
\label{app:evidence}
{\small
\textbf{BLiMP tie artifact (m.16).} ``In 22 of the 67 BLiMP subtasks, the two sentences\,\dots\ are
permutations of the same multiset of words\,\dots\ ties are counted as correct, which inflates accuracy.''
Removing the 22 subtasks drops a word-frequency baseline from 0.663 to 0.498 (chance).

\textbf{Winners' audit (m.31, Appendix ``Should we trust BabyLM Metrics?'').} An untrained model scores
$\approx$78 on adjective-nomination; a broken run (spiked loss) beat every properly-trained model on the
aggregate via EWoK/adj-nom/COMPS/AoA. Verdict: trust BLiMP and (Super)GLUE; reading/eye-tracking ``stay
relatively similar, regardless of the model''; ``the rest\,\dots\ a baby fist of salt.''

\textbf{AoA miscomputation (m.29).} ``only very few data points (1--5 words) are considered\,\dots\ due to an
unpassed condition on the parameters of the fitted sigmoid function\,\dots\ these results can be misleading.''

\textbf{EWoK data coverage (m.15).} ``EWoK\,\dots\ remaining around 50\% regardless of the experiment
conditions''; 37.7\% of items have a concept appearing $<$100 times in training, 13\% have both at zero ---
``the training dataset does not properly support EWoK evaluation.''

\textbf{Entity-tracking instability (m.34, m.22).} ``rises to 41.8 at 20M tokens before collapsing to 13.4 by
the end of training''; seed SD 6--9 points (20.70$\pm$6.09).

\textbf{GLUE insensitivity (m.36).} (Super)GLUE frozen at exactly 57.7 across every proposed and controlled
configuration. Organizers: only two of seven Strict systems beat the best Strict-Small system on GLUE.

\textbf{Metric decoupling (m.32, m.13).} RL ``primarily enhances narrative coherence\,\dots\ while leaving
surface-level fluency --- measured by the BabyLM tasks --- largely unchanged''; combining metrics into one
score ``would obscure these distinctions and reduce interpretability.''
